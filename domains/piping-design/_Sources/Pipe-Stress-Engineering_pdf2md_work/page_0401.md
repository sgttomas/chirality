$$DLF = \frac{F_{max}}{F_0} = 2 \quad \text{when } t_d \geq 0.5T$$

$$DLF = \sqrt{(t_d/t_0)^2 + (t_d t_0)^2} \quad \text{when } t_d < 0.5T$$

The second expression will be further explained later in Fig. 12.9.

The above procedure is generally applicable to all idealized impulse loads mentioned. The response time-history can be calculated at both the forced and free vibration stages. However, the often-needed practical information for engineering design is the DLF corresponding to the maximum displacement produced. Because the elastic structural force, moment, and stress are all proportional to the displacement, we can then apply the maximum DLF on the applied force to convert it into a static equivalent force for performing static analysis. The values of DLF for some common shapes of impulse loads are plotted in Figs. 12.5 and 12.6. Some of the detailed calculations are outlined in a number of books on structural dynamics [1, 3].

With these figures, one can get a feeling of the load's effect on a system by estimating the natural frequency of the system. From the natural frequency and the duration of the impulse, the maximum DLF is determined from the appropriate figure. By applying the DLF on the maximum load, a static equivalent load is arrived. This static equivalent load should give us an idea of the effect of the impulse on the system. With non-reversing loading, the maximum DLF is less than or equal to 2.0 for all loads.
