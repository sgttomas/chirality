When a load reaches the structural system with an initial velocity like the weight load with a free drop, as shown in Fig. 12.1(e), it is called an impact load. The DLF of the weight load with a free drop can be calculated, again by energy-balance, as follows.

$$W \cdot (h + \delta_S) = \frac{1}{2} k \delta_S \cdot \delta_S \quad \text{or} \quad 2\lambda_0 h + 2\lambda_0 \delta_S = \delta_S^2$$

or

$$\delta_S = \frac{2\delta_{st} + \sqrt{4\delta_{st}^2 + 8\delta_{st}h}}{2} = \delta_{st}\left(1 + \sqrt{1 + \frac{2h}{\delta_{st}}}\right)$$

i.e., $\text{DLF} = \frac{\delta_S}{\delta_{st}} = 1 + \sqrt{1 + \frac{2h}{\delta_{st}}}$

Because $\delta_{st}$ is independent of the free drop gap *h*, the DLF increases as the gap increases. When the free drop gap is zero, the DLF is 2.0 as derived previously. With a free drop gap, the DLF is always greater than 2.0.

The above examples are applicable only to a sustained-constant load with the magnitude of the load maintaining the same throughout the deformation. In such cases, the DLF is always greater than 1.0. However, depending on the duration of the load and the mass of the structure, the DLF can also be smaller than 1.0, and sometimes even approaches zero.

## 12.2 SDOF STRUCTURES

The dynamics of piping and structures is very complicated. However, because we are mainly dealing with linear systems, most of the structural behaviors are linear combinations of the behaviors of simple systems.
