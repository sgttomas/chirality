As it travels downstream with sonic velocity *a*, the corresponding wave at point 2 arrives $t_{12}/a$ of time later. Therefore, the wave at point 2 becomes

$$P_2 = P_1 \sin\alpha \left(t - \frac{\ell_{12}}{a}\right) = P_1 \sin\left(\omega t - \frac{\omega \ell_{12}}{a}\right) \quad \text{at point 2}$$

where $\ell_{12}/a$ is the time lag and $\omega\ell_{12}/a$ is the phase angle of point 2 with respect to point 1. The wave at point 3 can be expressed in a similar manner, using $\ell_{13}$ instead of $\ell_{12}$, where $\ell_{13} = \ell_{12} + \ell_{23}$, etc. The pressure force at point *n* is obtained by multiplying the pressure with the pipe inside cross-sectional area as

$$F_{xn} = AP_n = AP_1 \sin\left(\omega t - \frac{\omega \ell_{1n}}{a}\right), \quad \ell_{1n} = \ell_{12} \cdots \ell_{n-1,n} \tag{13.65}$$

$F_{x1}$ is the traveling wave force at point *n*. A similar force exists at every corner and end point of the piping system. Each point generally has two components of force. Take point 2, for instance; it has $F_{x2}$ and $F_{y2}$ — two components each with the same $F_{x1}$ magnitude. The analysis procedure is discussed in the next section.

For a standing wave, the wave shape is generally sinusoidal, the same as the structural vibration mode shapes of a straight beam. The open-open pipe volume has a pressure wave shape similar to the structural mode shape of a simple supported beam. The pressure at points located within each half-wave oscillates at the same frequency and phase, but with different amplitude.
