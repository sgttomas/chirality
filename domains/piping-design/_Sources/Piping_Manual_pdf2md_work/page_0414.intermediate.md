## IMPACTS OF EXCESSIVE PIPE STRESS

We have discussed why stress analysis is important. We have also reviewed the work process steps the designer and the stress engineer take to accomplish a quality stress analysis. The next step is to address what the stress analyst specifically looks for when analyzing pipe. This section will provide the basis for understanding what stress analysis tools the layout designer should use when performing piping design.

The following paragraphs discuss the three basic types of problems that the stress engineer faces. These three problems are overstressed piping components, overstressed vessel nozzles, and overloading the nozzles on mechanical equipment. There are a wide variety of other problems that the stress engineer addresses on a typical petrochemical project. However, these three types of problems are the cause for piping layout revisions.

## Overstressing Piping Components

The first item a stress engineer avoids is overstressing a piping component. Various forces act on a pipe from its own weight, thermal growth, wind, and other causes. As the forces are applied, the piping system begins to deflect or sag. The more forces, the more the pipe deflects. The greater the pipe deflection, the higher the stress becomes in the pipe material. Once the stress in the pipe reaches a maximum value, the deflection becomes permanent. By "permanent deflection" we mean, when the cause of the deflection is removed, the pipe will not return to its original shape. The stress engineer calls this phenomenon yielding. This is what he seeks to avoid.

To avoid yielding a piping component during operation, the stress engineer manipulates the layout and the supporting scheme of the piping system. His goal is to keep the pipe stress below the limits described in the ANSI/ASME B31.3, Code. This is the code used for petrochemical projects in the United States.
