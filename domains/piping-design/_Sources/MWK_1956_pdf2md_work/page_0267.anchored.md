The effective concentrated weight (at center) of a total uniformly distributed load $W$ (lb) on a simply supported circular plate may be taken as

$$W_{\text{eff}} = \tfrac{1}{8}W \tag{9.30}$$

Using the above units, the natural frequency of a circular plate clamped at the edge and acted on by a concentrated load $P$ at the center is (assuming $\mu = 0.3$)

$$f_n = 23.3 \frac{t}{r^2}\sqrt{\frac{Et}{P}} \tag{9.31}$$

For a uniformly distributed load $W$ on a clamped circular plate, the effective concentrated load (at center) may be taken as:

$$W_{\text{eff}} = \tfrac{1}{3}W \tag{9.32}$$

A high-frequency periodic pressure variation in a pipe might induce a structural vibration in a radial mode, which will result in alternate hoop tension and compression. The fundamental natural frequency of a pipe, with a free length equal to, say, at least 5 diameters, in a radial mode is [4]:

$$f_n = \frac{6.25}{D}\sqrt{\frac{E}{\rho}} \tag{9.33}$$

where $D$ = mean diameter of pipe, in., $E$ is in lb/in.², and $\rho$ = density of material, lb/in.³ However, for typical cases the frequency $f_n$ (cps), as given by eq. 9.33, is so high that resonance of this type is hardly ever likely to occur.

*Ovalling Mode.* Another mode of vibration (observed on large stacks at certain wind speeds) is what is often referred to as *ovalling*. In this mode of vibration the circular cross section assumes an elliptical shape with the major and minor axes alternating in perpendicular directions once per cycle.

For steel pipe, the natural frequency (cps) corresponding to this mode may be estimated from the expression

$$f_n = \frac{50,000}{D} \times \frac{t}{D}$$

where $D$ = diameter, in.
$t$ = thickness, in.

Apart from large vessels or stacks, the natural frequency of ordinary pipe in an ovalling mode is high.

## 9.4 Structural Resonance and Magnification Factors

Since resonance is the most important phenomenon in vibration, a few simple derivations of magnification will be given, although for an exhaustive discussion the reader is referred to standard references on the subject.

The motion of a spring-mass combination when excited with a periodically varying force ($F \sin \omega t$) is governed by the differential equation

$$m\ddot{x} + kx = F \sin \omega t$$
$$\ddot{x} = d^2x/dt^2 \tag{9.34}$$

$x$ being the displacement of the mass or deflection in spring, relative to the system configuration for $F = 0$. Although no damping is assumed so far, a small amount of damping may always be supposed to exist. Because of this effect the initial transients will eventually be damped out, so that the remaining "steady state" solution is of the form $x = R \sin \omega t$. Substituting this solution into eq. 9.34 gives $(-m\omega^2 + k)R = F$, or

$$\frac{R}{F} = \frac{1}{k - m\omega^2} = \frac{1/k}{1 - (\omega^2/\omega_n^2)},$$

since $\omega_n = \sqrt{k/m}$ or

$$\frac{R}{F/k} = \frac{1}{1 - (\omega^2/\omega_n^2)}$$

Now, $F/k$ represents the static deflection of the spring under the force $F$, while $R$ is the maximum *dynamic* deflection under the periodic force $F \sin \omega t$. That is $F/k = X_{st}$, $R = X_{\text{dyn}}$. The absolute value of the ratio of maximum dynamic deflection (maximum amplitude) to static deflection is defined appropriately as the *magnification factor*, thus:

$$\text{M.F.} = \left| \frac{X_{\text{dyn}}}{X_{st}} \right| \tag{9.35}$$

or, from the previous results,

$$\text{M.F.} = \left| \frac{1}{1 - (\omega^2/\omega_n^2)} \right| \tag{9.36}$$

A plot of M.F. vs. the ratio of forcing frequency $\omega$ to undamped natural frequency $\omega_n$ (i.e. $\omega/\omega_n$) is sometimes referred to as a *resonance* curve (for zero damping). This curve is shown in Fig. 9.14, labeled $\xi = 0$. For this extreme case (i.e. no damping), the magnification factor becomes infinite when the forcing frequency $\omega$ coincides with the undamped natural frequency $\omega_n$.

In the presence of a finite amount of viscous damping, the differential equation takes the form $m\ddot{x} + c\dot{x} + kx = F \sin \omega t$, $c$ being a coefficient of viscous damping. It is simpler to consider the (ultimately equivalent) differential equation

$$m\ddot{x} + c\dot{x} + kx = Fe^{i\omega t}$$
