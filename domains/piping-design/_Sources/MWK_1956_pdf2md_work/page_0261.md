Equations 9.3 and 9.5 are of great usefulness, primarily because of their immediate generalization to configurations far more complex than the simple system for which they were derived.

[FIGURE: Fig. 9.2 Structure supporting a weight.]

Thus, Fig. 9.2 shows a structure supporting a concentrated weight $W$ (lb). Assume that the weight of the structure can be neglected as compared with $W$, and that it is desired to estimate the natural frequency of vibration of the mass in a vertical direction. By virtue of eqs. 9.3 or 9.5 the vibration problem is reduced to a purely structural one. To use eq. 9.3, calculate the concentrated force (lb) required to deflect point $O$ vertically 1 ft (i.e. the spring constant $k$(lb/ft) in the vertical direction in structural deflection theory). With $W = mg$ given, $f_n$ is found by substitution into eq. 9.3. Alternately, the static vertical deflection at point $O$ under the concentrated weight $W$ can be found and $f_n$ calculated from eq. 9.5.

[FIGURE: Fig. 9.3 End weight on a cantilever.]

*Example.* Given a concentrated weight $W = mg$ being supported at the end of a massless cantilever of length $L$, structural moment of inertia $I$, and material modulus of elasticity $E$ (see Fig. 9.3). Find the concentrated weight $W$ in the structural moment of inertia $I$, and material modulus of elasticity $E$ (see Fig. 9.3). Find the natural frequency of vibration of the system.

The vertical (static) deflection of $O$ under the weight $W$ is, from structural theory,

$$\delta_{st} = 48 \frac{WL^3}{EI}$$

using dimensions of $W$ (lb), $L$ (ft), $E$ (lb/in.$^2$), $I$ (in.$^4$), and $\delta_{st}$ (ft). From eq. 9.5,

$$f_n = 0.900 \sqrt{\frac{EI}{48WL^3}} = 0.130 \sqrt{\frac{EI}{WL^3}} \quad (9.6)$$

In general, eq. 9.3 is of greater usefulness than

eq. 9.5, since in the former case the notions of spring constant or the purely elastic effect and notion of mass or inertia effect are *separate*, while in eq. 9.5 the two notions are combined; as a result, eq. 9.5 lends itself more readily towards generalization. This is illustrated by the concept of effective mass, which will be introduced in the next subsection.

**9.3b Frequency and Mass Effectiveness Factors for Different End Constraints.** Between supports or anchors, a pipe is a vibrating system with uniformly distributed mass. Each restrained length possesses an infinite number of degrees of freedom, hence vibration may occur in an infinite number of modes singly or in combination. In practice, the fundamental mode is of main interest, but occasionally the second mode may also be of some importance.

The general expression for the natural frequencies of beams with uniform mass distribution, such as pipes, is of the following form:

$$f_n = \alpha \sqrt{\frac{EI}{WL^3}} = \frac{\alpha}{L^2} \sqrt{\frac{EI}{w_p}} \quad (9.7)$$

where $\alpha$ is a coefficient which varies with the end conditions and mode. In order to follow the piping practice used in the remainder of this book, the numerical constant $\alpha$ in eq. 9.7 and the dimensions (lb/in.)/(ft/sec$^2$)$^{2}$* and the symbols in eq. 9.7 have the following units$^2$:

$W$ = total weight, lb.

$w_p$ = weight per ft of pipe (including pipe contents and insulation), lb/ft.

$E$ = modulus of elasticity, lb/in.$^2$

$I$ = moment of inertia, in.$^4$

$L$ = length of pipe, ft.

$f_n$ = natural frequency, cps.

Values for $\alpha$, $L$, and $E$ can be found in Appendix C. Table 9.1 gives values of $\alpha$ for the cases indicated.

The concept of "effective mass" is a convenient approach by which a distributed mass is treated as an equivalent concentrated mass. Thus, for a massless cantilever of length $L$ (ft) with concentrated end load $P$ (lb) the natural frequency is given by eq. 9.6. For uniform weight distribution, the fundamental mode of vibration is:

$$f_n = \frac{0.205}{L^2} \sqrt{\frac{EI}{w_p}} = 0.265 \sqrt{\frac{EI}{WL^3}}$$

(see Table 9.1 and eq. 9.7)
