Suppose now infinitesimal perturbations relative to a quiescent state, i.e. $\bar{U} = 0$. Eliminating $\Delta\rho$ via the 3rd relation gives:

$$\bar{p} \frac{\partial(\Delta U)}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial(\Delta p)}{\partial x}$$

$$\bar{p} \frac{\partial(\Delta U)}{\partial x} = -\frac{1}{C^2} \frac{\partial(\Delta p)}{\partial t}$$

(B.13a)

From which, by further differentiation:

$$\frac{\partial^2(\Delta U)}{\partial x^2} = \frac{1}{C^2} \frac{\partial^2(\Delta U)}{\partial t^2}$$

$$\frac{\partial^2(\Delta p)}{\partial x^2} = \frac{1}{C^2} \frac{\partial^2(\Delta p)}{\partial t^2}$$

(B.13b)

Equations B.13b are the familiar wave equations for one dimensional velocity, governing the propagation of small (infinitesimal) disturbances in a fluid medium ($C =$ speed of "sound" = speed of propagation of infinitesimal disturbances).

Particular solutions of the wave equation are obtained by separation of variables. Thus if:

$$\Delta p = f_1(x) f_2(t)$$

Then

$$f_2(t) \frac{d^2 f_1}{dx^2} = \frac{1}{C^2} f_1(x) \frac{d^2 f_2}{dt^2}$$

Or

$$\frac{1}{f_1} \frac{d^2 f_1}{dx^2} = \frac{1}{C^2} \frac{1}{f_2} \frac{d^2 f_2}{dt^2} = -\lambda^2$$

Since $f_1$ is a function of $x$ only and $f_2$ is a function only of $t$, $\lambda$ must be a function of neither, i.e. a constant.

The solutions for $f_1$ and $f_2$ must be of the form:

$$f_1(x) = B_1 \cos \lambda x + B_2 \sin \lambda x$$

$$f_2(t) = D_1 \cos \lambda C t + D_2 \sin \lambda C t$$

An equivalent form of the solution is also:

$$\Delta p = E_1 \cos\left(\frac{\omega x}{C} + \phi\right) e^{i\omega t}; \quad \omega = \lambda C; \quad i = \sqrt{-1}$$

Likewise, by analogous reasoning:

$$\Delta(\bar{p}U) = E_2 \cos\left(\frac{\omega x}{C} + \phi_2\right) e^{i\omega t}$$

However, $E_1$, $E_2$, $\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$ are not completely independent of one another, since the solution must also satisfy equations B.13a. It can be verified by direct substitution that the forms:

$$\Delta p = i C R \cos\left(\frac{\omega x}{C} + \phi\right) e^{i\omega t}$$

$$\Delta(\bar{p}U) = R \sin\left(\frac{\omega x}{C} + \phi\right) e^{i\omega t}$$

(B.13c)

satisfy not only the wave equations B.13b but also equations B.13a, and hence represent a solution of both systems of partial differential equations. $R$ is in general a "complex amplitude," while $\phi$ is, of course, a real quantity.

**Boundary Conditions at a Junction.** The boundary conditions at any junction are given by continuity of both pressure and mass flow rate. Thus at any junction of $n$ branches or segments, for pressure continuity:

$$i C R_1 \cos\left(\frac{\omega L_1 x_1}{C} + \phi_1\right) = i C R_2 \cos\left(\frac{\omega L_2 x_2}{C} + \phi_2\right)$$

$$= \cdots = i C R_n \cos\left(\frac{\omega L_n x_n}{C} + \phi_n\right)$$

(B.13d)

where $L_1 x_1$, $L_2 x_2$, ... $L_n x_n$ were defined previously. While for continuity of mass flow rate:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n} \pm a_i R_i \sin\left(\frac{\omega L_i x_i}{C} + \phi_i\right) = 0$$

(B.13e)

the $a$'s being the areas and the ($\pm$) sign to be taken as defined previously.

Substituting Eqs. B.13d into B.13e yields:

$$R_i \cos\left(\frac{\omega L_i x_i}{C} + \phi_i\right) \sum_{i=1}^{n} \pm a_i \tan\left(\frac{\omega L_i x_i}{C} + \phi_i\right) = 0$$

Or, assuming the 1st factor $\neq 0$, then

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n} \pm a_i \tan\left(\frac{\omega L_i x_i}{C} + \phi_i\right) = 0$$

Now

$$\frac{\omega L_i x_i}{C} = \frac{2\pi f_0 L_i x_i}{C/L} = \frac{2\pi f}{f_0} \cdot \frac{L_i x_i}{L}; \quad f_0 = \frac{C}{4L}$$

and writing $\beta_i$ instead of $\phi_i$:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n} \pm a_i \tan\left(\frac{\pi}{2} \frac{f}{f_0} \cdot \frac{L_i x_i}{L} + \beta_i\right) = 0$$

(B.13f)

which is the result given in the text above.
