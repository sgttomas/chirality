alternative, then the system must be designed on the basis of the second alternative, that is by *increasing* the natural frequencies sufficiently. Again this can be achieved in piping essentially only through a *reduction* in free length by the introduction of additional supports, rigid or elastic. If neither alternative is possible, artificial damping must be introduced, in the form of friction devices which dissipate energy through friction, (hydraulic shock absorbers, etc.).

The results given above are based upon the use of the spring-mass-dashpot system as a model. This model, being a system of one degree of freedom, necessarily exhibits only one resonance peak. A piping system or even a simply supported beam is, in reality, a system possessing an infinite number of degrees of freedom and such a system will exhibit, theoretically at least, an infinite number of resonance peaks. However, in general the first (fundamental) mode with the lowest natural frequency is the most important one in any system, and the resonance behavior in the neighborhood of the first peak in any system is essentially the same as that for the simple spring-mass model.

## 9.5 Damping of Structural Vibrations

### 9.5a Hydraulic Snubbers.³

In vibration surveys the amplitude and frequency of vibration is determined at a point (or various points) of a vibrating structure. If the amplitude of vibration is excessive and if this amplitude can be reduced only by the installation of a hydraulic snubber, it is of importance to estimate the maximum force the snubber must transmit and the degree of damping required to reduce the amplitude of vibration to a tolerable magnitude.

In this discussion it is assumed that the vibration is of the forced type and the damping characteristic of the snubber is of the purely viscous type. The following quantities be known or be estimated or measured:

1. The amplitude of vibration, $R_0$, ft
2. The frequency of vibration, $\omega_a$, rad/sec  $\Big\}$ from vibration survey
3. Natural frequency of vibrating structure, $\omega_n$  $\Big\}$ from calculations or experiment
4. Spring constant of vibrating structure, $k$, lb/ft

(at point where amplitude is measured)

³The term "hydraulic snubber" and "hydraulic shock absorber" are both used herein to denote the kind of damping device described in Chapter 8.

If

$R$ = amplitude of vibration measured, ft

$\zeta$ = viscous damping coefficient (nondimensional),

$F_S$ = maximum force to be transmitted by snubber, lb,

Then, we have, from the relations given in the text,

$$R/R_0 = \cfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \cfrac{2\zeta(\omega/\omega_n)}{1 - (\omega/\omega_n)^2}}} \quad (9.44)$$

or

$$\zeta = \frac{1}{2} \frac{|1 - (\omega/\omega_n)^2|}{\omega/\omega_n} \sqrt{(R_0/R)^2 - 1} \quad (9.45)$$

$$F_S = kR_0|1 - (\omega/\omega_n)^2| \sqrt{1 - (R/R_0)^2} \quad (9.46)$$

Thus, $k$, $R_0$, $\omega$, and $\omega_n$ being known, by hypothesis, the maximum snubber force $F_S$ can be determined from eq. 9.46 for any given amplitude ratio $R/R_0$ of damped to undamped vibration. The required damping coefficient $\zeta$ for a desired amplitude ratio is given by eq. 9.45, or if $\zeta$ is known, the amplitude ratio is determined by eq. 9.44.

If $\zeta$ is not known, eq. 9.45 suggests an experimental method whereby this quantity can be determined. Consider a simple system whose natural frequency $\omega_n$ is known, subject it to a forced vibration of frequency $\omega/\omega_n$ and measure the amplitude $R_0$; then connect a hydraulic snubber to the point where the amplitude is measured; the system is now subjected to forced vibration under the same conditions as before and measurements are obtained for the reduced amplitude $R$. The damping coefficient $\zeta$ of the snubber can then be determined from eq. 9.45.

A study of eqs. 9.44, 9.45, and 9.46 shows that viscous damping is really effective only in the neighborhood of resonance ($\omega/\omega_n = 1$). This can also be shown from Fig. 9.14; equation 9.44 represents the reduction of the M.F. obtained by reading along a vertical in Fig. 9.14 from the no damping ($\zeta = 0$) curve to that for a given $\zeta$. Outside the vicinity of resonance (say when $\omega/\omega_n \leq \frac{1}{2}$ or $\omega/\omega_n \geq \frac{3}{2}$), high damping coefficients are required to reduce the amplitude to a reasonable degree, i.e. very large maximum snubber forces may be needed. This point will be further illustrated numerically in Section 9.9.

From the above expressions is also obtained:

$$F_S = kR_0 \frac{|1 - (\omega/\omega_n)^2|}{\sqrt{1 + \left[\frac{1 - (\omega/\omega_n)^2}{2\zeta \omega/\omega_n}\right]^2}} \quad (9.47)$$
