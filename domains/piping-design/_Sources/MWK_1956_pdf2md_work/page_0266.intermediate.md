[FIGURE: Fig. 9.12 Two-member model $\theta = \pi/2$.]

to two parallel cantilevers and $\alpha = 1.06$. In the other extreme, $\theta = \pi$, the bend reduces again to a fixed-end beam and $\alpha = 1.69$. Consider now the case, $\theta = \pi/2$ (Fig. 9.12). It can be shown that the deflection $y_o$ (in ft) at the point $O$ due to a unit load at that point, in a direction perpendicular to the plane of the bend, is given by

$$y_o = \frac{48/EI}{\dfrac{1/L_1^2}{1 + (EI/GJ)(L_2/L_1)} + \dfrac{1/L_2^2}{1 + (EI/GJ)(L_1/L_2)}} \quad (9.26)$$

For $EI/GJ = 1$ as assumed and $L_1/L_2 = 1$, the result reduces to

$$y_o = \frac{5}{384} \frac{L^3}{EI} \times 144 = \frac{15}{8} \frac{L^3}{EI} \quad (9.27)$$

The effective end weight for a cantilever due to uniformly distributed mass is approximately $\frac{1}{4}$ of the total weight of the cantilever. For a fixed-end beam the effective weight at the center due to uniformly distributed load is $\frac{5}{8}$ of the total weight of the beam.

Clearly the coefficient of effectiveness for the 90° bend of equal pipe lengths is situated between $\frac{1}{4}$ and $\frac{5}{8}$. If then the effective weight is taken as $W_{\text{eff}} = \frac{5}{16}$ total weight of bend, the result can not be greatly in error. With this value of effective weight at the junction point and the previous deflection for $y_o$ given above, it is found that $\alpha = 1.2$. Thus for the bend of equal lengths:

[TABLE: Values of $\alpha$ for $\theta = 0$, $\pi/2$, $\pi$ for bend of equal lengths]

| $\theta$ | 0 | $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$ | $\pi$ |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| $\alpha$ | 1.06 | 1.2 | 1.69 |

If a curve of $\alpha$ vs. $\theta$ is drawn through these three points it appears as shown in Fig. 9.13. In the other extreme case ($L_2 = 0$; $L_1 = 1$), $\alpha = 1.69 =$ Constant for all $\theta$, as already remarked above. This "curve," i.e., the horizontal line $\alpha = 1.69$, is also shown in Fig. 9.13.

Obviously curves of $\alpha$ vs. $\theta$ for all other length ratios $L_1/L_2$, must be situated between the two extreme cases (a) and (b) discussed above and shown in Fig. 9.13. Even though it is not exactly correct, a simple interpolation between the two extreme cases for any given length ratio is good enough for engineering estimates. Thus the two extreme cases plus simple interpolation define the values of $\alpha$ for any length ratio and any angle $\theta$ to within reasonable approximation.

## 9.3f Plates and Radial Mode in Pipe.

The spring constant (for deflection caused by a concentrated load at center) of a simply supported circular plate is

$$k = 261 \frac{Et^3}{r^2} \quad (9.28)$$

where  $k =$ spring constant, lb/ft.
       $r =$ radius of plate, ft.
       $t =$ thickness of plate, in.
       $E =$ modulus of elasticity, lb/in.$^2$
and    $\nu =$ Poisson's ratio, assumed to be equal to 0.3.

For a concentrated load $P$ (lb) at the center of the plate, large with respect to the plate weight, the natural frequency in cps is then

$$f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{261 E t^3 g}{Pr^2}} = 14.6 \frac{t}{r}\sqrt{\frac{Et}{P}} \quad (9.29)$$
