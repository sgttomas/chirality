## 1410  Introduction

In 1995 Chevron USA Products Company and Chevron Chemical Company commissioned CRTC to assess existing and evolving technology to reduce fugitive emissions from valves. The results were published in a report released by CRTC's Health, Environmental and Safety group in November 1996. The report included sections with detailed information on Regulatory Compliance Programs and Estimating Emissions. These sections are available from the Health, Environment, and Safety Unit website, or from Technical Standards at CRTC. The balance of the initial report regarding reducing fugitive emissions from valves has been incorporated into this section of the *Piping Manual*.

## 1411  Definition of Fugitive Emissions

Fugitive emissions result from routine leaks of process fluid (gas or liquid) from joints, seals, or rotating surfaces. In the refining and chemical industries, fugitive emission leaks mostly result from valves, connectors, agitators, pressure relief valves, sampling connections, pumps, and compressors. Although the emissions from each fugitive emissions source are relatively small compared to most point sources, the large number of such sources in a facility can lead to a significant amount of volatile organic compound (VOC) and hazardous air pollutant (HAP) emissions.

## 1412  Why Reducing Fugitive Emissions is Important

There are several reasons why reducing fugitive emissions from valves is important, including:

- preventing incidents
- complying with regulatory requirements
- reducing inspection and maintenance costs

### Preventing Incidents

A key reason for reducing fugitive emissions is to prevent incidents. A study of incidents caused by valve failures over a ten year period showed that 39 fires within Chevron have been attributed to leaking valves. Leaks from valves have been responsible for 18 incidents at one facility resulting in injury, product loss, plant upsets, fire, and environmental releases in just a two year period. These incidents result in lost profit opportunity and increased liability in addition to the direct impacts on operations, personnel, and the environment. Clearly, by reducing the number and magnitude of leaks from valves, we will prevent incidents from happening.

### Regulatory Compliance

Regulatory agencies believe that fugitive emissions are a significant part of the refining and petrochemical industries' total emissions. Several federal, state, and local regulations have been enacted over the past 25 years targeting fugitive emissions. We are interested in reducing our fugitive emissions, not only to comply with the regulations, but to reduce our impact on the air quality of surrounding communities. In some cases, reducing fugitive emissions or the occurrence of VOC leaks will lead to a reduction in required monitoring frequency which can equate to significant cost savings for a facility.

### Maintenance Costs

Overall cost reduction is another driving force behind fugitive emission reduction. By knowing what factors cause fugitive emissions and the best ways to prevent leaks from occurring, significant saving can be realized by reducing the amount of maintenance needed on a valve over its lifetime. Maintenance has been estimated to make up 28% of the total cost of valve ownership. Costs to remove, repair, and reinstall a valve can range from $300 for a small valve to $1100 for a large valve. This does not take into account other costs such as erecting staging or supplying fresh air. Maintenance and replacement costs can be reduced by selecting the proper valve, using low emissions packing, and selecting high quality suppliers.

## 1413 Estimating Fugitive Emissions

Emissions estimating is used in preparing emission inventories for regulatory reporting purposes as well as to calculate permit fees. It can also help in focusing fugitive emission control efforts by identifying equipment or services with large potential leakage. Because it would be ridiculously expensive and onerous to measure the actual emissions from each fugitive emission source in a facility, emissions are usually estimated. Currently, there are several methods available to estimate emissions, ranging from simple-to-use but conservatively high, to more complicated but more accurate methods.

Several regulations require that facilities report releases of pollutants to the environment, most notably Section 313 of the Superfund Amendments and Reauthorization Act (SARA). Because this information is released to the public, a facility needs to consider carefully how much time and effort it wants to invest in preparing an accurate emission inventory. Using the easiest method may cost the least in the short run, but the easiest method may impact the public relations of the facility if the estimated emissions are needlessly high.

If a facility's emissions inventory is used to calculate local permit fees, using the more accurate methods, although more complicated, may lead to significant cost savings by reducing the reported amount of fugitive emissions.

Many facilities are automating their fugitive emission estimating function by incorporating the calculations into a Leak Detection and Remediation Program data management system. Even if this is the case, it is important to know how the emissions are calculated for data quality checks and for one-time or non-routine estimates.

There are a number of methods available for estimating emissions. Details on methods and factors can be obtained from the CRTC Health, Environment and Safety Unit, Air Team.

For estimating emissions, average emission factors are the easiest to use but are the least accurate; they can greatly overestimate emissions. Correlation equations, which calculate emissions using screening data, are the most accurate method for estimating fugitive emissions, but they are more complex to use than the average factors and require a lot of data. If screening data is collected in a facility, correlation equations should be used to estimate emissions. The resulting estimate will be significantly less than the average emission factor estimate.

## 1420 Critical Factors in Reducing Fugitive Emissions from Valves

### 1421 Summary

This section focuses on what can be done to reduce fugitive emissions from valves. The three critical tasks are:

1. making sure the valve is in good condition
2. using the right packing for the valve
3. installing the packing correctly

If any one of these three factors is ignored, the other two may have no impact on emissions. These three factors, good valve conditions, proper packing, and correct installation, can be represented by the three sides of a triangle representing low valve emissions. If any one side of the triangle is missing, it is incomplete, and low emissions may not be achievable. For example, installing a good packing in a valve in poor condition may not reduce emissions and neither will improperly installing a good packing into a new valve. All three of these factors must be considered in order to reduce emissions from valves. Figure 1400-2 shows the relationship between these three critical factors.

## 1423 Hand Operated Valves Versus Automatic Control Valves

It is important to distinguish between the two main types of valves used in the petroleum and chemical industries: the hand operated valve and the automatic control valve.

Typically, hand operated valves are block valves that do not control flow rate; they are usually either fully open or completely closed. Typical examples are suction and discharge valves on pumps, drain and vent valves, instrument connection root valves, and valve manifolds. However, it is possible to have hand operated valves that are performing the function of controlling flow rate. Hand operated valves can be either gate, globe, or quarter turn (ball or plug) valves.

An automatic control valve, also referred to as a throttling valve, is a device, installed in a hydraulic system, that controls flow through the system by introducing and modulating pressure drop within its body. By increasing pressure drop through the system, the control valve creates a resistance to flow, thus decreasing the flow. The pressure drop is adjusted through the positioning of the valve stem.

Automatic control valves use actuators to constantly adjust the valve stem position to carefully control the flow rate. These valves are generally either globe or rotary (full ball, segmented ball, high performance butterfly, eccentric disk, etc.) valves. Segmented ball valves can be characterized to provide specific control characteristics.

The main difference between hand operated valves and automatic control valves is the frequency of operation. Hand operated valves are typically operated between once a week to once an hour, and sometimes only once a year. Automatic control valves are constantly moving as constant adjustments are made to the flow rate. In addition, the actuators of automatic valves must be able to reliably and smoothly overcome stem friction in order to match valve stem output within 0.5% to 2%. To maintain controllability while maintaining low fugitive emissions, control valve packings must produce low stem friction while maintaining a superior stem seal. Hand operated valves do not have this requirement of low friction for smooth stem movement.

Because of the differences in valve design and packing requirements, fugitive emission control for hand operated valves and automatic control valves will be addressed separately.

## 1431 Valve Condition of Hand Operated Valves

One key factor to reducing fugitive emissions from valves is ensuring that the valves being used are in good condition. The following section covers various topics related to overall valve condition, including:

- the types of valves and how emissions occur
- research on the effect of various mechanical parameters on fugitive emissions
- existing standards for valves
- the benefits of the newly established valve manufacturer and reconditioner alliance

### Summary of Valve Condition Research

The findings and recommendations relating to valve condition are summarized below:

- A PERF study found that stem finish and stem corrosion were the valve mechanical variables with the largest impact on emissions. Another study found that almost half of leaking valves had some form of stem damage.
- Examine leaking valves during repair to assess the valve condition. If the valve is obviously in poor condition, it should be reconditioned or replaced.
- Handle valves with care: avoid lifting valves by their handwheel or stem, cradle valves when transporting, recognize that cheater bars can damage a valve, and clean an exposed valve stem before operating the valve.
- To prevent incidents and emissions due to fires and corrosion, follow all existing specifications for valve selection taking into consideration material, service, temperature, pressure, and tolerances.
- Only purchase your own reconditioned valves. Do not buy other's surplus or reconditioned valves.
- Use Chevron alliance suppliers to reduce the total cost of ownership for valves. The alliance supplier for valve reconditioning is Plant Specialties Inc. For new valve purchases, McJunkin is the alliance distributor and there are several valve manufacturers that have been recommended for alliance partnerships.
- When using other suppliers, check that manufacturer's or reconditioner's valve specifications are similar to the PERF study recommendations and meet API or ANSI standards for valve design (see "Research on Effect of Mechanical Parameters" on page 1400-13).

In general, the condition of the valve is a critical factor in achieving low emission performance. Anything that can be done to ensure or improve the integrity of the valve in service should help prevent incidents and emissions from occurring.

## Valve Types and How Emissions Occur

Several types of hand operated valves are used in the petrochemical industry. These include gate, globe, plug, ball, butterfly, and check valves. Emissions from valves are the result of leakage around the valve stem as well as at the valve bonnet. The most common source of leaks is at the seal between the stem and the valve packing. Few leaks are found at the valve body/bonnet bolted flange joint although this is a possible location for leaks [1]. Figures 1400-6 through 1400-10 show some of the common hand operated valve types and the locations of screening points for leak inspectors to look for leakage. These screening locations correspond to the common leak points.

Hand operated valves can be classified by the type of stem motion: rising stem (gate, globe), non-rising stem, and quarter-turn stem (plug, ball, butterfly). It is thought that quarter-turn valves may offer an advantage for reduced fugitive emissions because of the limited range of stem motion. The lack of an exposed stem may help prevent stem corrosion. Previously their use had been limited in hydrocarbon service because elastomer seats and seals were used which limited the allowable operating temperatures. The recent introduction of metal seated butterfly and ball valves is allowing their use in more petrochemical services. Metal seated quarter-turn valves offer other advantages, in addition to potential lower fugitive emissions, such as possible weight, size, and initial cost benefits.

Currently, no data is available indicating if one type of valve has lower emissions than another type. It is expected that all valve types will work equally well if all guidelines for achieving low emissions from valves are followed, including making sure the valve is in good condition, using the proper packing, and installing the packing correctly.

## Research on Effect of Mechanical Parameters

Valve mechanical design features such as stem and stuffing box clearances and finishes, stem taper, corrosion, and runout can all affect the fugitive emission leak rate from valves. For instance, stem corrosion and pitting can damage the valve packing each time the valve is cycled leading to increased emissions. Damage to the stem or nuts can lead to increased emissions by not allowing for proper compression of the packing. Stem runout may lead to misalignment of the stem allowing increased emissions.

To further assess the effects that the various mechanical variables have on fugitive emissions, a Petroleum Environmental Research Forum (PERF), Project No. 93-20, was established in 1994 and completed in early 1996 [2]. This project identified the following eight mechanical variables for investigation:

1. stuffing box finish
2. stem to bushing clearance
3. stem corrosion
4. gland to stuffing box clearance
5. stem finish
6. stem runout
7. stem taper
8. stem to gland clearance

From this testing it was concluded that stem corrosion and stem finish had a definite influence on the emission rate as shown in Figure 1400-12. It was also concluded that stuffing box finish, stem to bushing clearance, and stem taper had minimal influence on emission rate.

This study found that the following specifications give the minimum amount of emissions:

- Clearances:
  - gland to stuffing box – 10 mils
  - stem to gland (diametrical) – 30 mils
- Stem finish – 32 rms
- Stem corrosion – 0 pits
- Stem runout – 3 mils

Some of the mechanical variables were all held at a constant value since they were found to have a minimal impact on emissions:

- Stuffing box finish – 125 rms
- Stem to bushing clearance (diametrical) – 60 mils
- Stem taper – 3 mils

## Existing Valve Standards

Much guidance exists on valve specifications that, when followed, should help reduce the chance of poor fugitive emission performance. Standards for valves have been written that take into consideration the type of valve service and appropriate valve material, fire protection, stream temperature and pressure, as well as fugitive emission reduction. All of these factors need to be considered in ordering valves, in addition to the research results presented above. When replacing a valve it is important to follow the appropriate piping specification and ordering procedure. "Stash piles" should not be used. By using valves that have been manufactured within specific tolerances and by using the appropriate materials, incidents and fugitive emissions due to problems with the valve itself should be minimized.

In general, valves designs for petrochemical facilities meet American Petroleum Institute (API) Standard 600 and/or American Society of Mechanical Engineers/American National Standards Institute (ASME/ANSI) B16.34 for basic design and pressure and temperature Class ratings [5].

To prevent releases due to corrosion or fire, the material chosen for the valve is very important. For valve stems, seats, and discs, hardened 12% chromium steel is the most widely used material for general service. Based on research results presented in the previous section, it may be more cost effective to use higher quality stem metallurgy and prevent leaking valves due to stem corrosion. Guidance on material selection is available in Section 220 of the *Piping Manual*, project piping specifications, or from Materials and Equipment Engineering at CRTC.

Reconditioning of valves should meet the OEM's (Original Equipment Manufacturer's) design dimensional tolerances and the standards to which it was built. The purchase of reconditioned valves or surplus valves (other than your own) is not recommended.

## 1432 Valve Packing for Hand Operated Valves

Tighter emission standards require better packing than what has been used in the past. Minimization of incidents, conservation of resources, and ever tightening controls governing fugitive emissions of volatile organic compound (VOC) streams require that careful attention be given to the selection and installation of packing materials shown to be the most effective in satisfying valve stem sealing requirements. This section covers the types of packings available, how some packings have performed during testing, and guidelines on how to evaluate other packings. Bellows sealed valves and packing cost are also discussed.

### Summary

For new valves in volatile organic compound service (VOC), use the manufacturer standard graphitic packing as long as it meets the general guidelines discussed in "Packing Guidelines from Valve Manufacturers" on page 1400-41. Valves purchased from the recommended manufacturer's listed in Figure 1400-13 should meet these criteria. The packing sets should consist of 2 braided graphite end rings and 3 flexible graphite middle rings. In addition, valves should be tagged with the type of packing so the packing's performance can be tracked through a facilities VOC Inspection and Maintenance program.

For situations where a valve can be disassembled for repacking, flexible graphite die-formed ring packing are recommended (with braided graphite end rings to prevent extrusion). The following packings have done well in research studies:

- Garlock EVSP Simplified
- Garlock 70#

**Repacking in the field.** For repacking valves in the field, braided packing is recommended because it is the easiest to install. Garlock 1303 FEP is the recommended braided packing. Slide 3300G and RM Graphseal have been used at some Chevron facilities, but have had mixed results in research and field tests. Die-formed middle rings can also be used for field installations by cutting the ring and twisting it over the stem. However, be careful not to break apart the rings.

**Non-graphitic packings.** The best performing non-graphitic packings are DuPont Kalrez and Garlock 5888 (Teflon). These packings are only appropriate for service

temperatures lower than 400°F and are not appropriate for hydrocarbon service. Before using any packing, check the compatibility of the packing with the intended service (see "Chevron Packing Guidelines" on page 1400-27).

**New packings.** Because new packings are continuously being developed, new packings guaranteed by the manufacturer to meet the general packing description outlined in the discussion in "Packing Guidelines from Valve Manufacturers" on page 1400-41 should also be suitable for use. These guidelines outline what the graphite packing should contain and indicate that performance data from the manufacturer should be requested. It is recommended that the packing type be recorded and used with emission screening data to evaluate the performance of new packings.

**Packing performance.** It is important to note that the performance of packings may vary significantly between valves or facilities because the condition of the valve and the packing installation also play a very important role. As has been mentioned installing a packing incorrectly or into a valve in poor condition could outweigh any benefits that the packing could provide. It is very important to follow the performance of the packings over time and make decisions on future packing selection based on the actual performance of the packings in your facility.

**Bellows sealed valves.** Bellows sealed valves are an alternative to using new emission packings for reducing fugitive emissions. Under normal operation, bellows sealed valves are able to achieve zero emissions. In most cases, the high cost and large size of bellows sealed valves make their use impractical, although some cost recovery may be achieved through reduced monitoring requirements. These valves are recommended for use in benzene service for valves less than 2 inches due to the low allowable exposure level to benzene [6].

## Packing Types

In the past, the valve stem packing used in rising stem outside screw-and-yoke (OS&Y) style valves in the petrochemical industry was primarily either a braided asbestos rope packing or Teflon (PTFE) packing. The good features of asbestos are its high temperature resistance and resilience. The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) banned the use of asbestos in the late 1980's requiring that a suitable alternative packing be found. Although no longer installed, asbestos packing may still be found in existing valves.

A suitable non-asbestos material should not only satisfy primary service conditions but also be able to sustain higher temperature conditions in the event of a fire. Some of the alternative packings that are being used include:

- Flexible Graphite ("die-formed" rings)
- Spool type packing (Braided carbon or graphite)
- Braided Teflon
- Carbon Composite (graphite and PTFE)
- Solid Teflon
- Solid Krylon
- Injectable Packing Lubricant / Sealant

Flexible graphite and spool type packing are discussed in detail below.

Chevron has developed non-asbestos valve packing recommendations relative to service, temperature, and packing configuration. This information is contained in Standard Drawing GC-L99771 "Valve Packing Recommendations," located in Volume 2 of this manual, and will be covered in more detail in the following section. The Standard Drawing is intended for use with outside screw-and-yoke (OS&Y) style valves with the traditional stem stuffing box, gland, gland follower, and bolting.

**Flexible Graphite Valve Packing [3].** In March of 1987, Materials and Equipment Engineering at CRTC completed a test program to find a suitable replacement material for asbestos packing. The primary emphasis at that time was on the ability of packing to seal after fire exposure.

For normal hydrocarbon service, the packing system identified as the only suitable fire-safe replacement for asbestos packing was flexible graphite. More specifically, flexible graphite die-formed rings with braided carbon/graphite fiber end-rings, proved to be the most effective combination.

Based upon its superior ability to seal in Chevron fire tests and on available literature from manufacturers, users, and research organizations, we believe the flexible graphite system is also the most suitable for reducing fugitive emissions through valve stuffing boxes.

The fire test results are consistent with tests completed by a major petrochemical company and several valve manufacturers. Flexible graphite packings have been used for many years in Chevron's plants in critical services such as high pressure, high temperature hydrogen and steam. In power plant applications, flexible graphite packing has been found to be far superior to asbestos packing in its ability to seal.

*Advantages of Flexible Graphite Packing.* There are several positive attributes of flexible graphite stem packing:

1. It is essentially pure graphite with no binders, lubes, or blocking agents. (Binders in some braided graphite yarns serve to hold the threads together so they can be braided. Blocking agents are added to maintain a better seal by filling the voids between braids.) The oxidation of these additives is partly responsible for high temperature weight loss, which can be directly correlated with loss of sealing ability.

2. It is resistant to attack by most service environments ranging in pH 0-14, except strongly oxidizing services such as sulfuric and nitric acid.

3. It is thermally stable which prevents hardening with temperature, pressure, or time, which makes gland adjustment possible while the valve is in service.

4. It has an excellent ability to seal. Flexible graphite under the proper stem and stuffing box conditions can effectively seal with less gland pressure than asbestos, as shown in Figure 1400-14. As a direct result of using flexible graphite packing, some valve manufacturers are redesigning stuffing boxes to reduce the box depth and require fewer packing rings.

5. It is dimensionally and volumetrically stable (resilient) with a maximum resistance to relaxation and creep over long periods of pressure and temperature cycling.

6. It has a low coefficient of friction which reduces stem breakaway and sliding torque. The friction coefficient for flexible graphite is about 0.1 versus 0.3 - 1.0 for asbestos. Friction forces are further reduced when the valve is cycled, due to the micropolishing effect on the stem.

7. It is naturally lubricating, which prevents abrasion of the valve stem.

8. It can conform to fill in cracks and voids present in the valve stem and stuffing box.

9. It is non-asbestos and therefore eliminates the health risks associated with asbestos packing.

10. It has a lower permeability which increases the sealing capability for low molecular weight gases such as hydrogen or steam.

With the above combination of characteristics, flexible graphite is the most viable material available for reducing fugitive emissions through valve stem packing.

*Disadvantages of Flexible Graphite Packing (Die-formed ring systems).* One disadvantage of flexible graphite is that many sizes of die-formed rings are required to be in stock to fit every packing box size. However, with some pre-planning it is possible to establish an inventory for die-formed ring sets that minimizes the number of set sizes that are required.

Although die-formed rings are easiest to install in new or reconditioned valves where the valve can be disassembled, it is possible to use them in field installations also. Each die-formed ring can be cut and carefully formed over the stem to avoid disassembling the valve. Extreme caution must be used such that the flexible graphite rings do not break apart. They are more difficult to properly install in the field than braided graphite packing.

**Spool Type Packing (Braided Graphite).** Spool type graphite packing can be used to fit many valve sizes and helps keep packing inventory to a minimum. This type of packing is convenient for packing valves in the field since one spool can be used on many different valve sizes and the valve does not need to be disassembled for installation. Installation is done by wrapping the spool packing around a dowel sized to the required stem diameter, then cutting it to form a ring. Several rings of braided packing are stacked in the stuffing box to achieve the seal. Rotate ring cuts progressively 90 degrees apart when installing and compress each individually. The main disadvantage of this type of packing is that it is not expected to minimize emissions as well as uncut die-formed ring systems. However, only limited emissions performance data is available.

## Chevron Packing Guidelines

The existing guidelines for valve packings are summarized in Standard Drawing GC-L99771. Additional information is given below. Code Letter Types A, B, and C mentioned below refer to the generic descriptions given on the drawing. See Reference [7].

1. **Braided Carbon or Graphite (Code Letter "A"):** Packing "end rings" are commonly made from braided, carbon-fiber-reinforced graphite strands. Some forms of braided-strand packing have carbon fiber corners - and are manufactured using a square braid or lattice braid method.

   The reinforced structure of this material makes it suitable for use as "anti-extrusion" or "wiper" ring material to prevent cold-flow and to stop graphite particle migration from "die-formed" rings into the process stream.

   The packing must contain a corrosion inhibitor to prevent stem pitting caused by the galvanic corrosion phenomenon set up in wet valve packing chambers, and it must contain a graphite lubricant. Alternately, for corrosion control, the packing chamber might contain an anti-oxidant to prevent corrosion of the stem or chamber.

Characteristics of braided carbon (versus braided graphite):

- Higher in tensile strength (fiber for fiber)
- More resilient than braided graphite and less prone to damage
- Less costly
- Ends tend to fray less
- Not as easily compressed by the packing gland

There is no clear indication at this point whether braided carbon is better than braided graphite.

- Examples of material type "A" are Garlock Style 98, RM Graphaseal, Garlock 1303, and Slade 3300G.

A recent development has been the use of braided packing alone for repacking valves in the field. Braided packings for this use also fall under the above description.

2. **Flexible Graphite (Code Letter "B"):** Packing rings referred to as "flexible graphite," "die-formed" or "pre-formed" graphite are the relatively rigid, high-density graphite rings (70-to-100 lb/ft³, 99.5% carbon) pressed into rectangular, or chevron V-ring, or cup-and-cone configurations made up from flexible graphite ribbon (Union Carbide "Grafoil" or Polycarbon "Calgraph").

   - Recommended service temperature limits for this type of material is in these ranges: 650°F in hot air, 1200°F in steam; 1600°F in nitrogen or carbon dioxide.

   - The cold-flow property of die-formed graphite rings requires that they be contained within anti-extrusion rings (braided carbon rings, code letter "A") in order to keep the die-formed rings from cold-flowing between the stem and the gland or the bottom opening in the packing chamber.

   - Flexible graphite is available in a range of densities which can be tailored somewhat to suit the condition of the valve. For new valves, the packing box tolerances are tighter and a higher density is appropriate. However, higher density rings can make the valve gland adjustment quite difficult by increasing stem friction and reducing compressibility. Also, older valves with reworked stuffing boxes require more packing forgiveness. For these valves, a less dense flexible graphite is more suitable. However, low density packings will require more tightening of the packing gland leaving less room for future tightening. We recommend purchasing 70 to 90 lb/ft³ density, which allows enough compressibility in the packing to accommodate both new and old stuffing boxes.

   - Flexible graphite should have a minimum of 95% carbon, a maximum 100 ppm of leachable chlorides and 1000 ppm sulfur, and contain no binders, no non-graphite lubricants, and no blocking agents.

   - When purchasing die-formed rings, the user must specify the stem outer diameter, packing box inner diameter, and packing box depth. The packing

comes in nominal sizes, from 1/8 inch up to 1 inch. The rings are die-formed from ribbon tape.

- To repack valves in the field, without disassembling the valve, die-formed rings can be cut to fit into the stuffing box. To fit over the stem, the rings should be cut at 45 degrees to level, twisted carefully, and opened into a spiral to prevent breakage of the ring. Splits should be staggered during installation. Die-formed rings in new valves should not have a split.

- An example of material type "B" is Garlock Graph-Lock 70# and Garlock EVSP Simplified.

Some examples of flexible graphite and braided graphite product forms are shown in Figure 1400-15.

3. **Braided Teflon (PTFE, Code Letter "C").** Braided Teflon packing shall contain 100% PTFE (Teflon) and be free of graphite lube and binders. Ring construction shall be interbraid weave. Teflon has a lower limit of -100°F at which it becomes too hard, and an upper limit of about 450°F at which it vaporizes. Teflon should not be used in hydrocarbon service for fire safety purposes.

    - An example of this type material is Garlock Style 5888.

4. **Injectable Packing Lubricant / Sealant.** The type of lubricant or sealant used depends on the valve type and compatibility with the process fluid. A major manufacturer of carbon-base packing materials has made this recommendation:

    Wet-type lubricants are not recommended for use within the stuffing box cavity itself. These lubricants will create leakage voids or paths through the stuffing box when they are off-gassed as a result of temperature exposure. If additional lubrication is needed, it should be of the dry type (graphite, molybdenum disulfide, or tungsten disulfide powder).

Nevertheless, it is recognized that valves in low-temperature service, those valves with tall bonnet assemblies (good heat dissipation) as with control valves, and valves which are frequently injected with lubricant (control valves and some Orbit valves) will not experience leakage paths due to vaporization of the lubricant. Orbit valves depend upon pressure from a "wet-type (plastic) lubricant" to seal the packing against the stem and bonnet, and they perform favorably with regard to fugitive emissions. Packing lubrication can reduce friction and reduce the leak rate through graphite packing. The lubricant must be compatible with the process fluid, must not attract dirt and other abrasives, and must remain stable at high process temperatures.

## Packing Performance Studies

The previous sections discussed the types of packings that are currently available for use and guidelines for selecting a packing material. This section will discuss how some of these packings have performed in various studies. As more and more types and brands of packing become available, it has become important to determine which packing type performs best in reducing fugitive emissions. This information will help in deciding which packings should be used within a facility.

As regulatory pressure on reducing fugitive emissions increased, so did the research on the leak rates of graphite packings. The newest information on the performance of low-emission valve packings (flexible graphite packings) includes:

- A 1991 Petroleum Environmental Research Forum (PERF) laboratory study (90-05) measuring emissions from 16 packing types, and the effect of aging on the different packing types.
- A 1992 PERF study (91-17) using field data from several petroleum companies to determine the performance of several different kinds of low-emission packing.
- A 1996 PERF study (94-05) evaluating the use of spool type packings alone as a complete packing system.
- Analyses of low-emission packing performance data from the Richmond Refinery based on quarterly screening data.

Each of these studies will be discussed in more detail below.

**Laboratory Emissions Testing (PERF 90-05).** In 1991, under the auspices of the Petroleum Environmental Research Forum (PERF), eight petroleum companies sponsored a research project (90-05) aimed at reducing emissions from valve packing glands [8]. This project focused on measuring the emissions from 16 different commercially available low-emission valve packing types in a controlled laboratory setting. The packings tested were:

–Argo 60006300 –Garlock Style 1312 –Chesterton GTP-I
–Garlock EVSP Simplified –Garlock 127 AFP –Chesterton Style 370
–Garlock 708 and Style 98 –RM Graphaseal –Marlo Style 7311
–Garlock 958 and Style 98 –DuPont Kalrez CRCC –EGC Co. Thermabraid
–Garlock Style 5888 (Teflon) –Slade 3300G
–Garlock Style 1300 –J.Crane 287 I

A graphical representation of results is shown in Figure 1400-16. The best performing packings in this study were found to be:

–Garlock EVSP Simplified –DuPont Kalrez –RM Graphaseal
–Garlock 5888 (Teflon) –Argo Composite w/o Teflon spacers

![Fig. 1400-16 PERF Project 90-05 Base Phase—Packing Emissions Comparison at 100% Compression for Block Valves](figures/03-piping-materials-and-line-classes-16_p0033_fig01_fig-1400-16-packing-emissions-comparison.png)

The following are additional results and conclusions of the PERF 90-05 study:

Significant emission differences were observed among packings as shown in Figure 1400-16. Five packings (group D) had measured emission rates of 0.06 - 0.2 lb/yr while the group A packings had >200 lb/yr emissions, a factor of 1000 difference.

- No packing tested could achieve zero emissions, and some of the tested packings may not achieve emissions as low as 500 ppm, as required by some regulations.

- The more expensive packings will not necessarily have lower emissions.

- The manufacturer's recommended packing compression level may not always be the optimum for lowest emissions. The effect of packing compression on valve emissions varies significantly among packing types. Some packings are more "forgiving" than others during installation. Emissions from the best performing packings were, in general, less sensitive to the level of compression than other packings.

- Lower density (70 lb/ft³) die-formed graphite packings resulted in lower average emissions than higher density (95 lb/ft³) packings.

- Packing manufacturers recommended either a certain "percent physical compression of the packing" or a certain "packing gland bolt torque" to be applied to their packing. It was found that it is more reliable and easier to apply and measure "packing gland bolt torque" at installation than "percent compression".

- For the most part, the relative performance of the packings did not change over the 9 month "Aging Phase".

- Valve cycling increased emissions for most packings.

- Repeat tightening to initial compression levels (with a torque wrench) in general improved the performance of the packings.

- The presence of the Teflon spacers between the ARGO packing graphite rings reduced the emissions control effectiveness of the packing.

Because these results were obtained under a carefully controlled experimental set-up, with new identical valves under constant pressure of methane at ambient temperature, they may not be applicable to all possible conditions that can be encountered in an industrial facility.

**Field Installation Emissions Testing (PERF 91-17).** Upon the completion of the previous PERF study (90-05), it was decided that field data was necessary to confirm the performance of low-emission valve packings. Five petroleum companies sponsored a multi-year research project, PERF 91-17, to gather data on the emissions performance from field installations of the best performing packings identified in the previous study [9]. A total of 178 block valves from 12 sites were

monitored under this project. Nine different packing types were used. Eight different service groups were identified from the data which allowed a comparison of the packing performance in similar service conditions.

In summary, the project found that the plant data verified the results obtained previously in the laboratory testing under Project 90-05. The packings which have the lowest emissions include:

- Garlock (or equal) braided end-rings and die-formed middle rings (low density)
- Garlock EVSP Simplified
- Argo Composite
- Braided spool packing (mostly Graphaseal)

The emissions performance found in this study is shown in Figure 1400-17 for various packing types. The key features of this box plot are labeled on the far right of the figure. One packing, Carrara GR8807 (listed as a die-formed braid), had much higher emissions than the other packings, however, this may partly be due to the fact that this packing was only in the most severe service group (highest temperatures and pressures). All of this data must be treated with caution since the data is limited with some packing types being represented in as few as five valves.

This field study found that the median emissions for all packings were relatively low (below 250 ppm). The project also found that valves in high temperature and pressure services (700-1050°F, 450-1010 psig) had the highest median emissions. In all cases where excessive emissions from a valve were detected, the valves could be brought into compliance simply by tightening the packing gland.

**Spool Type Packing Emissions Testing (PERF 94-05).** There has been increasing interest by companies in using spool type (braided graphite) packing (without die-formed middle rings) because of the advantages of this packing type in field repair. The advantages include:

- the packing can be sized by the installer at the valve location in the field
- multiple sizes of die-formed rings do not need to be kept in stock
- trips to the stockroom for correct size packing are eliminated

Because of this interest, another PERF study was conducted to evaluate the performance of spool type (braided graphite) packing during both fire tests and emission tests [10]. The following nine braided packing types were evaluated:

–Garlock 1393 FEP &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;–Slade 3300G &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;–Nippon Pillar 6711

Only Garlock 1303 FEP passed all the test requirements, including the emissions requirement that the packing not exceed 500 ppm (after 1 tightening).

## Packing Guidelines from Valve Manufacturers

Valve manufacturers often use their own choice of packing as standard packings in new valves. Specifying a different packing type when purchasing new valves could increase the price significantly since the valve is packed with the standard packing and then repacked before shipping with the specified packing. To avoid this additional cost, and to make sure that valve manufacturers understand Chevron's packing needs, use the following packing description, which outlines the minimum packing requirements [12]:

Gland packing shall consist of three to five die-formed flexible graphite middle rings, with one braided carbon/graphite ring top and bottom. Materials shall conform to the following:

**Braided Carbon/Graphite** - Fibers shall contain 95% carbon by weight minimum (Union Carbide, Polycarbon, or equal approved by purchaser), zinc powder or molybdenum corrosion inhibitor, graphite lubricant, and 100 ppm leachable chlorides maximum, 1000 ppm total sulfur maximum. Ring construction shall be interbraid weave and shall have no more than 5% weight loss upon ignition. No Teflon suspension shall be used in the manufacture of this packing.

**Flexible Graphite** - Shall contain 95% carbon by weight minimum (Union Carbide, Polycarbon, or equal approved by purchaser), zinc powder or molybdenum corrosion inhibitor, 100 ppm leachable chlorides maximum, 1000 ppm

total sulfur maximum, and be free of lubricant and binders. Material construction shall be die-formed rings or flexible graphite spiral pack, pre compressed to 70-90 lb/ft³ density. New valves shall not contain split rings.

Packing leak rate shall not exceed 100 ppm. (This final requirement can be relaxed to 500 ppm if it will still satisfy current and future regulatory leak limits.)

In addition, it is recommended that new valves be tagged or marked with the type of packing installed so that the facility can track the packing's emission performance in their database.

The best performing packings listed earlier (based on the research studies) do not have to be specified for new valves if the valve manufacturer's standard packings meet the above criteria. These packing requirements were used in selecting the recommended and acceptable valve manufacturers (discussed in "Valve Manufacturer & Reconditioner Alliances" on page 1400-22).

Facilities should not be restricted to using the packing types that were determined to be best performers in the above studies for valve repacking. New packing types that have not been evaluated in studies can still be used by the facilities for VOC service valves provided that the packing manufactures follow the above recommended guidelines and provide test data to show the packing's leak performance. It is recommended that valves with any new packing types be tracked in a data management system and periodically be evaluated to determine the effectiveness of the packing in reducing fugitive emissions.

## Bellows Sealed Valves [4]

An alternative to using low emission valve packing to control valve emissions is to use bellows sealed valves. Figure 1400-21 shows a typical bellows sealed valve. Because the bellows covers the valve stem, emissions from the valve are essentially zero during normal operation. However, in the event the bellows breaks or cracks, the resulting leak initially may be large due to the back-up packing system being loose. The packing, once tightened, should give anti-emission performance equivalent to a regular packed valve. In addition to fugitive reduction considerations, some other factors that must be considered when evaluating the use of bellows sealed valves include intended service, stem sizing and orientation, and cost.

Manufacturer-cited examples of services where bellows sealed valves were tested and demonstrated good performance are steam and benzene. The application in steam service is driven by the economics of maintenance cost reduction and elimination of steam loss. Application in benzene service is to satisfy regulations for emission control [6]. There are also reports of the use of bellows sealed valves in dry gaseous chlorine service. Bellows sealed valves are an option for VOC service, and they may be the preferred solution when frequent monitoring is not possible or desired. The El Segundo Refinery has had a good experience with using bellows sealed valves in several services over a four year time period (1992-1996).

Considerations for the bellows sealed valve selection are both technical and economical. Technically, in order to accommodate the physical size of the bellows, the bonnet and stem need to be extended. This adds to the overall weight and stem height of the valve. For valves up to NPS 1-1/2 the weight and height increases are moderate, but for larger valves the increases can create pipe routing and support problems. For new projects, the larger physical size needs to be accounted for but should not create any problems.

Orientation of the bellows sealed valves needs to receive special attention. The preferred orientation is a vertical stem position to facilitate draining of the bonnet and to prevent settlement of particulates and scale in the bonnet cavity. Accumulation of scale in the bonnet is undesirable from two aspects: interference with the bellows movement and as a site for inducing pit corrosion of the bellows. Drain valve installations generally require a horizontal valve stem position, which may be feasible for clean fluid services.

The bellows may be manufactured by three processes: mechanical forming, hydraulic forming, or welding. The welded bellows are shorter for the same stem movement but tend to have shorter cycle life and are more vulnerable to particulate matter accumulation than the formed bellows. At the present time the welded bellows are also more expensive. Our recommendation is to use hydroformed bellows unless the added expense of welded bellows is necessary for the valve to fit into a tight layout.

Economical considerations are also a big part of selection. In general the price of bellows sealed valves runs about 6 times higher than regular packed valves and the price difference increases as the valve size increases. Limiting the recommended bellows sealed valve sizes to less than two inches is based on the high cost of larger valves and the difficulties of accommodating the increased stem and bonnet lengths. It should be kept in mind that the initial cost of bellows sealed valves may be offset by lower inspection and maintenance costs for facilities where the regulatory agency will accept bellows sealed valves as "leakless" technology that does not need to be monitored regularly. If this is the case, bellows sealed valves may pay out over the long term.

Analyses of Richmond Refinery data showed similar control effectiveness for bellows sealed valves (>99%) and valves with conventional packing (>98%). In most cases, the very small additional emissions control provided by bellows sealed valves over a good valve packing does not justify the cost of bellows sealed valves in VOC service. However, for benzene service, because of the low allowable exposure limit, bellows sealed valves are recommended for use for valves less than two inches [6].

There may be other situations were regulatory agencies require bellows seal technology for small size valves. In cases where bellows sealed valves are being proposed as a requirement, it may be possible to work with the agency and use low emission valve packing data to relax the requirement.

## 1433 Packing Installation for Hand Operated Valves

Installing the packing properly is critical for reducing fugitive emissions from valves. Various aspects of packing installation are covered in this section, including an example of the impact packing procedures may have on emission rates, information on packing configurations, installation procedure, and enhancements for improved performance.

### Summary

A summary of Company recommendations follows:

- All existing packing should be removed carefully, preferably with a water jet, before installing new packing.

- Visually inspect the valve condition to make sure no obvious flaws exist, such as a corroded stem, which would affect the packing performance. A damaged valve may need to be reconditioned or replaced.

- In general, manufacturer guidelines, if available, should be followed when installing packing.

- Train personnel or contractors on installing new packings before large repacking efforts take place. If possible, have packing manufacturers give the training.

- Verify that the correct packing is being installed considering the valve service. Use packing with available information on its ability to control emissions well (vendor, facility, or PERF data).

- For deep stuffing boxes, reduce the depth by using a carbon bushing or central lantern ring.

- While packing, each ring should be compressed 2-3%. When the entire packing set is in place, it should be compressed to about 20-25% less than the original height (30% for spool type packing). Alternatively, a torque wrench can be used to tighten the packing gland if a recommended bolt torque is specified by the manufacturer.

Installing the packing correctly is a critical component to controlling valve emissions, along with having the valve in good condition and using the right packing. The above recommendations will help ensure that the packing is installed correctly.

### Importance of Proper Installation

In addition to making sure the valve is in good condition, and that the appropriate packing type has been chosen for a service, installing the packing correctly also plays a key role in achieving low fugitive emissions. Improper installation could likely outweigh any benefits resulting from installing low emission valve packing. Some data has shown that when personnel unfamiliar with a particular packing type installed that packing into valves, emission increased dramatically. The conclusion was that the emissions increase was likely due improper packing being installed.

Much time, effort, and money can be saved by installing packing carefully and properly, thereby avoiding having to repair or repack the valves later.

If outside contractors are used for repacking valves, it may be necessary to certify their training or to have facility personnel oversee their performance. Having a Chevron employee overseeing the repacking effort will make sure that Chevron's interests are represented. Familiarity with the facility will also diminish work effort and the possibility of costly mistakes.

For any large valve repacking effort where new personnel, contractors, or packing type are being used, training on the correct installation procedures is recommended. It may be possible to have a representative from the packing manufacturer provide the training.

## Packing Configurations

This section discusses the following packing configurations:

- Flexible Graphite
- Braided Graphite Systems
- Teflon

**Flexible Graphite.** The recommended configuration for flexible graphite packing system is the "stacked" configuration, shown in Figure 1400-23. It consists of three die-formed flexible graphite rings, with one braided graphite end ring top and bottom. The end rings serve to prevent extrusion of the die-formed rings between the valve stem and gland and to wipe the stem and keep it free of graphite buildup. It also increases the resiliency of the flexible graphite, and thus decreases the frequency of packing adjustments. For valves with lantern rings, the recommendation is to use a stack, both above and below the lantern ring.

The required number of rings will vary depending upon the depth of the packing chamber, but should not exceed seven rings. **Ideally, only five rings should be used.** One of the findings of the EPRI report completed in May 1988, entitled "Valve Stem Packing Improvements," was that deep stuffing boxes are inefficient and can actually reduce the effectiveness of the packing [13]. EPRI found that, with more than five rings, gland pressure is not sufficiently transmitted to the lower rings, and therefore they are not compressed. Later, the packing loosens to accommodate the extra space left by the lower rings and the seal is lost or reduced.

Older valves with deep packing chambers should have a carbon or metal bushing installed in the bottom of the packing chamber so that the optimum five-ring set can be used. If that is not possible, for deeper stuffing boxes with seven or more rings, we recommend that a central lantern ring or a load braided ring be installed mid-way to compensate for the additional depth (Figure 1400-24).

**Braided Graphite Systems.** Braided graphite system installation is similar to flexible graphite packing installation, except that five rings of the spool packing should be used instead of using die-formed middle rings. A bushing should be installed to compensate for a deeper stuffing box instead of installing additional rings.

![Fig. 1400-24 Packing Guide for Deep Stuffing Boxes](figures/03-piping-materials-and-line-classes-16_p0048_fig02_fig-1400-24-packing-guide-deep-stuffing.png)

**Teflon Packing Systems.** Teflon packing systems will resemble flexible graphite systems except that braided end rings are not used. Die-formed Teflon rings are installed in a stacked configuration. The number of rings to install and deep stuffing box requirements should be covered in the manufactures' instructions.

## Installation Procedures

Proper attention during packing installation is essential to maintaining an effective seal. Some typical maintenance problems are: failure to remove all old packing, installing the wrong packing size, improper packing technique, improper gland loading, and not paying attention to the condition of the valve.

To remove existing packing, a water pick or water jet is recommended. It may be possible to rent the necessary equipment for use during large repacking efforts. If regular picks or hooks are used, care should be taken not to damage the stem. Be sure that all of the old packing is removed before installing new packing.

Typically, packing manufacturers will provide information on how to install packing systems. If these instructions are not provided automatically with the packing purchase, they should be requested. Some manufacturers will provide training on-site on how to properly install packing upon request.

For braided carbon or graphite installation, the selected width of the packing corresponds to the difference between stem diameter and packing chamber wall. The length of packing for each ring is found by wrapping the packing around a stem-sized mandrel and cutting the packing strip at 45° to level to produce overlap ends. The cutting tool should be a razor-blade or other very sharp tool to produce smooth cuts and to minimize damage to the packing structure. The rings should be installed with the split staggered as specified in the manufacturers' instructions.

Die-formed rings should be installed without any splits if possible, which requires that the valve be disassembled. If die-formed rings are to be installed in the field, they should be cut at 45° (to level) and very carefully twisted and slid over the stem. Care should be taken not to damage the packing structure. The rings should be installed with the splits staggered to prevent the formation of a leak path.

After the packing is installed, the valve should be stroked several times to ensure proper packing adjustment. Reset the packing to the recommended compression levels if necessary. Then the valve should be pressurized and checked for leakage. In the case of leakage, each gland bolt nut should be tightened approximately one-fourth turn or two flats. Finally, the valve needs to be rechecked for leakage.

## Gland Adjustment and Compression

In most cases, manufacturers will recommend an appropriate bolt torque or physical compression level for their packings. However, as the PERF studies revealed, these recommendations are not always the ideal compression level. If a particular packing does not work as well as expected with the recommended packing compression, one should work with the manufacturer to determine a more appropriate compression level.

Follow manufacturer instructions on installation and compression if available. Recommended compression levels for some packing are included in Figures Figure 1400-29 on page 1400-66 and Figure 1400-31 on page 1400-68. If no specific information is available, use the following guidelines:

- For die-formed ring systems, use the packing gland and a spacer ring to precompress each die-formed ring 2-3% of its original height as it is installed. After the entire packing set has been installed, compress the stack 20-25% from its original height to achieve a seal.

- For braided graphite systems, install the first four rings and then tighten the packing gland until resistance is felt. Then install the fifth ring, and compress the entire set 30%. [8,10]

Alternatively, some manufacturers may specify a specific bolt torque be used in order to achieve the compression necessary for a seal. In this case, a torque wrench can be used.

## Packing and Stuffing Box Enhancements

Enhancements to the standard flexible graphite packing configuration include wedge packing and live loading.

**Wedge Systems [4].** There are a number of improvements available to the standard square flexible graphite packing configuration. Generally, these try to maximize the transfer of axial gland loading to radial packing movement, thus obtaining a "better" seal. Examples of these are Garlock's EVSP 9000 (expandable valve stem packing) and the EPRI Wedge System, manufactured by Chesterton. Garlock's EVSP will expand approximately 1/8 inch on the diameter, or 1/16 inch per side. (This is compared to about 1/32 inch per side expansion for square packing, according to Garlock.) The packing sets come as "standard" and "simplified." The die-formed rings are cut at angles in the EVSP and EPRI designs so that they slide toward the stem or stuffing box wall as the gland is tightened. EVSP uses two different ring densities - 70 and 45 lb/ft³. (See Figure 1400-25.) Garlock EVSP has performed well in emissions tests in hand operated valves.

Wedge type systems, for the most part, have been applied in actuated valves, motor operated valves and control valves. The advantage in these applications is that the packing tends to correct for wear due to frequent stem movement. Vendors claim that they also help to compensate for worn or pitted stems. Control valves will be discussed more in Section 1440, "Controlling Fugitive Emissions in Automatic Control Valves."

**Live-Loading.** The live-load system centers on Belleville spring washers, available for many years for piping flanges, heat exchanger channels, etc. Their function is to maintain a constant load on the packing, regardless of stem wear or temperature changes induced by the process. The load system stores from 20 to 30 times more energy than the conventional system. Figure 1400-26 shows a generic live-load system. Be aware that live-load systems do not increase gland loads over time, but only maintain the initial gland load.

Packing loads can change due to packing wear at the stem, consolidation of the packing, or temperature changes in the valve. A common problem occurs when a hot valve is opened. The hot stem, which has thermally expanded, passes through the packing, compressing the packing toward the packing box. As the extended stem cools, the packing may not be resilient enough to expand again toward the stem.

![Fig. 1400-25 Cup and Cone Type System Courtesy of Garlock Sealing Technologies](figures/03-piping-materials-and-line-classes-16_p0051_fig01_fig-1400-25-cup-and-cone-type-system.png)

Since the packing is no longer tight against the stem, the packing may leak. The problem is more common in valves with larger stem diameters that undergo more expansion and contraction.

Although live-loaded systems can be used with hand operated block valves, they typically are used with automatic control valves. Each operating company must judge whether live loading or wedge-type packing enhancements are justified for a particular valve. Factors such as fluid toxicity, valve accessibility, and valve availability for shutdown or isolation, must be evaluated on a case-by-case basis. If a valve can be isolated and repacked without undue risk, then these enhancements may not be justified.

## 1440 Controlling Fugitive Emissions in Automatic Control Valves

The main difference between control valves and block valves is their purpose. Control valves are used to vary the flow rate of a process and need to have more precise control over the stem movement. Block valves operate mostly in either the on or off position and are usually subject to less cycling than control valves. Because of this need for automatic control valves to maintain reliable and precise stem movement, automatic control valves have different design specifications and packing requirements than block valves.

This section discusses reducing fugitive emissions from automatic control valves. Factors that play a role in low emissions such as valve condition, valve packing, and packing installation will be discussed.

## 1441 Valve Condition

One key factor to reducing fugitive emissions from control valves is ensuring that the valves being used are in good condition. Stem condition and finish are especially critical. The following discussion details various areas having to do with overall valve condition, including:

- the types of valves and how emissions occur
- valve standards
- valve reconditioning
- the benefits of the newly established valve manufacturer alliance

### Valve Types and How Emissions Occur

One factor in the amount of fugitive emissions released is the valve type. Several types of valves are used in the petrochemical industry for process control. These include sliding stem (globe-style body) and rotary valves. Bellows are offered for sliding stem valves. Variations at the valve body affect where and how fugitive emissions take place. Emissions are the result of leakage at the base of the valve stem as well as at the valve bonnet. The most common source of leaks is at the seal between the stem and the packing.

Sliding stem valves operate by adjusting the distance between the plug and the seat to change the flow rate through the valve. A linear motion of the valve stem is used to change the relative position of the plug with respect to the seat. Ball and butterfly valves use a rotary motion to position the disk or ball into the path of fluid flow. Bellows sealed valves are a variation of the sliding stem valve with the stem encased in a bellows like sheath that prevents any emissions from escaping around the valve stem.

Rotary valves were initially expected to offer an advantage in fugitive emission controls over sliding stem valves. Some manufacturers still maintain that hydrocarbon emissions are lower through a "rotary seal" around a shaft than through a "sliding stem" seal between the stem and the control valve packing. Actual performance data shows that sliding stem valves with live loaded fugitive emissions packing systems perform better than rotary valve fugitive emissions type packing systems.

### Valve Standards

There are many factors to consider when selecting and specifying control valves including fire safety, ability to perform during process upsets, good control characteristics, and low fugitive emissions. The design of control valves is covered in detail in Chevron's *Instrumentation and Control Manual* [15].

Two industry standards have been recently developed to cover control valve packing performance ANSI/FCI 91-1-1994 defines performance standards for sliding stem control valve packing systems in fugitive emission applications. ANSI/ISA Standard 93 is the standard for certifying fugitive emissions performance of rotary control valve packing systems.

## Maintenance and Reconditioning

The Richmond Refinery has developed a process to recondition or retrofit VOC service control valves to ensure that they comply with the 100 ppm fugitive emissions limits set by the Bay Area Air Quality Management District in 1997. The process includes:

- using a Management of Change (MOC) document
- issuing a standing Shop Order to recondition/retrofit fugitive emission packing systems into VOC service control valves
- defining the process the maintenance personnel must follow to ensure that the right packing is selected and installed into the VOC service control valves
- training the maintenance personnel must go through to ensure that they are properly trained to execute the modifications

This process, when implemented and debugged, will be shared with other facilities to ensure that they have a template to follow in setting up processes that comply with local fugitive emission regulations. The El Segundo Refinery is already using the process prototype to develop and document their own procedures.

Stem condition and finish is one of the most critical components in maintaining a low rate of fugitive emissions. Stem finishes of 4-rms provided the best performance during the in-house study. If a valve is not performing well and has obvious mechanical defects, it should be removed and reconditioned. At the minimum, the stem should be replaced and a live-loaded packing should be installed.

End users need to ensure that the valves they purchase or have reconditioned are handled with the care necessary to ensure proper operation after installation. Avoid lifting valves by their actuators and imposing lateral forces on the stem; instead, cradle/sling the body area with nonmetallic slings. Improper lifting methods can bow the valve stem which in turn will warp the stem passage through the packing and contribute to leakage problems.

## 1442 Packing for Automatic Control Valves

Tighter regulatory standards require higher performance packing systems. Conservation of resources, plant safety, environmental considerations and ever tightening controls governing fugitive emissions of Volatile Organic Compound (VOC) stocks require that careful attention be given to the selection and installation of the most effective packing systems. Approval of these materials and packing set arrangements is based upon the best available test data and is subject to change pending the outcome of future field performance tests.

Packings that are acceptable for hand operated block valves produce extremely high stem friction forces. These forces do not affect performance of hand operated block valves because block valves are either wide open or completely shut for extended periods of time. Automatic control valves, however, must constantly modulate to maintain or change process flow rates, and must be able to match controller output within 0.5% to 2%. Installation of block valve style packings in control valves, after asbestos was outlawed, resulted in existing actuators being undersized and control valve hysteresis went up to as high as 15%.

### Summary

In developing the VOC Packing Replacement Process at the Richmond Refinery we found that graphite packings will produce excessive friction and will disable advanced control strategy applications unless oversize actuators are specified. As a result, the recommendation is to use live-loaded, combination packings as the first choice, as long as they meet the temperature limitations of the fluorocarbons used in the packings.

For higher temperatures (over 450°F), graphite packings, oversize actuators, and positioners are required for advanced control strategy applications.

## Packing Types for Automatic Control Valves

**Packing Description.** Packings, installed into a packing gland assembly, prevent leakage of process fluid past the stem or shaft. The packing gland assembly consists of a packing box machined into the valve bonnet, a wiper ring, a lantern ring, packing, a packing follower, a packing flange, and stud bolts and nuts. The wiper ring sits at the bottom of the packing box and provides mechanical support for the lantern ring. The lantern ring is a metallic spacer that provides mechanical support for the packing and also provides a cavity to receive the lubricant when lubricant is used. The packing flange contacts the follower which has a ball-shaped surface to compensate for unintentional misalignment of the packing flange thus minimizing side loading on the stem. Adjustment to nuts on packing gland studs compress the packing against the packing box walls and the surface of the stem.

A loosely adjusted packing will allow process fluid to leak past the packing. Over-tightened packing will "grab" the stem and will keep it from moving. Some packing gland designs use two sets of packings separated by the lantern ring. In these designs, the lantern ring cavity can be used either to accept the lubricant, or, in a dry packing system, to monitor intermediate packing leakage through the lubricant and isolating valve connection. All manufacturers specify that a torque wrench be used to adjust the stud bolt nuts to ensure that packing is tightened properly.

*Historical Overview.* For control valves, past practice has been to use asbestos composition "jam-type" packings, where a packing was installed into a packing gland. The stud nuts tightened, and the resiliency of the packing provided the sealing forces needed to keep the process from leaking. To reduce packing friction and to optimize sealing, lubricants were injected into the lantern ring area. Lubricants filled the voids in the resilient packing as well as filled the sacrificial voids between the packing and the stem. Most petrochemical facilities used Teflon packings, which consisted of Teflon formed into either solid rings, split rings, or "chevron vee rings." Teflon chevron rings relied on the mechanical shape of the rings to provide the needed lateral loading to maintain the seal.

Use of asbestos was banned by the EPA in the late 1980's. At about the same time, Teflon was banned from hydrocarbon services because it is not fire-safe. In 1990, the Clean Air Act required strict fugitive emission sealing performance.

Today, control valve packings must be fire-safe, mechanically reliable, chemically inert, must have high sealing capability, and must produce minimal friction on the stem. To meet these criteria, control valve packing material options have been narrowed to fluorocarbons (Teflon or Kal-Rez), graphitic packings, and combination packings (combination of fluorocarbon and graphite).

**Fluorocarbon Packings.** Fluorocarbon packings are chemically inert in most process applications, provide outstanding sealing characteristics, and have a very low coefficient of friction. However, fluorocarbon packings are not fire-safe and are also temperature limited. Teflon sublimates at about 450°F. Kal-Rez decomposes at about 500°F. They are the optimum choice for most chemical and non-hydrocarbon services. Teflon packings come in solid, split-ring and chevron form. Kal-Rez comes in chevron form only. Chevron packings are usually installed with anti-extru-

sion back-up rings to preclude packing degradation through cold flow. Fluorocarbon packings can only be used for hydrocarbon services in the "combination" form described below. Fluorocarbon packings can be installed in "jam-type" packing glands, however, for optimum performance (long intervals between maintenance while complying with low fugitive emission limits) they should be installed in "live-loaded" packing gland assemblies. Live loading is produced by Belleville spring washers which deliver a constant and uniform load on the packing.

**Graphic Packings.** Graphic packings (graphite or carbon) come in pre-formed rings that slip over the valve stem or in braided ribbon form. Graphitic packings are relatively inert and can be used for most hydrocarbon services. Some die-formed graphite rings have a temperature rating of up to 1,000°F. Braided ribbon packings are easy to handle but are difficult to install correctly. Proper installation procedures require that graphitic packings are installed one layer at a time with each layer being "crushed" to eliminate all voids. When multiple layers are installed without the intermediate "crushing," only the top layer gets loaded and properly. Voids remain in the uncompressed layers and packings start to leak shortly after installation. Graphitic packings, once compressed, do not have the property to "spring back" when compressive forces are released (or when packing material volume is reduced through consolidation and/or wear). Experience shows that properly installed graphitic packings perform best when they are live-loaded.

Graphitic packings have a coefficient of friction that is many times higher (up to thirty times higher) than fluorocarbon packings. To overcome packing friction forces, control valves with graphitic packings require larger actuators than valves with fluorocarbon packings. Valves which initially had Teflon packings but were retrofitted to graphitic packings (late 1980s) typically ended up with undersized actuators. Several Chevron facilities tried using braided graphite packings with lubricants to both reduce friction and to "seal" the voids. Experience shows that lubricated braided graphitic packings give borderline performance and require high maintenance. The lubricant must be replenished frequently while friction is not reduced enough to provide good control valve performance.

**Combination Packings.** Combination packings, which were introduced in early to mid-90s are the most efficient, and in the long run most economical packing type. Within their temperature limits they satisfy all the criteria that a packing must meet. Combination packings consist of fluorocarbon chevron rings supplemented with solid graphite rings. Fluorocarbon rings provide low friction and excellent sealing while graphite rings satisfy the fire-safety requirements by restricting hydrocarbon releases if the fluorocarbon packings are destroyed by a flash fire. Combination packings also require live-loading to work effectively.

Packing recommendations for specific petrochemical services are tabulated in Specification ICM-EG-2350 "Control Valve Fabrication", located in the *Instrumentation and Control Manual*, and are also discussed in the following section.

**Special Note on Lubricants.** Modern control valve packing systems are designed for long-term, maintenance-free operation and are designed to work without lubricants. Periodic injection of packing lubricant is labor intensive, drives up maintenance costs, and should be considered only as a "last resort," e. g., in a situation

where a packing has developed a leak and the valve cannot be taken out of service (no bypass manifold) until the plant is shut down for maintenance.

Lubricants, when used, must be injected through lubricator and isolating valve assemblies (lube & iso valves) which must be specified at the time the valve is purchased. New control valves should be specified without lube & iso valves but should be specified with their bonnets drilled, tapped, and plugged for future addition of lube & iso valves if needed. Lube & iso valves should be specified at time of purchase only if the valve will be installed without a bypass manifold. Lubricants for specific process applications are tabulated in Specification ICM-EG-2350.

## Chevron Packing Guidelines

Valve manufacturers are working feverishly to develop valve packing systems that will comply with fugitive emission regulations. The valve manufacturers dilemma is that for a valve to control efficiently, packing friction must be minimized. High packing friction contributes to hysteresis, which is not acceptable in control valves. To minimize packing friction, valve packing compression forces have to be minimized. Reducing valve packing compression reduces the effectiveness of the valve packing and allows VOCs to escape.

Teflon packing minimizes packing friction but by itself is not acceptable to Chevron from a fire safety standpoint. Graphite packings are satisfactory for fire safety but impose large friction forces on the control valve stems. Reducing compressive forces on the graphite packings may not meet the fugitive emissions requirements. In 1993 the Fire Protection Staff approved the use of the combination of graphite and Teflon-type rings for hydrocarbon applications within Chevron.

Lubricants and grease seals are being used in some Chevron locations as an interim solution. In the hydrocarbon industry, lubricators and lubricants were always furnished on control valve packing glands. "Grease seals" were once the only acceptable method to control unacceptable H₂S and mercaptan leakage from control valve packings. Non-lubricated systems will be required in the long term, and grease seals will be considered acceptable only for emergency repair.

## Manufacturer Packing Guidelines

In 1994, the Fluid Control Institute published ANSI/FCI 91-1-1994, "Standard for Qualification of Control Valve Stem Seals to Meet EPA Emission Guidelines for Volatile Organic Compounds." This standard defines and classifies performance levels that control valve packing systems must meet and requires the manufacturer to certify that their packing system will meet the required performance level. For example, Class A1 defines a packing system which will not exceed 100 ppm emissions over a period of 100,000 mechanical cycles and three thermal cycles (60°F to 450°F to 60°F = one thermal cycle). ISA is in the process of publishing a similar standard for rotary control valve packing systems.

## Packing Performance Studies

As part of the PERF 91-17 Project, Chevron has been monitoring packing fugitive emissions performance at several Chevron facilities. Test data shows that use of lubricants and grease seals to meet VOC fugitive emission compliance is only marginally successful. Lubricated valves are barely able to meet the 500 ppm emissions limit while they require frequent packing gland adjustments and lubricant injection. Also, lubricating graphitic packings does not seem to reduce the high friction that is normally associated with dry graphitic packings. Because of high friction, control performance of valves with lubricated graphitic packings has not been entirely acceptable. By comparison, live-loaded "dry" packing systems that Chevron is monitoring for fugitive emissions have been delivering better than 50 ppm sealing performance (< 20 ppm average emissions) for over two years without any maintenance or adjustments. Because properly sized actuators have been furnished for these "dry" packings, control valve performance has also been outstanding.

## Hermetically Sealed Valves

Control valves with bellows seals fall into the "hermetically" sealed control valve category. They provide zero leakage as long as the bellows are intact. Bellows seals have a history of work hardening, fatiguing, and failing. Bellows seals are usually furnished with Teflon back-up packing systems. Although manufacturers have changed bellows seal design and construction to extend bellows seal life, Chevron's most recent experience was that a "new design" bellows seal failed within four months after being placed in service. When a bellows seal fails the entire bonnet assembly must be replaced as a unit. Replacement bellows seals cost between six and ten times more than live-loaded, fire-safe fugitive emission-type packings.

One control valve company advertises their valves as hermetically sealed "zero-leakage" control valves for refinery services. The valves were developed for distilled water control on nuclear powered submarines, and, with cutbacks in military spending, had to look for new markets. The company picked the petrochemical industry because it is a major user of control valves. Four valves were installed successfully in Chevron to control clean (filtered and polished) products in a pipeline application where reliable instrument air was not available.

Although the sales representatives claimed that the valve is the answer to fugitive emission concerns in all petroleum services, the company's technical manager says that the valves are susceptible to plugging in over 75% of petroleum processing applications. The valves are actuated by means of a large solenoid coil which opens or closes a pilot port inside the control valve body. To open or close the valve, the pilot diverts process pressure to the upstream or downstream side of the plug. Valve position is fed back to remote control electronics through a Linear Variable Differential Transformer (LVDT). For maintenance considerations, the electronics must be mounted remotely in an environment that is protected from rain and electrically classified as General Purpose.

Failure mode of these valves is indeterminable because of many parameters that can fail, e.g., power failure, electronics failure, loose wiring, loss of process pressure, etc., each of which would make the valve fail in a different direction. This would be a safety concern because a control valve must fail in a predetermined, fail-safe direction. Because of different signal levels (power to coil and LVDT feedback), two conduits must be run from the remote electronics to each control valve. As a result, the total initial installed cost of these valves can be six to ten times higher than the cost of conventional control valves. There is no operating and maintenance cost data available to establish the total life cycle cost of these control valves.

## 1443 Packing Installation for Automatic Control Valves

Environmental packings should be installed per manufacturers procedures, guidelines and recommendations. Critical items that must be checked when retrofitting high-performance, fugitive-emission packings into existing control valves are:

### a. Stem condition and finish

For high-performance, fugitive-emission packings to perform effectively a stem finish of 4 rms is required. Benchmarking of competitors' practices shows that

it is more cost effective to replace the control valve stem each time a VOC service control valve comes into the shop for repairs than it is to inspect the condition of the stem and to try to re-machine it to restore the right surface finish.

#### b. Dimensions and condition of the packing box

The dimensions and condition of the packing box determine if process fluid will have an external leak path through the packing gland. With input from our Alliance partner, Fisher-Rosemount, Chevron is currently developing a matrix that tabulates the packing gland dimensions, tolerances, and surface finish for each major control valve manufacturer. Based on this matrix and the condition of the packing box a maintenance person would be able to decide whether the bonnet needs to be re-machined, re-sleeved, or completely replaced.

#### c. Training

To install high-performance, fugitive-emission packings into existing control valves properly, maintenance personnel must be trained to satisfy federal (OSHA) regulations. The training that maintenance personnel must go through is also being developed jointly by Fisher-Rosemount and Chevron.

# 1450 Leak Repair

Another factor in achieving reduced fugitive emissions from valves is the proper repair of valves that are determined to be leaking by periodic monitoring of the equipment through some type of Leak Detection and Repair (LDAR) Program. Repairing a valve may consist of anything from tightening the packing gland bolts to removing the valve from service altogether and replacing it. This section will give a brief discussion of the various repair methods used in the field and their relative success rate based on refinery monitoring data.

## 1451 Summary

The following summarizes current leak repair recommendations:

- When a valve is determined to be leaking, the first attempt at repair should be to tighten the packing gland and fittings (a torque wrench can be used if specified in the packing's instructions). If this does not stop the leak then other options need to be evaluated.

- If the valve can be taken out of service, it should be checked for corrosion damage and repacked using low emission valve packing. If repacking does not stop the leak or there are signs of corrosion or damage, the valve should be put on the list for reconditioning or replacement at the next shutdown.

- If the valve cannot be taken out of service, and it cannot wait until the next shutdown for repair (toxic material or compliance violation), the valve can be leak-sealed. Injection of soft packing may be successful in stopping leaks but should only be used as a last resort.

## 1452 Repair Methods

Some of the common, recorded repair methods used are:

- Adjust Packing Gland
- Inject Soft Packing (Orbits, WKM's etc.)
- Repack Valve
- Leak Seal (Furmanite, Kopp)
- Replace Valve or Fitting
- Tighten Flange or fitting
- Renew Gaskets or O-Rings
- Apply Thread Sealant
- Install Plug
- Replace with Upgrade
- Renew Minor Parts
- Rebuild Component, Packing Box
- Adjust Gland & Inject Soft Packing
- Repair in Shop
- Use Cartridge Seal Repair

In general, the first attempt at repair should be a tightening of the packing gland and other fittings as this can usually bring the valve into compliance. If this does not work, try alternative methods. Repacking the valve is an effective option if the valve can be taken out of service. The valve should be reconditioned at the next shutdown if it is obviously in poor condition. Some of these methods are far more time consuming and costly than others and should be used only if other methods of repair fail.

There are some procedures for tightening that can enhance the effectiveness of the repair [14]. Overtightening can damage a flange, gasket or packing and increase leakage. Unequal tightening of bolts that are spaced around a flange can result in higher leakage. Tightening the bolts in the wrong order can create stresses in the gasket that result in higher leakage. Based on these principles, the following protocol is suggested for flanges:

- Tighten the first bolt (selected at random) by one half turn.
- Tighten the bolt 180 degrees from the first bolt by one half turn.
- Tighten the bolt at 90 degrees from the first bolt by one half turn.
- Tighten the bolt at 270 degrees from the first bolt by one half turn.
- If there are more than four bolts, continue to tighten them by a similar procedure that crosses the flange to promote even stresses around the gasket.
- Once all of the bolts are tightened by one half turn, check the emission with a portable instrument.
- If emissions are acceptably low, the repair is finished. If not, then repeat by tightening another one half turn in the same sequence on all bolts.
- Check the emissions and repeat as necessary or until the bolts will not move or are being stripped off, at which point the component would need to be put on the non-repairable list until shutdown.

The above discussion focused on flanges, because that is usually the most complex bolt pattern. The same principles apply to valves, but there are typically only two

packing bolts. The appropriate procedure would be to put one half turn of tightening on each bolt and then check the emission level, repeating until the repair is successful or the component is declared non-repairable.

Other repair methods are lubricating a plug valve, or replacing the cap or plug on an open-ended line that had been left off by mistake.

Many deeper gate or globe valve stuffing boxes have a lantern ring and a "grease" injector. Sealant injection is an option if the valve cannot be taken out of service, but should be considered very carefully, particularly if hot work is required to install injection taps. Also, the effectiveness of sealant injection over long time periods is unknown. The lantern ring provides the mechanical cavity to ensure the flow of sealant around the stem and against the perimeter of the stuffing box. This type of stem packing has been used successfully in services that contain significant levels of hazardous material as a means of stopping minor stem leaks while the plant remains in operation. Sealants should be compatible with packing material and system service. It is important to follow the valve manufacturer's maintenance instructions, and use only the sealant injection equipment recommended. It is possible to over-pressurize a stuffing box and extrude the packing, or, under extreme circumstances, rupture the stuffing box wall and cause a valve failure.

If a valve can be put on the shutdown list, and the leak is not threatening, factors such as needing staging or a fresh air supply should be taken into consideration when deciding whether to attempt an immediate repair or to have the valve be on the "allowable leaker" list.

## 1460 Corrosion of Valve Stems

High levels of salts and other materials are often used in manufacturing mechanical packing. In addition, it is thought that salts can be transferred to the packings during handling and installation. In the presence of water from hydrostatic testing or condensation, those salts go into solution to form an electrolyte, and can cause galvanic corrosion of the metal valve parts. Such corrosion will roughen the stem surface, create leak paths, and increase friction and packing wear. Corrosion is most likely to occur during storage of a packed valve. Galvanic corrosion can be reduced by incorporating a sacrificial material or a passivating inhibitor into the packing materials. Zinc and aluminum powders are used as sacrificial inhibitors (zinc inhibitors are preferred). The passive metallic ones typically are molybdate salts such as MoS₂ or Ba₂Mo.

When PTFE (Teflon) is utilized in service conditions above 450°F it sublimes and gives off hydrogen fluoride which, in the presence of water moisture, forms hydrofluoric acid and can cause stem corrosion. Some graphite packing materials are impregnated with PTFE which can also cause the generation of hydrofluoric acid under service conditions above 450°F. This limitation should be taken into consideration before specifying the use of PTFE packing.

### 1472 Service Limitations

In spite of its sealing advantages in a variety of services, there are a number of service limitations to flexible graphite packings. These are discussed below and are also outlined on Standard Drawing GC-L99771.

Flexible graphite is usable in the full pH range from 0 to 14, but with the service limits stated below.

- Oxidizing acids: chromic acid, nitric acid, >85% phosphoric acid, sulfuric acid

## 1480 Emissions Data and Key Players

### 1481 Emissions Data

With most facilities already subject to some kind of fugitive monitoring requirement, tracking emissions data is already taking place. However, there are many more reasons to track emissions data than compliance. One reason, and benefit, is to track the performance of existing equipment. This information will help identify which equipment is working best in different types of service and will allow for more informed decisions to be made in the future. Another reason is to determine the progress towards meeting an upcoming regulatory requirement and to identify and plan for any necessary improvements to achieve compliance.

Leak Detection and Repair Programs play a huge role in reducing fugitive emission from valves. The wealth of data produced by such programs can be used to improve emissions performance even more. Although many guidelines and recommendations are given in this section and related documents, nothing is better than concrete, accurate facility data. A packing type that performed exceptionally well in one study may not perform well at all in the field for a variety of reasons. Keeping track and using this type of information will alert you to these trouble spots and will allow you to make necessary adjustments. Sharing this information across facilities may also help reduce the burden and cost of LDAR programs.

In order to track this information, a good data management system is needed. The system should be flexible enough to allow entry of the required leak detection data, and also additional data to characterize the monitored components. We recom-

mended that for each valve in the data base, all available data on the following should be noted:

- the make and type of valve
- packing type
- service
- temperature and pressure

Data on valve age, frequency of use, or overall condition can also be recorded. Tracking this information, allows data to be gathered on whether a valve type or particular packing type has a history of leaking or performs particularly well. It can also be used to determine which plants or services are more prone to high leak rates and should receive more monitoring attention. Also, by tracking the average leak rate or percentage leakers over time, one can assess if progress is being made towards upcoming regulatory requirements. The amount of work to be done at upcoming shutdowns to reduce leaking valves can be determined, and manpower and purchasing decisions can be made accordingly.

A robust data management system will allow for the addition of data fields to meet facility needs. It will also perform data queries, extracting screening data for valves meeting a certain set of criteria (i.e., all valves with packing type A in high temperature service). It may also use the screening data to calculate current emission rates based on more accurate correlation equations, which gives a significantly lower emission estimate than average emission factors. If enough screening data is collected, facility specific average emission factors can be developed (with approval from the regulatory agency).

## 1482 Key Players

Reducing fugitive emissions from valves is not a one person job. Many people will play a role including the valve manufacturers and reconditioners, packing manufacturers, maintenance personnel, plant operators, LDAR monitoring personnel (contractors or in-house), environmental staff, purchasing staff, and others. In developing a strategy for reducing fugitive emissions, it is important to communicate with all involved parties, at least within the facility.

One way to foster effective communication is to establish a team of people within the facility to periodically discuss emission reduction strategies, results of the data tracking, compliance status, problem areas and next steps, evolving technology, and purchasing plans for upcoming shutdowns, etc. Establishing a team that works together reduces miscommunication among the involved groups, saves money, and reduces emissions.

Management support is also a key factor in achieving emissions reductions. Management's commitment to this type of effort is necessary to support the manpower resources, costs, and operational issues that might arise. Getting initial management support is key to successfully reducing emissions.
