## 4.2.9 Spent acid flow back

In the spent acid flow back scenario, the effluents can contain a very high chloride brine (up to 300,000 ppm), low pH fluids and additional unknown fluid chemistry. The continuous and/or batch inhibitors in use might not be effective, due to the change in fluid properties and increasing inhibitor adsorption. This is caused by a potentially large concentration of very fine solids from the formation in the spent acid stream. The existing FeS scales on the pipeline might not be stable under this fluid chemistry.

To combat these problems, spent acid flow back fluids are not allowed to enter the pipeline until both of the following conditions are met:

- pH ≥ 4 and
- chlorides ≤ 50,000 ppm.

Temporary separation facilities shall be used, and pH and chloride measurements are required with separate disposal of liquids until the pH and chlorides targets are met.

Once the pH and chloride targets are met, the liquids can be put back in the pipeline. This should be treated as a "start-up", in line with Table 4.2. Should the flowline be batched when liquids are put back into flowlines. Extra continuous corrosion inhibitor should be added in accordance with Table 4.2, depending on flow regime, for up to 2 weeks. Weekly samples of water should be taken and analysed for chlorides, inhibitor residuals, and pH. The extra mitigation requirements given in Table 4.2 shall be maintained until stable well production is established; once this is confirmed the mitigation plan may revert to a steady state production mitigation strategy.

Any corrosion resistant alloys in the systems should be selected against this high chloride rate (300,000 ppm), because cracking due to high chloride levels can happen very quickly.

For all the 8 sour gas types, an enhanced mitigation program shall consist of the following as a minimum:

- batching of the pipeline 2 days to 3 days prior to spent acid flow back;
- daily sampling of the produced water;
- increasing inhibitor injection rates (see Table 4.2) until water chemistry returns to normal;
- weekly batching if the chloride level stays above 25,000 ppm (if normal Cl⁻ level of the fluids is less than 25,000 ppm). Batching will be discontinued when the Cl⁻ level returns to normal.

Due to the complexity of corrosion by spent acid flow back, all enhanced corrosion control programs require approval from the Principal's corrosion engineer.

## 4.2.10 Methanol injection

Experience and research have shown that excessive methanol injected for hydrate control can cause top-of-line (TOL) as well as bottom-of-line (BOL) corrosion in sour systems. According to field experience, "excessive" methanol injection occurs when the volume of injected methanol is greater than the total volume of water produced in the pipeline (water of condensation and of production). The methanol injection is thought to influence corrosion in the following ways:

- The primary effect is that oxygen injected with the methanol causes corrosion (oxygen is much more soluble in MeOH than in water) – this could lead to BOL or TOL corrosion. The oxygen reacts with H₂S to give deposits of elemental sulphur. This is not a significant change for types IS to IIIS, which already contain elemental sulphur, but would, for example, effectively change a type I gas to a type IS fine.
- The secondary effect is that methanol condensing on TOL with water, dissolving corrosion product films and carrying away corrosion products, could lead to added TOL corrosion.
