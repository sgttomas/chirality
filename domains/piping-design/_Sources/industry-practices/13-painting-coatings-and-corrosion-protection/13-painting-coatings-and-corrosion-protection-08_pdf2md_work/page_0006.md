**Table 3.2  Relationship between Corrosion and Flow Regimes in Wet, Sour Gas Transport**

| Flow regime | Detrimental effect of flow | Beneficial effect of flow |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Stratified & V$_{sl}$ < 3 m/s$^{1)}$ | Water separation and settling of solids, leading to protective scale disruption, as follows:<br>- moving solids may erode the protective scale;<br>- corrosion may occur under deposits;<br>- settled solids may prevent the inhibitor reaching the surface;<br>- settled solids may contain elemental sulphur which is itself corrosive and can lead to scale disruption;<br>- high levels of chloride in solution act as a scale disrupter; this is most severe when the water is stagnant or at low flow. | |
| Stratified & V$_{sl}$ > 3 m/s$^{1)}$ | | Solids transported through the system. Formation of protective scales and inhibitor films. |
| Intermittent slug | Protective scales disruption by increased mass transfer and/or mechanical forces and/or removal of inhibitor films. Impact of effect depends on slug characteristics (shear forces). | |
| Annular dispersed flow (or dispersed bubble flow) | | Improved scale protection |

NOTE:  1)  V$_{sl}$ > 3 m/s is used here as a temporary critical velocity above which the risk of scale breakdown due to settling of solids is considerably reduced. This criterion may be later replaced by a more appropriate rule for this transition based on actual flow velocities. The critical velocity for solids transport is V$_t$, the liquid velocity (an actual velocity, not a superficial velocity). The V$_{sl}$ value used here has been practically found to be the required gas velocity to give a high enough V$_t$ to keep the solids suspended. V$_{sl}$ is used rather than V$_t$ because it is easier to measure.
