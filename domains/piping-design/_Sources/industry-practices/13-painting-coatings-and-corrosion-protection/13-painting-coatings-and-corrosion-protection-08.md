## 1. INTRODUCTION

### 1.1 SCOPE

This new DEP specifies requirements and gives recommendations for corrosion mitigation in wet, sour gas, carbon steel pipelines. It is the repository of current state-of-the-art knowledge of and practices by Shell and the petrochemical industry. Corrosion control methods are continually evolving to remove conservatism and extend the operating boundaries; in these areas, advice should be sought from the Principal's corrosion engineer.

This DEP applies to the use of inhibitors to mitigate general and localised corrosion. It does not apply to the prevention of sulphide stress corrosion cracking (SSCC) and hydrogen induced cracking (HIC); these are generally controlled by materials selection. If the materials in use are not resistant to SSCC and HIC, specialist advice should be sought.

## 2. BACKGROUND

For corrosion to occur three elements have to be present: a steel surface that will corrode, a free water phase, and corrodents. It is possible to control pipeline corrosion by:

- removal of water (dehydration);
- selecting materials that do not corrode (corrosion resistant alloys and non-metallic systems);
- use of carbon steel with a corrosion inhibitor.

Selection of the corrosion control option between removal of water, use of corrosion resistant materials, or use of inhibitors to control corrosion is an economic decision, taking into account safety and local environmental factors. For many situations, Shell has determined that the use of carbon steel pipelines in conjunction with continuous injection inhibition, batching and/or pigging is often the most cost efficient corrosion control strategy for wet, sour gas pipelines. If carbon steel pipelines are used, the process described below to assure asset integrity should be followed:

- understand the corrosion mechanisms and flow regimes to determine the likely locations of corrosion, the severity of corrosion, and the likely mode of failure;
- identify all possible production scenarios including start-up, steady-state production, shut-in, turn-around, spent acid flow back, etc., as corrosion mechanisms can and will change with field operations;
- select the mitigation measures that are compatible with the expected corrosion mechanisms in all production scenarios;
- monitor the efficiency of the mitigation measures with a corresponding sampling and corrosion monitoring program;
- validate the integrity of the mitigation program by inspection.

The steps of the process are described in the following sections.

### 3.2 RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN FLOW REGIMES AND CORROSION MECHANISMS

It is known that identical or similar systems (in terms of chemical compositions) might exhibit entirely different corrosion behaviours, because corrosion behaviours depend not only on the aggressiveness of the fluids, but also on how the water is transported to the internal surface. Transport of the water is the result of the fluid flow inside the pipeline. Flow patterns can create conditions that support corrosion initiation and growth. Flow patterns can also lead to conditions where there is less corrosion due to formation of a protective layer or where an oil film has a significant effect. *Table 3.2* shows the relationship between H₂S corrosion and the main flow regimes, covering the detrimental and the beneficial flow effects for a wet, sour gas pipeline.

NOTE: The importance of the relationship between flow regimes and corrosion mechanisms is reported in separate documents: see (6. [1]) and (6. [2]).

In a wet sour gas system, if the correct control measures are not applied, it is not unusual for a pipeline to suffer severe localised corrosion, up to several mm/yr, when the flow regime switches from annular mist flow (with practically no corrosion) to stratified or slug flow due to production decline.

The flow regime may affect the inhibitor type selected, e.g., for CO₂ corrosion in stratified flow, water-soluble corrosion inhibitors are used to ensure the inhibitor gets into the water phase. For flow regimes where there is a large degree of mixing between water and hydrocarbon phases, it is possible to use a hydrocarbon soluble inhibitor.

| Condition | Dominant Mechanism |

| 20 ≤ CO₂/H₂S ≤ 500 | Mixed CO₂/H₂S corrosion; |

## 3.4 MITIGATION MEASURES

It is important to formulate mitigation measures to suit the expected flow regimes and corrosion mechanisms. For example, if stratified flow exists, continuous-injection inhibition will not mitigate top-of-line corrosion, as the inhibitors will not be transported to the vapour-phase portion of the pipeline. Corrosion might be severe enough to require alternatives such as corrosion allowance, pigging, batching, glycol injection, or even CRA. If the corrosion is dominated by H₂S, top-of-line corrosion is usually so low that no action is needed. However, bottom-of-line corrosion in H₂S might be so severe that continuous-injection inhibitors shall be formulated to disperse well in stagnant water pools to provide protection. ## 3.6 CORROSION MONITORING CONSIDERATIONS FOR WET SOUR GAS

To be effective and efficient in a wet sour gas environment, a corrosion-monitoring device shall be capable of detecting isolated pitting, which is the most dominant form of corrosion. A single point coverage device such as a coupon will often fail to detect pitting. Recommendations for corrosion monitoring are described in (6,[5]).

## 3.8 INSPECTION

To validate the performance of the mitigation program, an inspection plan shall be formulated.

Any changes in the mitigation strategy shall be linked to an increased inspection strategy, to verify that the changed strategy has not resulted in increased corrosion.

## 3.9 CORROSION CONTROL DESIGN

The corrosion mitigation strategy shall be developed with the aid of the data given in (4). This is part of the corrosion control design. For each identified corrosion risk (3.1), the corrosion control design shall include:

- mitigation strategy;
- inspection requirements;
- monitoring and sampling requirements;
- special considerations (timing / remarks / recommendations).

This shall be assessed for each of the major phases of operation:

- initial pipeline start up;
- pipeline start up after well stimulation;
- pipeline start up in all possible operating modes (e.g. line pressurised, line depressurised, methanol injected etc.);
- steady state production;
- shut-ins (of all possible durations and types; see (4.2.3 through 4.2.8);
- abandonment.

## 3.10 MITIGATION STRATEGY REVIEW

The mitigation strategy should be reviewed annually. This should include:

- Check the operating envelope of the corrosion inhibitor (provided by inhibitor supplier) against the operating conditions for each pipeline segment. Flag cases where the inhibitor is being used outside its specified criteria.
- Look for cases where the current mitigation strategy is more severe than in this DEP. Review the history of these lines. Consider a relaxation of the mitigation strategy for these lines.
- Look for cases where the current mitigation is less severe than indicated in this DEP. Review the history of these lines. If it is decided that it is acceptable to do less than is indicated in this DEP, this should be agreed with and signed off by the Principal's corrosion engineer. Otherwise, the mitigation program should be revised to comply with this DEP.
- Review known corrosion problems.

---

- Review overall corrosion control system availability against the required design availability.

This review may result in a revised mitigation strategy. It should also be used as part of the selection process for pipeline inspection.

## 4. CORROSION MITIGATION GUIDELINES

### 4.1 SOUR GAS PIPELINE CLASSIFICATION

In a simplified combination of flow regimes and fluid corrosivity, wet, sour gas pipelines may be classified into 8 types, according to the values of the following parameters:

- flow regimes;
- chloride (Cl⁻) contents of the water;
- presence of elemental sulphur.

The flow regime profile for the pipeline is one of the outputs from HYDROCOR modelling. There may be several different flow regimes over the length of the pipeline; the mitigation strategy shall be based on the most severe regime, from the point of view of selecting line type. For example, if the line contains intermittent slug flow in some sections and annular dispersed flow in other sections, the mitigation strategy for the whole line shall be based on the intermittent slug flow regime.

If the flow regime profile for the pipeline is not known, the superficial gas velocity/critical gas velocity ratio (V_sg/V_crit) can be substituted. V_crit values are field specific and have, for example, been determined empirically for all of Shell Canada's fields (6. [6]). Application of this approach in other fields would require careful review by the Principal's corrosion engineer.

The critical chloride concentration of 10,000 ppm has been determined from field experience and laboratory testing; above a concentration of 10,000 ppm, the chloride ion can destroy the protective FeS scales. The 10,000 ppm limit has been conservatively selected.

The presence of elemental sulphur promotes corrosion in two ways: it destroys the protective FeS scale by offering a fresh steel surface for corrosion, and in addition the elemental sulphur is itself corrosive to steel. At present, any elemental sulphur is considered a problem. This may be a conservative approach when there are only low levels of elemental sulphur, but there is no data to support any alternative approach at this time. The primary risk of elemental sulphur deposition from the production fluids can be addressed by taking a bottom hole sample and analysing the fluids.

Elemental sulphur can also be caused by poor operating practices. Whenever elemental sulphur is detected, the root cause of the sulphur presence should be understood before a mitigation strategy is implemented. For example, if the sulphur deposition is caused by oxygen being injected with methanol injection, the best remedial action may be to address the oxygen being injected rather than changing the mitigation strategy.

Finally, where elemental sulphur is detected, this could also be due to poor sampling and analysis practices.

Tables 4.1(a) and 4.1(b) show the eight classes of wet sour gas pipelines.

NOTE: In classifying sour gas pipelines, the boundary values for the parameters are not absolute differentiators. Pipelines with values near those boundary limits should not be classified unthinkingly, but according to field experience. The Principal's corrosion engineer shall be consulted in such cases.

The rules given in this DEP shall be used as the basis for the mitigation strategy if no other information is known. Any changes from this mitigation strategy should be understood, documented and captured in the management of change process. An example of the type of information required is:

"Pipeline xyz is classified as a type I line. Because of past corrosion resulting in a maximum of ab % wall loss, the mitigation strategy is based on a type IA line."

Where a change is required for a specific line, the mitigation strategy for the line should be based on a different type of line, rather than changing individual elements of the strategy. For example, a line should be treated as type IA instead of type I, rather than treating it as a type I line but with a steady state pigging frequency of 4 weeks. This is to ensure that a

---

changed mitigation strategy is also adopted for the non-steady state conditions as well as the steady state conditions.

Any mitigation programme should be closely linked to changes in production and changes in the operating window for the pipeline, because this could result in a change in line classification.

## 4.2 WET SOUR GAS CORROSION MITIGATION RECOMMENDATIONS

### 4.2.1 Start-up

During initial well start-up, the well effluents might be more corrosive than the ones under steady state conditions. Intermittent production flows can further aggravate corrosion. Continuous injection inhibition alone might not be effective due to the much higher adsorption of inhibitors by competing solid particles.

As a rule of thumb, double batches (2 days to 3 days before and 2 days to 3 days after start-up) are required for most situations (Table 4.2). Increasing the continuous inhibitor injection rates to compensate for inhibitor adsorption by solids is also required for some types of sour gas clusters. Inhibitor adsorption by solids should be tested during inhibitor selection. Batch treatment programmes may have an impact on the downstream plant (large volumes of chemical arriving at the downstream plant); possible negative impacts should be reviewed prior to starting the batch programme.

For pipelines that have been shut in for more than 6 months, especially under a fuel gas blanket, these start-up procedures also apply. This is because one cannot rely on the existing iron sulphide scales and residual corrosion inhibitor films for start-up corrosion protection. During the prolonged shut-in, they might have undergone significant changes.

Other issues to be considered during start up include:

- status of line prior to start up (e.g. containing sour gas, fuel gas or nitrogen, at full pressure or at low pressure, cold or at operating temperature);
- sulphur solvent (if required) on line prior to start up;
- sampling of Total Suspended Solids to check for elemental sulphur;
- continuous inhibition (if required) on line prior to start-up;
- monitoring inhibitor residuals in produced water;
- handling spent acid fluids if the well was acidised before start up, see (4.2.9);
- monitoring pH and chloride content of produced water, see (4.2.9);
- checking impact of low flow and high flow;
- hydrate control;
- methanol injection requirements for start up, see (4.2.10);
- sand detection;
- ensuring no oxygen is left in the system prior to start-up.

## 4.2.9 Spent acid flow back

In the spent acid flow back scenario, the effluents can contain a very high chloride brine (up to 300,000 ppm), low pH fluids and additional unknown fluid chemistry. The continuous and/or batch inhibitors in use might not be effective, due to the change in fluid properties and increasing inhibitor adsorption. This is caused by a potentially large concentration of very fine solids from the formation in the spent acid stream. The existing FeS scales on the pipeline might not be stable under this fluid chemistry.

To combat these problems, spent acid flow back fluids are not allowed to enter the pipeline until both of the following conditions are met:

- pH ≥ 4 and
- chlorides ≤ 50,000 ppm.

Temporary separation facilities shall be used, and pH and chloride measurements are required with separate disposal of liquids until the pH and chlorides targets are met.

Once the pH and chloride targets are met, the liquids can be put back in the pipeline. This should be treated as a "start-up", in line with Table 4.2. Should the flowline be batched when liquids are put back into flowlines. Extra continuous corrosion inhibitor should be added in accordance with Table 4.2, depending on flow regime, for up to 2 weeks. Weekly samples of water should be taken and analysed for chlorides, inhibitor residuals, and pH. The extra mitigation requirements given in Table 4.2 shall be maintained until stable well production is established; once this is confirmed the mitigation plan may revert to a steady state production mitigation strategy.

Any corrosion resistant alloys in the systems should be selected against this high chloride rate (300,000 ppm), because cracking due to high chloride levels can happen very quickly.

For all the 8 sour gas types, an enhanced mitigation program shall consist of the following as a minimum:

- batching of the pipeline 2 days to 3 days prior to spent acid flow back;
- daily sampling of the produced water;
- increasing inhibitor injection rates (see Table 4.2) until water chemistry returns to normal;
- weekly batching if the chloride level stays above 25,000 ppm (if normal Cl⁻ level of the fluids is less than 25,000 ppm). Batching will be discontinued when the Cl⁻ level returns to normal.

Due to the complexity of corrosion by spent acid flow back, all enhanced corrosion control programs require approval from the Principal's corrosion engineer.

## 4.2.10 Methanol injection

Experience and research have shown that excessive methanol injected for hydrate control can cause top-of-line (TOL) as well as bottom-of-line (BOL) corrosion in sour systems. According to field experience, "excessive" methanol injection occurs when the volume of injected methanol is greater than the total volume of water produced in the pipeline (water of condensation and of production). The methanol injection is thought to influence corrosion in the following ways:

- The primary effect is that oxygen injected with the methanol causes corrosion (oxygen is much more soluble in MeOH than in water) – this could lead to BOL or TOL corrosion. The oxygen reacts with H₂S to give deposits of elemental sulphur. This is not a significant change for types IS to IIIS, which already contain elemental sulphur, but would, for example, effectively change a type I gas to a type IS fine.
- The secondary effect is that methanol condensing on TOL with water, dissolving corrosion product films and carrying away corrosion products, could lead to added TOL corrosion.
