# 1. INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 SCOPE

This new DEP specifies requirements and gives recommendations for the application of Human Factors Engineering (HFE) principles in project design. It identifies best practice HFE procedures to enable project teams to identify what level of HFE input is required and where in a project it would be most beneficial.

This DEP adopts many of the principles and recommendations of human-centred design established in ISO/DIS 6385 and ISO 13407.

## 1.2 DISTRIBUTION, INTENDED USE AND REGULATORY CONSIDERATIONS

Unless otherwise authorised by Shell GSI and SIEP, the distribution of this DEP is restricted to companies forming part of the Royal Dutch/Shell Group, and to Contractors nominated by them.

This DEP is intended for use in oil refineries, chemical plants, gas plants and, where applicable, exploration and production facilities and supply/marketing installations. When DEPs are applied, a Management of Change (MOC) process should be implemented. This is of particular importance when existing facilities are to be modified.

If national and/or local regulations exist in which some of the requirements may be more stringent than in this DEP the Contractor shall determine by careful scrutiny which of the requirements are the more stringent and which combination of requirements will be acceptable as regards safety, economic and legal aspects. In all cases the Contractor shall inform the Principal of any deviation from the requirements of this DEP which is considered to be necessary in order to comply with national and/or local regulations. The Principal may then negotiate with the Authorities concerned with the object of obtaining agreement to follow this DEP as closely as possible.

## 1.3 DEFINITIONS

### 1.3.1 General definitions

The **Contractor** is the party that carries out all or part of the design, engineering, procurement, construction, commissioning or management of a project or operation of facility. The Principal may undertake all or part of the duties of the Contractor.

The **Manufacturer/Supplier/Vendor** is the party that manufactures or supplies equipment and services to perform the duties specified by the Contractor.

The **Principal** is the party that initiates, and ultimately pays for, the project. The Principal will generally specify the technical requirements. The Principal may also include an agent or consultant, authorised to act for the Principal.

The **Purchaser** is the party that buys the equipment and services for its own use or as agent for the owner. The Purchaser may be either the Principal or the Contractor.

The word **shall** indicates a requirement.

The word **should** indicates a recommendation.

The word **may** indicates a possible course of action.

## 2. MANAGEMENT SUMMARY

### 2.1 GENERAL

The Royal Dutch/Shell Group's diverse activities require projects to be designed to allow a wide range of end users to reliably use the end product in an effective, efficient, safe, healthy and productive manner. In some cases, the end users may also be members of the public who must interface with certain facilities and products, which requires additional design measures to ensure their health, safety and satisfaction.

Industry today is even more conscious about how people work and recognises that the design of the working environment plays a critical role in the effective functioning of a work system and the productivity a working population can achieve. Any limiting factors that hinder an efficient and effective design, and hence overall system performance, should therefore be properly identified early in the design stages and minimised by a user-centred, participative design approach as outlined in this procedure.

The Royal/Dutch Shell Group's Committee of Managing Directors (CMD) has endorsed a set of Minimum Health Management Standards (MHMS). These are the *minimum* requirements for the implementation of Health standards where Shell has operational control. At the same time, compliance with the national statutory requirements of each country where the Group operates is mandatory for all aspects of health management.

This DEP is one of several HFE-related DEPs which support the MHMS *Human Factors Engineering (HFE) in New Facilities Projects*, and are designed to support a wide range of projects.

***Human Factors Engineering principles shall be considered and applied during early design stage of new facilities projects where design can have a critical impact on equipment usability and user safety or health.***

### 2.2 GOALS AND OBJECTIVES

The primary goal of this DEP is to help Project Teams with the implementation of HFE principles and to assist Shell OUs to become compliant with the MHMS *Human Factors Engineering (HFE) in new facilities projects*.

This DEP will ensure that HFE principles are properly considered and applied in the early design stages of new projects where design can have a critical impact on project success.

This DEP also provides a best practice methodology for OUs to meet the mandatory achievement criteria. The process presented and the accompanying appendices provide further guidance to assist in meeting these mandatory criteria; see (3.4).

### 2.3 TARGET READERSHIP

The target readership for this DEP is primarily the Project Team of new projects. However, Process Engineers, Discipline Engineers, IT designers, Retail station designers and Operations and Maintenance personnel should also refer to this DEP throughout the design of new facilities. This DEP is aimed at the promotion of health, safety, efficiency and usability.

When work systems are changed or new projects are developed there may also be changes in the organisational structure/procedures/practices. As described in (3), HFE considers the organisational elements of projects as well, recognising that the system as a whole must be properly designed. Therefore, Human Resources, Organisational Design and Resource Planners should also make use of this DEP.

# 3. HUMAN FACTORS ENGINEERING (HFE)

## 3.1 BENEFITS

NOTE: The term "ergonomics" is used by many organisations and can be considered synonymous with HFE. Shell has adopted the term "Human Factors Engineering" and this term will be used throughout this DEP.

A driving philosophy behind the application of HFE is that strong operational performance starts with good design and that an understanding of what constitutes good design requires a detailed knowledge of how humans interact within the work system.

As a formal definition, HFE is a multidisciplinary field that considers the integrated knowledge of human capabilities, limitations and needs in the interaction between humans, technology and the working environment. HFE is applied to the design of work systems, workplaces and products, with the following objectives:

1. To increase the operational performance, safety, health and comfort of the work system (through attention to safety, operability, maintainability and constructability in design).
2. To reduce the likelihood of or prevent human errors and/or limit the consequences (through attention to human and systems reliability in design).
3. To enhance the productivity of human efforts (through attention to efficiency in design)
4. To enhance overall system performance by improving the ease and efficiency of use (through attention to usability issues in design).
5. To incorporate user knowledge in the design of the system/product (through attention to user acceptability and marketability).

These objectives will form the philosophy of this procedure and be the key drivers in the process outlined in the following sections.

HFE includes the design of user centred products, workstations and work systems. This science also addresses organisational issues and looks at how the job and tasks are structured and designed within a wider context so that all human efforts are optimised.

Some potential benefits of HFE in projects include:

- CAPEX reduction.
- Engineering hours reduction.
- Operational and maintenance life cycle costs reduction.
- Reduced re-work during construction.
- Overall improvement of HSE performance.
- Improved usability of designed products, information technology and buildings
- Enhanced client commitment ('buy in') often resulting in a reduction of approval cycles in the design phase.
- Improvement of knowledge of operations and maintenance tasks during design and engineering leading to improved life cycle cost awareness and cost reduction measures in design.

## 3.2 HFE IN PROJECTS

Human Factor Engineering shall be applied in the following projects:

- New plants/facilities (onshore and offshore),
- Revamp of existing plant/facility (unless there is no change to the work system, e.g. like-for-like change out, and no benefits from design improvements can be justified).
- Buildings and workstations design (e.g. retail stations, central control rooms, offices, workshops, laboratories);

- IT software/hardware, instrumentation.

An important principle in the approach to meeting the HFE MHMS is that the process of identifying, understanding and specifying the detailed HFE design requirements should be started at an appropriately early stage in the design process, so that these 'requirements' (technical design specifications and/or HFE scopes of work to be executed to derive/check design specifications) are incorporated in the Basic Design and Engineering Package (BDEP) / Project Specification (PS) documents. While further refinement activities may be required at later stages (e.g., in detailed design), this avoids the need to change design at a late stage, when to do so may not be cost effective.

Application of the principle is based on the premise that strong operational performance begins with good design. The operational performance requirements/expectations need to be actively considered in order to specify design requirements that will serve as a platform for desired performance levels.

## 3.3 HFE IN DESIGN

At a high level, the key design principles that should be considered include the need for a user-centred, integrated, proactive and participative approach to design. HFE principles have been developed for a range of work contexts and are all aimed at guiding the designer in the achievement of fit for purpose work systems.

The user-centred design approach should be initiated at the development phase. While HFE efforts are initiated at the development stage, the overall HFE input may well span the entire development and implementation phases.

Design activities shall be addressed from an HFE standpoint and shall not only cover hardware and software, but also consider other critical issues and design interfaces that arise from a project such as organisational design, work cycles, locations, job/task design, and operational and manning philosophy.

The following aspects shall be addressed:

1. Critical impact - This is where poor design can have a negative impact on task and system performance.

2. Equipment usability - This describes the ease with which people interface with machines or technology.

3. User safety and health - Human performance during work is optimised by a user centred design approach, leading to a safe, comfortable and healthy working environment that minimises the likelihood of human error and work related injuries or ill health. Safety refers to occupational health and safety and also major hazard safety.

The HFE design analysis process is detailed in Appendix 4.

## 3.4 ACHIEVEMENT CRITERIA

The Minimum Health Management Standard is achieved when:

1. All new facilities projects are reviewed for minimum HFE requirements.

2. Where the screening indicates that HFE input is necessary, the location or business applies the correct processes, tools and competencies at the correct stages of the project.

3. The HFE recommendations have been discussed with management and action parties and target dates agreed.

## 4.2.2 Project Development (Figure 2)

### 4.2.2.1 Basic Design

1. *Assemble Project Ergonomics Team (PET)*

The PET meets at the beginning of the Basis of Design Phase, and shall include the:
- Project Manager, Project Engineer;
- Process Engineer;
- Operations/Maintenance representative.

2. *Apply HFE in Project*

The Project Manager shall address this question in order to assure that the project complies with MHMS (3.3).

3. *Identify Design Hotspots and Performance issues*

This is achieved by means of the HFE Focusing Tool (Appendix 1), and the Work Systems Analysis Tool (Appendix 2). These tools are used to help identify relevant Performance Factors in the project that may require more detailed thought and HFE attention.

The PET shall consider the 10 Performance Factors in the HFE Focusing Tool through discussion of past experiences from similar projects and knowledge of the current project. Such discussions shall be open with participation from all relevant team members. Where specific past experience is not known or clearly understood, it should be captured and evaluated for the purposes of the current project, e.g. by identifying end users with the required experience and competence, and by analysing the data and root causes of any accidents and incidents (e.g. from Tripod Beta databases, HFE relevant general failure types, and the associated organisation and procedures).

Once it has been established which specific design aspects would benefit from attention, those hotspots and associated performance issues shall be recorded within the project Work Systems Analysis Tool.

The Work Systems Analysis Tool is used by the PET to identify the work systems of the project that will benefit from HFE efforts with respect to the performance factors identified by means of the HFE Focusing Tool.

4. *Identify HFE Scope of Work*

Figure 4 (Appendix 2) assists in determining the appropriate level of HFE activity in order to achieve the desired design and performance outcome. Table 5 (Appendix 2) outlines the criteria for the three levels of HFE effort that may be applied.

Any Level 1 HFE input required (application of HFE specs alone) must adhere to the criteria laid out in Table 5. Activities for this level will not require the involvement of additional HFE competence (other than the awareness training that the team has already undertaken).

Identified Level 2 activities are carried out by a competent individual (6) and are incorporated as part of the regular review procedures of the project (e.g. at 30 %, 60 % and 90 % completion). This individual shall perform required reviews from the necessary HFE perspective.

For Level 3 activities, it will have been decided by the PET that either additional scrutiny of a particular project aspect is required (using a more formal front end HFE requirements identification tool) in order to identify key issues and requirements, or/and particular actions have been identified that will require specific targeted scopes of work to achieve the end result. For either of these, Human Factors Engineer (i.e. a skilled or mastery level HFE professional (6)) will be required to assist.

If any Level 3 HFE activities are identified as being required for the project, then the Project Team must utilise the support of a Human Factors Engineer. Level 3 inputs therefore require the addition of a Human Factors Engineer to the PET.

With Level 3 HFE activities identified, depending on the project scope, the PET should include, as appropriate, the following disciplines, e.g.:

- Operations;
- Process Engineering;
- Maintenance and/or shutdown;
- Piping and layout specialists;
- Discipline engineers, e.g., mechanical, instrumentation, running/static equipment;
- Human Factors Engineer;
- IT Developers;
- Retail Station Designers.

If Level 3 HFE activities are required for the project, the Human Factors Engineer should lead or facilitate them.

### 5. *Develop HFE Plan*

After the required levels of HFE input (or actions for further scrutiny) have been decided, conclusions shall documented and included in the overall project report for audit purposes.

By using the two Tools described here, the PET starts the process of considering how design will interact with specified future end-users, and what steps must be taken to ensure and confirm that task demands will be fully supported by design features. Design should obviously reflect operational conditions and the requirements of all end users, taking account of their mental and physical capacities and limitations. This process is likely to continue into subsequent stages of the design.

### 6. *Incorporate HFE Plan into PEP*

All actions required in the development phase shall be tracked throughout subsequent design phases.

## 4.2.2.2 Basic Design and Engineering /Project Specification

### 7. *Conduct HFE Analysis (when specified)*

At this stage either formal HFE scope of work will be ready to execute, and/or a front end HFE review session will have been requested. Appendix 4 provides an HFE design analysis tool specifically designed to identify end user requirements for a new project.

The PET supports the Human Factors Engineer in all HFE analysis activities throughout this stage of the project. Such support could come by way of regular design meetings, information gathering or the provision and development of human centred policies (e.g. manning philosophy).

The Human Factors Engineer is responsible for the required Level 3 analyses identified at the Work System Analysis Tool stage. The type of project will dictate how the analysis is carried out and which tools are appropriate.

The PT shall engage a Human Factors Engineer to carry out all level 3 HFE activities; see (Appendix 3) for a competence screening tool.

The Human Factors Engineer shall produce an HFE design report as part of the normal Level 3 analysis activities. The Project Manager shall consider each recommendation provided by the Human Factors Engineer and decide to accept or reject the recommendation.

Most PTs will have a process for accepting design recommendations and should include HFE design recommendations in that process.

### 8. *HFE Specifications and scopes of work accepted by Project Manager*

In case of dilemmas the Investment Justification Tool in DEP 30.00.60.12-Gen. should be applied to test the economics of HFE recommendations.

Both the Project Manager and the Human Factors Engineer should use this DEP.

9. *Include HFE requirements in the Project Specifications Document*

The approved recommendations shall form the basis for the Human Factors Engineering specifications in the Project Specifications Document.

## 4.2.2 PROJECT IMPLEMENTATION PHASE (Figure 3)

### 4.2.3.1 Detailed Design and Engineering

10. *Determine HFE Implementation Plan*

A project specific HFE implementation plan is produced, specifying all activities required (drawing on the results from the Work Systems Analysis tool and any subsequent analyses undertaken by the Human Factors Engineer etc.). The plan details what actions shall be executed in the subsequent project phases, and explains how these are to be executed. Again, the 3 levels of HFE activity should be represented in the plan, as appropriate, and as indicated from the Work Systems Analysis Tool. The plan should also indicate what additional activities are required in the Project Specification phase to confirm/further specify requirements, as a result of any additional information. An example of such a plan is given in (Appendix 5).

11. *Project Manager Approval of Plan*

Should the HFE Implementation Plan not be approved, a feedback loop is required to adjust the plan. The Human Factors Engineer should address points of contention and misinterpretation, possibly in the HFE Implementation Plan.

12. *Include HFE Implementation Plan into the Project Implementation Plan*

When the HFE Implementation Plan has been finalised it should be included in the final Project Implementation Plan (PIP) by the Project Manager (or his delegate).

### 4.2.3.2 Construction Phase

13. *Execute HFE Activities*

This step is to be carried out for all Level 2 and 3 HFE activities that were identified in Step 7 (4.2.2.2). HFE reviews and other required activities should be carried out during the same period when overall project design reviews are carried out, and should be executed as part of the overall design activities to ensure proper co-ordination and communication. Reviews shall be carried out by the Human Factors Engineer or alternatively can be carried out by the HFE focal point provided the individual has a working knowledge level; see (6)

The process for the reviews is:

- Identification of the objective of the review.
- Identify the appropriate design specifications for the work system under review.
- Carry out the review/verification process (i.e. does the design meet the required standards).

14. *Determine Construction Plan*

The Human Factors Engineer and representatives from the construction management team shall develop the construction plan inclusive of the HFE requirements outlined in the PS document.

15. *Construction Plan Approval*

Should the Construction Plan not be approved, a feedback loop is required to adjust the plan. Points of contention and misinterpretation should be addressed by the HFE Engineer in order for the project to move forward into the construction phase.

## 16. Implement Construction Plan

The purpose of the HFE construction plan is to guide the construction contractor with respect to installing equipment not usually shown in the 3D CAD model. This concerns mainly 'field run' installed equipment (e.g. small bore piping, lighting fixtures, secondary cable trays etc.). This will ensure that the HFE specifications are assured throughout the construction phase and are not compromised inadvertently.

It may be necessary to insert relevant requirements into the installation contractors' work scope, as well as monitoring and verifying that needs have been met. This process can be met through:

- Awareness sessions with on-site contractors.
- Execution of HFE verification rounds.
- A procedure which advises the contractor how to deal with a situation, diagnosed as ergonomically undesirable.

### 4.2.3.3 Post Implementation

#### 17. Evaluate the success of the Application of HFE in the Project

The Human Factors Engineer may, together with the Project Manager and/or client/owner, decide to evaluate the HFE undertaken on the project. This evaluation may include:

- Ergonomics evaluations during operations (to verify that ergonomics hazards have been properly controlled in design and to identify other possible issues that were not predicted adequately or dealt with).
- Organisational design issues (to verify that organisational demands were adequately predicted and dealt with during the design phase).
- Costs and benefits of HFE involvement in the project, etc.

This feedback process, together with other sources of data (e.g. from Tripod Beta analyses considering HFE related general failure types (GFTs) such as design, organisation and procedures), can generate an important source of data on opportunities for improving in designs in future projects. It can also be used to assess and/or improve the HFE methods and tools employed to ensure that required performance standards are achieved. Such data should be actively captured and made available as input to new project design specifications.

# APPENDIX 1 HFE FOCUSING TOOL

The Minimum Health Management Standards (MHMS) require Human Factors Engineering principles to be considered and applied during the early design stage of projects where design can have a critical impact on equipment usability and user safety or health. This process requires the identification of what could be done, and where and when to do it, in design. The focusing tool described below assists in the first step - identifying what could/should be done. The Work Systems Analysis Tool (Appendix 2) helps in the process of identifying where in the project HFE should be applied and when. Both tools combine to help Project Teams identify where project 'hotspots' and performance issues lie.

This HFE Focusing Tool should be jointly completed by the following individuals:

- Project Manager (PM).
- Process Engineer (PE).
- Operations/Maintenance representatives, owner of system.

Focusing questions - what are they used for?

The following questions should be asked by the team in a systematic fashion. The aim is to consider each performance factor in relation to the project being assessed (using the Work Systems Analysis Tool). Each performance factor challenges the team to identify risks and opportunities in the design that can be reduced/realised through targeted application of HFE efforts. The team is challenged to firstly recognise *if and where in the project design* there may be an opportunity, and then to be able to specify *what* should be done to realise the opportunity. Specifying *what HFE scope* needs to be planned into the design process to achieve the desired performance outcome is to be recorded on the Work Systems Analysis Tool. For example - specifying a particular scope of work at a particular time, specifying (existing) HFE standards/specs to be used in the design of particular items, or recognising what design processes may highlight additional opportunities to be exploited and to which HFE scope should be targeted at a later date - e.g. RAM studies, identification of safety critical items, & specific studies for which there should be an HFE component - e.g. escape evacuation and rescue, control room design and layout, etc.

The following questions relate to an aspect of performance to be considered, and should be applied in a creative fashion by the team.

1. **Efficiency in design**

   Is there an opportunity to achieve higher levels of operational efficiency on specified end user tasks through improvements in design? Examples: a more logical and shorter operator's round; easier and fewer manual valve operations; easier and more efficient pigging operations; easier change-out of removable items such as filters and consumables; pump start-up by one instead of two operators, etc.

2. **Maintainability**

   Is there a need to ensure a high level of maintainability for critical items through more focused attention to operator task demands? Examples: to assure achievement of availability targets; in exploiting shorter shutdown or change-out periods; to reduce the likelihood of maintenance errors on safety critical equipment; to reduce waiting time; to reduce adverse working postures; to provide hoisting equipment which is easier to use; saving on scaffolding cost, etc.

3. **Safety and Reliability**

   Is there an opportunity to improve safety and reliability through specific attention to human operations? Examples: reducing the likelihood and impact of identified critical human errors (e.g. reduced switching errors through a well designed overview; quicker and more effective urgent/emergency intervention; preventing unintended control actions, etc.).

4. **Health**

Would the application of targeted design features assist in achieving worker health standards? Examples: reduced exposure to hazardous materials; less force required in manual handling; better working/living environment; fewer adverse working positions such as working when bending, crouching, on one leg, with the body twisted, less pulling, pushing, lifting, reduced bolting effort, etc.

## 5. Usability and IT

Do specific usability issues need to be addressed to assure IT product effectiveness? For example, software usability, decision support tools for trip management, etc.

## 6. End users' needs

Does the success of this project depend on systematically assessing end users' needs in relation to specific task demands? Examples: through timely and systematic capture and incorporation of end users' knowledge of work systems in early design (for example by scheduling a front-end ergonomics evaluation session).

## 7. External customers

Will external customers and third parties interface with the designed facility/product? This may require a broader range of end users' needs to be accommodated in the design - e.g. design of office building entrances, the design of a motor oil bottle; design of filling stations for use by customers or third parties, etc.

## 8. Unfamiliar project or technology

Is this the first project of its kind or the first use of a new design/technology? If so, this may require trial use by end users of new work systems (hardware/software) to improve usability; task analysis of new tasks in order to understand hazards and opportunities and assist design.

## 9. HFE competence of design contractors

Would the project benefit if the design contractors to be used had HFE competence to deal with/identify HFE needs and opportunities? Without that competence and an agreed approach, HFE opportunities and improvements may not be exploited.

## 10. HFE audit trail

Is an audit trail required to demonstrate that HFE has been incorporated into the design of the project? Examples: as required by regulations (e.g. for a safety case where authorities require an explicit treatment of Human factors in design); or for insurance purposes (e.g. reduced premiums for reduced risks).

# APPENDIX 4 HFE DESIGN ANALYSIS

## 1. GENERAL

Best practice in HFE is to ensure that human tasks and the role of the operator/user of a work system (plant, controls room, workshop, laboratory, office etc.) are systematically assessed at an appropriate stage of design. The aim is to ensure that potential bottlenecks in the design of the human-machine interactions are identified at an early stage, to determine the subsequent basic design requirements and features, and to ensure that these are embodied during detailed engineering, procurement and construction phases. This strategy will enhance a more functional design resulting in a reduction of CAPEX and operational life cycle costs.

The analysis will require a competent HFE engineer, with familiarity of the business context, and experienced in using the relevant tools. The analysis should be conducted at an early stage and involve a complete representation of end users and key stakeholders, including for example, the Project Manager, process engineer, operations and maintenance representative, contractor representatives etc. Having all key players available at the right time and focused on the Human Factors Engineer issues is the strength of the approach, in a ‘right first time philosophy’.

The analysis is aimed at creating the environment within which a detailed ‘picture’ of task requirements can be created and visualized by the team, with the aim of identifying performance requirements and design issues for attention. The HFE facilitator will lead the session and prompt the team on those issues that will allow a focused view of design hotspots to emerge.

Typically draft or finalized Process Engineering Flow Schemes (PEFS) are used as a basis for the session (covering either the entire project or a particular part of it), and a walkthrough of the process is undertaken, so that the team can build an understanding of system and hence of the task requirements and constraints, in physical and cognitive terms.

Tasks and task demands are given particular attention and design measures to support task demands are discussed. Technical specifications are generated, and areas are identified that require a more focused evaluation. In this case, an HFE scope of work requirement is generated and recorded. At the end of the analysis a report is compiled and a plan of action presented, to define and co-ordinate future design activities regarding HFE.

## 2. QUALITY ASSURANCE

It is essential to assure human factors engineering input in a project by means of a Quality Assurance System. Use of an HFEengineer DEP or other HFE standard document in isolation may lead to unsuccessful implementation of human factors engineering in projects.

## 3. IMPLEMENTATION, INCLUDING ROLE OF HUMAN FACTOR ENGINEER

HFE design analysis shall already be implemented during the development phase of a project to enable a structured identification of the operation, maintenance and other task demands, and associated design requirements that will deliver the required levels of system performance.

The HFE design analysis is normally executed by a multi-disciplinary team. Process engineering operation/maintenance representatives, project management and human factors engineering can participate in the Project Ergonomics Team (PET). Depending on the type of project and the anticipated potential bottlenecks that may be identified, specialised competences may need to be involved.

The Human Factors Engineer will assess possible training needs in the field of HFE and will brief the team members concerned before the analysis commences on the "state of the art" of implementing HFE in projects.

The Human Factors Engineer shall chair the HFE design analysis, which gives him a facilitating role with responsibility for setting up and distributing the final HFE Report to the (line) managers concerned.

## 6. VALVE ANALYSIS

Special attention during the design of a plant is given to valve configurations in terms of accessibility and ease of operation. A special method has been developed for this aimed at eliminating poor valve design in category 1 valves and optimising design in category 2 and 3 valves. Category 1 valves are important for the efficient, reliable and safe working of a facility. Detailed information can be found in DEP 30.00.60.13-Gen.

After a brief introduction, operations personnel complete the categorisation of valves. If necessary, the results are coded in the appropriate drawings and attached as an appendix to the HFE Report.

# APPENDIX 5 HFE IMPLEMENTATION PLAN

## 1. SUMMARY and KEYWORDS

This Appendix describes a method for formulating an HFE Implementation Plan (HIP). It provides a standard layout for the HIP with a descriptive checklist of what must be included, so that an HIP is produced that contains sufficient detail for the purposes of the Project Implementation Plan (PIP).

**Keywords**

Plant layout, Man-machine interface design, ergonomics, user participation, operability, and maintainability.

## 2. AIM, SCOPE AND TARGET GROUP

The aim of this Appendix is to assist in developing a recognisable and consistent HFE Implementation Plan in order to ensure that the underlying concepts and design factors concerning 'critical' man-machine interfaces are satisfied during the detailed engineering, procurement and construction phases of a new construction or plant change project.

This Appendix is intended for all parties involved in the implementation phase of a project, e.g. project engineers, operations representatives, Human Factors Engineers, process engineers and Project Managers.

## 3. CONTEXT AND REFERENCES

The process described in this Appendix is applicable to projects worth more than US $ 10 million. With projects worth less than US $ 10 million, it is sufficient to incorporate the HFE report and/or parts of the HIP in the PIP.

## 4. ACTIVITIES DESCRIPTION

This Appendix does not include a flow scheme since normally a planning schedule is used to formulate an HFE Implementation Plan (HIP). Responsibilities for the HIP are laid down in the PIP procedure forming part of the Project Implementation process.

The author of the HIP shall have the HIP checked by the Human Factors Engineer and by one or more disciplines/departments, if necessary. The project leader then incorporates the HIP in the PIP.

## 5. CONTENT OF HFE IMPLEMENTATION PLAN

A guide for formulating an HFE Implementation Plan is given below, with details as to how such a Plan must be structured and what should be included in the various sections and sub-sections. The guideline is laid out as a table of contents, with a description of expected subject matter.

### 5.1 INTRODUCTION

#### 5.1.1 Background and scope

Describe briefly what the project entails, what its purpose is (e.g. boosting production, improving HSE, etc.) and what has prompted the initiation of the project (e.g. economic growth of a product, accident, operational or maintenance problems, etc.).

### 5.1.2 Potential bottlenecks/risks, man-machine interface

Summarise the potential bottlenecks affecting the man-machine interface, referring to the PS/BDEP document incorporating the HFE specification report (e.g. HFE report) with the findings of the HFE design analysis.

## 5.2 READER'S GUIDE

Describe the layout in the form of a "reader's guide", i.e. content and sequence of the individual sections.

## 5.3 POLICY AND OBJECTIVES

Briefly describe policy on ergonomics. For example:

- Refer to this DEP and other relevant DEPs.
- Briefly describe the objectives. For example:
- Implementation of up-front (formulated during BDEP/PS phase) ergonomic requirements and/or requirements mutually agreed between Principal and Engineering/Construction Contractor during project implementation phase.
- Integration of ergonomics in normal working processes (to be raised in engineering co-ordination meetings, bid reviews, seller document checks, etc.).
- Ensuring a multi-disciplinary approach.
- Ensuring participation of end users during all project phases.
- Designing and constructing a user-friendly plant against minimum costs.
- Ensuring that systematic reviews are conducted in order to minimise conflicts with policy.

## 5.4 DEFINITION, LEGISLATION AND GUIDELINES AND DILEMMA HANDLING

Describe whether specific legislation is applicable. For example, ergonomics legislation is stricter in Europe than outside Europe. DEP 30.00.60.11-Gen. gives an overview of ISO standards and guidelines on ergonomic matters.

Indicate how ergonomic and (for example) technical constraints are to be balanced with one another; for this purpose, use can be made of the DEP 30.60.00.12, HFE investment justification model.

## 5.5 ORGANIZATION AND RESPONSIBILITIES

### 5.5.1 Project Ergonomics Team (PET)

Appoint the members of the Project Ergonomics Team (PET) and specify their terms of reference. The PET members are preferably the same as during the feasibility phase. The PET comprises representatives of the following disciplines as a minimum:

- Project Management, Project engineering,
- Process engineering,
- Maintenance and/or shutdown,
- Operations,
- HFE,
- (Engineering Contractor's lead HFE engineer if applicable).

Consider involving Mechanical and E/I instrument engineers, for example, depending on project scope.

Indicate who is responsible for the input of operational and maintenance aspects from Operations and include the relevant job descriptions in an Appendix.

If applicable, assign the Engineering Contractor's lead HFE engineer to the PET.

Indicate the frequency with which the PET is to meet.

### 5.5.2 Supporting PET per project phase

Indicate which disciplines will be available to support the PET on an ad-hoc basis, depending on project phase; e.g. representatives from Procurement, Subcontracting, Construction, HSE **and** Quality engineer, Value cost engineer, etc.

### 5.5.3 Organigram

Lay down the organisational and reporting lines in an organigram and include this as an Appendix in the Implementation Plan.

## 5.6 TRAINING AND AWARENESS

### 5.6.1 Training discipline engineers

Indicate which disciplines and how many personnel require training. Refer to the HFE and Engineering Training Programme. Consider: lead discipline engineers, discipline engineers procurement, and construction.

Consider training for Vendors, e.g., skid packaged unit suppliers.

### 5.6.2 Training Construction Contractors

Indicate that Contractor personnel will be given training/awareness sessions during the construction phase (refer to Construction Phase sub-section).

### 5.6.3 HFE bulletin

Consider publishing an HFE progress bulletin for distribution to the project team and other parties involved, in order to foster general awareness. This can be achieved by any knowledge/information propagation system that is currently in place within the organisation.

## 5.7 STANDARDS AND GUIDELINES

Indicate which standards and guidelines are applicable. Include this list as an Appendix.

## 5.8 HFE ASSURANCE DURING BASIC AND DETAILED ENGINEERING

### 5.8.1 Organisation: HFE assurance by engineering contractor

Ergonomics assurance shall be accomplished in accordance with the Engineering Contractor's quality system, of which ergonomics assurance shall form an integral part. For example, most of the 'regular' Engineering Contractors for Shell projects have an ergonomics procedure in their respective quality systems, and also make provision for an HFE lead engineer. This function is responsible for HFE practice within the Engineering Contractor's organisation and has a job description as such.

### 5.8.2 Mock-up studies/simulation

If applicable, the execution of these review methodologies is recommended in the HFE project specification report (e.g. HFE report), e.g. the construction of a simple mock-up in order to carry out simulations, etc.

### 5.8.3 Model reviews

During the first model review (block model and/or 30 %), it is important to verify the ergonomic principles of the project against the HFE specifications (e.g. HFE report).

Ensure that HFE forms an integral part of the subsequent (3D CAD) model reviews. Ensure that the reviews are witnessed by the Engineering Contractors HFE lead engineer and PET or its representatives.

### 5.8.4 Use of 3D CAD system

A major tool during the design work, reviews and audits is the 3D CAD system. In this sub-section, describe what the system should show and how much space has to be reserved for what activities, as a 'soft box'. The use of 'soft boxes' is recommended in accordance with the findings of the HFE analysis (e.g. HFE design analysis), for example:

- Coded (e.g. colour-coded) category 1 valves.
- Space for escape routes, space for operator's round, space for 'critical' operational or maintenance work, e.g. flare work.
- Space for routine maintenance transport routes.
- Space for shutdown activities, shutdown maintenance areas, etc.
- Free hoisting space, free filter withdrawal space, etc.
- Free space for E/I equipment, etc.
- Space for fireproofing, insulation, etc.

NOTE: List what will **NOT** be shown in the 3D CAD. Generally, this will be the field run items.

## 5.9 HFE ASSURANCE DURING PROCUREMENT

### 5.9.1 General

Indicate how ergonomics assurance is to be accomplished in procurement. State priorities, e.g. rapid action required for long lead items.

### 5.9.2 HFE in equipment requisitions

Indicate whether special attention is required for certain equipment; e.g. incorporate ergonomic specifications in requisitions for dust filters, skid packaged units, etc.

Stipulate whether equipment is to be inspected during fabrication on the Vendor's site.

## 5.10 HFE ASSURANCE DURING CONSTRUCTION

Indicate that an ergonomic construction is to be achieved, referring to the HFE Plan for Construction Phase, see DEP 30.00.60.14-Gen.

This plan includes:

- Incorporation of ergonomics in contracts with Installation Contractors; this plan (or parts thereof) is to be referred to in the enquiry package for subcontractor.
- Training/briefing session for Contractors on-site.
- Conducting of regular ergonomic rounds.
- Check list/action list; a checklist for the above-mentioned inspection rounds can be based on the design requirements and desirables in the HFE specification report (e.g. HFE report).

## 5.11 HFE PROJECT DIRECTIVES

These directives will be written and issued by the Engineering Contractor's project management team if new HFE guidelines are to be implemented and/or corrective action is required in the course of the project.

# 5.12 EXPERT SUPPORT

## 5.12.1 Competence of contracted Human Factors Engineer

If use is to be made of the services of contracted ergonomists, they must be listed for example in the Register of European Ergonomists or the local equivalent. Also find information on competence in (Appendix 3), Competence requirements for Human Factors Engineers.

## 5.12.2 Expert support per project phase

Indicate for which activities the support of an HFE expert is required during the basic and detailed engineering, procurement and construction.

Expert support is important in:

- Co-ordination and execution of training.
- Execution of HFE bottlenecks analysis of a reference plant (benchmarking).
- Execution/follow-up of HFE analysis (e.g. HFE design analysis).
- Auditing of Engineering Contractors.
- Auditing of Vendors.
- Formulating and advising on Engineering Contractor's Implementation Plan.
- Mock-up studies.
- Witnessing 3D CAD block model, 30 %, 60% and 90 % reviews.
- Formulating and advising on HFE Plan for Construction.
- Audits during Construction phase.
- Ergonomic evaluation after start-up.

## 2.4 HFE Focusing Tool

The project team then went on to fill in the Work Systems Analysis Tool as follows on the next page:

### 2.4.1 Efficiency in design

Is there an opportunity to achieve higher levels of operational efficiency on specified end user tasks through improvements in design? Examples: a more logical and shorter operator's round; easier and fewer manual valve operations; easier and more efficient pigging operations; easier changeout of removable items such as filters and consumables; pump start-up by one instead of two operators, etc.

*The design team recognised the importance of operability in the control room and feel they will be able to identify exactly where HFE efforts can be directed in future stages.*

### 2.4.2 Maintainability

Is there a need to ensure a high level of maintainability for critical items through more focused attention to operator task demands? e.g. to assure achievement of availability targets; in exploiting shorter shutdown or changeout periods; to reduce the likelihood of maintenance errors on safety critical equipment; to reduce waiting time; to reduce adverse working postures; to provide hoisting equipment which is easier to use; saving on scaffolding cost, etc.

*It was found that in past projects where maintenance tasks were not built into design, it was laborious to maintain some of the equipment in the control room and this also further distracted the control room operators.*

### 2.4.3 Safety and Reliability

Is there an opportunity to improve safety and reliability through specific attention to human operations? Examples: reducing the likelihood and impact of identified critical human errors (e.g. reduced switching errors through a well designed overview; quicker and more effective urgent/emergency intervention; preventing unintended control actions, etc.)

*The project team recognises that as this is the central control room for numerous offshore wells, the accuracy of the operator is a key project success driver. In this case the project team decided that it must focus more attention on human reliability.*

### 2.4.4 Health

Would the application of targeted design features assist in achieving worker health standards? Examples: reduced exposure to hazardous materials; less force required in manual handling; better working/living environment; fewer adverse working positions such as working when bending, crouching, on one leg, with the body twisted, less pulling, pushing, lifting; reduced bolting effort, etc.

*Past projects and operator input confirm that the working environment was not up to an appropriate standard. Therefore the project team decided to focus more attention on the user health and safety (and therefore increasing comfort)*

## 2.4.5 Usability and IT

Do specific usability issues need to be addressed to assure IT product effectiveness? For example, software usability, decision support tools for trip management, etc.

*Any new DCS will require IT usability. Due to the change that has been requested by operators, the project team will concentrate on this part of the project.*

## 2.4.6 End users' needs

Does the success of this project depend on systematically assessing end users' needs in relation to specific task demands? Examples: through timely and systematic capture and incorporation of end users' knowledge of work systems in early design (for example by scheduling a front-end ergonomics evaluation session).

*It was apparent to the project team that many of the bottlenecks presented above could have been easily avoided had operators been brought into the project at appropriate intervals.*

## 2.4.7 External customers

Will external customers and third parties interface with the designed facility/product? This may require a broader range of end users' needs to be accommodated in the design - e.g. design of office building entrances, the design of a motor oil bottle; design of filling stations for use by customers or third parties, etc.

*The control room will only be used by in-house staff and will not be a demonstration lab for third parties.*

## 2.4.8 Unfamiliar project or technology

Is this the first project of its kind or the first use of a new design/technology? If so, this may require trial use by end users of new work systems (hardware/software) to improve usability; task analysis of new tasks in order to understand hazards and opportunities and assist design.

*The team has confidence that they will be able to take on lessons that have been learnt from previous projects within the same OU.*

## 2.4.9 HFE competence of design contractors

Would the project benefit if the design contractors to be used had HFE competence to deal with/identify HFE needs and opportunities? Without that competence and an agreed approach, HFE opportunities and improvements may not be exploited.

*The engineering contractor was able to provide proof as part of their bid that Human Factors Engineering was incorporated in how they worked.*

## 2.4.11 HFE audit trail

Is an audit trail required to demonstrate that HFE has been incorporated into the design of the project? Examples: as required by regulations (e.g. for a safety case where authorities require an explicit treatment of Human factors in design); or for insurance purposes (e.g. reduced premiums for reduced risks).

*Local legislation dictates that Human Factors Engineering inclusion be an auditable process. Also, by complying with this legislation, some of the achievement criteria are met.*
