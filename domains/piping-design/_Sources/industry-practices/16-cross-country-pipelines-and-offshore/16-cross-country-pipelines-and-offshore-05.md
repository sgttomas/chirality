## 710 General Considerations

This section is primarily devoted to the internal pressure design of piping systems and related equipment, along with other important considerations.

### Fluid Flow

Sizing of pipe is thoroughly covered in the *Fluid Flow Manual*.

The PCFLOW computer program (software is included in the *Fluid Flow Manual*) can quickly solve fluid flow problems. This program is designed for IBM-PC (or compatible) computers.

The PIPEFLOW-2 computer program should be considered for multiphase flow in a single pipeline and for piping networks.

### Piping Layout

Layout seldom gets the attention it deserves. A good layout meets the requirements given in Section 320. Additional requirements of producing facilities are as follows:

- Connections for planned future additions should be installed during the initial construction phase.
- Lines in the plant should have labels showing their content, and should have arrows showing the direction of flow (see the *Safety in Designs Manual*).
- Easily removable pipe should be used around wells to allow workover room.

Some guidance in piping layouts can be found in the *Safety in Designs Manual*. See Sections 731 and 732 for more detailed layout guidelines.

### Piping Flexibility

Piping flexibility should be carefully checked at connections to machinery. Forces from thermal expansion and contraction must not be allowed to overstress the machinery connections or cause misalignment. See Section 330.

### Insulation

Insulation with weatherjacketing should be provided on hot (above 140°F) and cold piping for personnel protection and thermal stability. Insulation is also required to conserve energy in cold climates. Piping layouts should take insulation requirements into account. See the *Insulation and Refractory Manual*.

### Piping Supports

Supports should be spaced at short enough intervals to prevent sags (where liquid can trap) and excessive material stress; see Appendix D, Standard Drawing GB-M99653, Standard Pipe Shoes and Pipe Stop Shoes, the PC program CAESAR II, and Section 200 of the *Civil and Structural Manual*.

#### Relief Valves

Thermal relief valves must be installed where necessary to prevent overpressuring caused by liquid expansion between block valves and/or blinds; see the *Instrumentation and Control Manual* for guidelines. Any liquids that pose health or environmental hazards must be discharged to appropriate and safe locations and/or contained in some manner.

#### Liquid Removal

This is important in wet gas and instrument air lines to reduce pressure drop and erosion. Condensate can also collect in instruments and compressor valves and damage them. Remove liquid with manual knockout pots, dump traps, or poly-pigs.

#### Piping Vibration and Fatigue Failures

Vibration of reciprocating machinery is the primary cause of piping fatigue failures. Other sources of vibration are control valves with high pressure drops, out-of-balance rotating equipment, pump cavitation, and liquid slugs. See Sections 330 and 340.

#### Materials

**Carbon steel** pipe, valves, and fittings should be used for most hydrocarbon services. Do not use **cast iron** valves or fittings, except for water that is not used to fight a fire.

**Screwed galvanized** pipe and fittings are recommended for instrument air and fresh water service. **Copper** pipe is often used for plumbing systems within buildings. Do not use copper or **brass** if hydrogen sulfide (H₂S), ammonia (NH₃), or salts are present in the atmosphere or in the stock contained in the pipe.

**Stainless steel** tubing and fittings are required for instrument piping. Stainless steel tubing is more expensive than copper tubing. However, it has greater mechanical strength, is less susceptible to cracking in vibrating service, and is more fire resistant. When ordering control valve assemblies, it is important to specify stainless steel tubing; control valve assemblies generally come with copper tubing. Stainless steel piping may be required for corrosive services.

**Plastic** pipe (polypropylene, polyethylene, polyvinyl chloride, or chlorinated polyvinyl chloride) should be considered for water and corrosive service; see Section 400. Fiberglass-reinforced epoxy and polyester pipe are also available but are generally more expensive.

The plastics are attractive because of their low weight, resistance to corrosion, and ease of installation. Plastic pipe generally has better flow characteristics than steel.

Use caution when selecting plastic pipe for hydrocarbon service (see Section 400). It should not be used where fire is a hazard or near heat sources, which may soften and weaken plastic. It must also be protected from mechanical damage.

Generally, plastic pipe should be buried (below the frost line in freezing climates). Areas of particular concern aboveground include adequate support, thermal

expansion (greater for plastic than for carbon steel), protection from mechanical damage, and maximum and minimum temperature limitations, both operating and ambient. Ultraviolet light degradation can also be a problem. Section 400 recommends UV protective coating systems.

At ground level, plastic pipe is often placed in a steel sleeve for increased strength and resistance to sunlight. Properly spaced centralizers (cradles) are always installed around the plastic pipe to prevent rubbing. Centralizers are available from several manufacturers (e.g., Ribbit, T. D. Williamson).

Pressure calculations for plastic pipe are specific to the pipe material and operating conditions. Some general methods are included in Section 400. Manufacturers may be consulted for guidance.

**Plastic-lined steel pipe** is stronger than plastic pipe and is unaffected by sunlight. More expensive than steel, it is primarily used where pure plastic pipe is not strong enough. Most connections are made with shop-fabricated flanges. Field mounting of flanges is difficult, may be time-consuming, and may require special equipment. Good layout drawings are especially important.

## Considerations Other Than First Cost

This manual does not necessarily recommend the material with the lowest cost. Initial savings must be weighed against other considerations. For example, future operations should be considered. Higher maintenance and downtime costs could more than offset a slight initial saving.

**Cast iron valves**: while cheaper than steel, are not recommended for hydrocarbon service, because cast iron tends to fracture when sprayed with water during a fire or stressed by the piping. Cast iron valves require flat face flanges, a full face gasket, and special care during installation. Bolting a flat face cast iron flange against a raised face steel flange can break the more brittle cast iron. Another objection is that a cast iron valve might be mistakenly installed in a service that requires steel. Finally, cast iron is virtually impossible to repair. A steel valve can be welded. OSHA does not allow cast iron valves in hydrocarbon service. See Section 100.

**Lower grade pipe** can be considered, but the savings usually do not justify the special procedures required for safe use.

## Specific Project Considerations

The economics of material selection are different for every project. Even though cost might be prohibitive on a small project, the special handling and material control required for lower cost materials might be justified on a large project. Alternatives that may realize such savings include the following:

- Use thinner wall, higher strength pipe (API 5LX grades) rather than standard API 5L pipe. Welding may cost more. X grades require special care in purchasing, welding, and bending. Thin wall fittings may cost more to purchase and have longer delivery times than standard weight. A mixture of pipe grades in the same facility could cause errors during repairs.

- Surplus or used material may be used if the purchaser is certain that it meets all engineering requirements. If there is any question, it should not be used.

## Fabrication

The preferred fabrication method is butt welding, using flanges or self-energizing hub-and-clamp (Grayloc) connectors. Screwed pipe is normally used in small sizes (NPS 1½ or less) where welding cost is high.

**Welded Fabrication.** Welded fabrication is covered in Section 620, Welding and Stress Relief. See also the *Welding Manual*.

**Socket Welded Fabrication.** Socket welded joints are preferred over seal welded, threaded joints. Do not "bottom out" socket weld connections. Instead, use square-cut pipe and space the pipe 1/16 inch back from the bottom of the socket. This prevents the weld from cracking as it contracts during cooling.

**Screwed Fabrication.** Screwed fittings other than galvanized should be ASTM A105 carbon steel, 3000 psi class. Valves should be carbon steel and pipe should be Schedule 80, Grade B. Fittings and pipe for higher pressures and temperature are identified on specific piping class sheets.

Seal welding of screwed joints is required in vibrating, pulsating, and toxic services. Joints should be made up dry, because thread compound, Teflon tape, and lubricant adversely affect weld quality. Also, vapors from burning Teflon tape are hazardous. To be effective in reducing stress concentrations, seal welds should cover all the threads, including the last scratch mark of the threading die.

Galvanized pipe and fittings can be used to reduce corrosion in potable water service and in all air service except engine-starting air. Use steel pipe and fittings for engine-starting air. Do not seal weld galvanized pipe; poisonous zinc vapors may be generated.

## Testing

New hydrocarbon and other hazardous piping systems should be pressure tested with fresh water (hydrotested) to detect leaks and to allow their repair before the system is put into service. See Section 600 for detailed information.

# 720 Pressure Design

## 721 General Requirements

### Types of Pipe and Manufacture

Producing facilities generally require seamless or electric resistance weld (ERW) pipe. For most applications seamless pipe is more desirable because its composition and hardness is homogeneous and it does not have a longitudinal (or spiral) joint. Seamless pipe is produced by hot extrusion (hot piercing) and sized with rolls and plugs.

Electric Resistance Weld (ERW) pipe is formed by forcing together the edges of a long, thin ribbon of plate called skelp. The longitudinal butt joint is made after heating the joint area to fusion temperature with electrical resistance heating.

Both types have a 12½% mill tolerance for thickness.

See Section 312 of the *Pipeline Manual* for more information on pipe manufacturing processes, and Section 320 of this manual for guidance in material selection.

## Governing Codes and Standards

ANSI/ASME Code B31.3, *Chemical Plant and Petroleum Refinery Piping*, covers materials, design, fabrication, and installation for petroleum refinery pressure and vacuum piping systems. It is used for most facilities including wellhead piping.

The following related standards are outside the scope of this manual, but are sometimes encountered in producing facilities:

- ANSI/ASME B31.4, *Liquid Transportation Systems for Hydrocarbons, Liquid Petroleum Gas, Anhydrous Ammonia, and Alcohols*. See the *Pipeline Manual*.
- ANSI/ASME B31.8, *Gas Transmission and Distribution Piping Systems*. See the *Pipeline Manual*.
- Department of Transportation (D.O.T.) Federal Safety Standards for Transportation of Hazardous Materials, Code of Federal Regulations, Title 49, Transportation, Parts 192 and 195 (49 CFR 192 and 195).
- California Administrative Code, Title 8. Two sections of Title 8 pertain to piping: the General Industrial Safety Orders and the Petroleum Safety Codes. The former addresses work access requirements, insulating hot pipe for personnel protection, piping identification, pipe supports, etc.; the latter covers requirements for gas testing, blinding, drainage, etc.

## Pressure Design by ANSI/ASME B31.3

Pressure design formulas and methods for ANSI/ ASME B31.3 are given in Section 200.

## Pipe Specification

Piping used in surface installations is normally specified as follows:

**Nominal Pipe Size (NPS).** For pipe through NPS 12 the outside diameter (OD) is slightly larger than the nominal diameter. Above NPS 12 the OD equals the nominal diameter. In all sizes, the OD is constant and the inside diameter varies with wall thickness.

The Company normally uses pipe in sizes NPS ¾, 1½, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, etc. The Company never uses NPS 2½ or NPS 5. Tubing is generally used for sizes smaller than NPS ¾.

**Wall Thickness.** Std, XS, XXS, Sch 40, and Sch 80 describe the pipe schedule or nominal pipe wall thickness. The actual thickness can vary 12½% from the nominal thickness. Tubing sizes and wall thickness are defined differently. See Section 200.

**Material.** Pipe used in producing facilities is usually seamless or electric resistance welded (ERW). See Section 741 for more on ERW pipe applications. Seamless or ERW pipe is usually manufactured under one of the following specifications:

- API SPEC 5L Grade B. This is the most common pipe grade used in producing facilities. It is a general purpose pipe with good weldability. It can be seamless or ERW.

- ASTM SPEC A53 Grade B. This is equivalent to API SPEC 5L Grade B and is approved for Code piping installations. It has good weldability and can be seamless or ERW.

- ASTM SPEC A106 Grade B. Although this pipe meets the same mechanical requirements as the previous two, its good quality control of chemical properties makes it superior at both high temperatures (above 450°F) and low temperatures (below 40°F). It has good weldability and is always seamless.

- API SPEC 5LX Grades X42 to X65. This high strength pipe is generally not used for producing piping. It is normally economical only in larger sizes and at higher pressures. Special welding procedures may be required. X grade pipe may not be suitable where stress corrosion cracking could be a problem.

Pipe not marked with specification numbers should be limited to noncritical services or tested to determine if it meets acceptable specifications.

## 722 High Temperature Service

As temperature increases, the yield strength of steel decreases. Pipe with a yield strength of 35,000 psi at ambient temperature has negligible strength at 1500°F. Most steels should not be used above 800°F. ANSI/ASME B31.3 takes this into account by derating the allowable stress (SE).

Few rubbers, elastomers, and fluoroelastomers can survive elevated (200°F to 400°F) temperatures. Consult Materials and Equipment Engineering at CRTC and manufacturer's data for more information. See also Section 200 for information on valve seals and packing.

## 723 Low Temperature Service

Low metal temperatures may result from ambient conditions, stock temperature or autorefrigeration by expanding gas. Metal subjected to low temperatures requires special treatments to avoid embrittlement failures. For seamless pipe, the proper treatment depends on the metal temperature, as follows:

- Below 40°F: Pipe is normalized if it is thicker than 3/8 inch. If thicker than ¾ inch it is also impact tested.

- Below -20°F: Pipe is normalized and impact tested for all wall thicknesses (per ANSI/ASME B31.3).

Thickness calculations and allowable stresses are the same as for higher temperatures. Thus, the formulas and graphs in ANSI/ASME B31.3 Section 721.C are valid for low temperatures.

Pipe welds must also be impact tested. This is done during the weld qualification tests. Stress relieving of welds should also be considered to improve low temperature performance and resistance to brittle fracture.

ANSI/ASME B31.3 specifies minimum impact test results required; however, the Company has stricter requirements:

- Below 65,000 psi tensile strength the average Charpy V-notch value should be 15 ft-lb, with no value less than 12 ft-lb.
- Above 65,000 psi tensile strength, the average Charpy V-notch value should be 20 ft-lb, with no value less than 15 ft-lb.

Normalizing and impact testing are not necessarily required merely because the ambient conditions fall below the established temperature limits. The pipe wall temperature may be hotter if the stock inside the pipe is hotter. Each situation should be evaluated individually, and the following should be taken into account:

- If flow is stopped, the pipe will cool to the cold ambient temperature. This is not serious providing the pressure is released at the same time.
- Pipe and welds that are not normalized and impact tested may have to be heated before applying pressure.

# 730 Piping Practices

This section discusses design of producing facility piping. The general topics covered here are frequently included in piping specifications and are intended as convenient references.

## 731 Site Layout

Site layout must be adequate for maintenance and operations access, fire protection, and efficient and expandable operation.

### Maintenance and Operations Access

Aside from fire protection requirements, field facilities should be designed to allow operators free access to all controls, instruments, and equipment. Field locations generally consist of a graveled location, one or more pieces of major equipment (tanks, pumps, compressors, filters, etc.), a pipe rack, and an electrical panel. The location is frequently fenced.

Designers should allow sufficient space around all equipment for easy maintenance access. To allow the operator to drive around equipment, there should be a

minimum of 15 unobstructed feet between pieces of equipment (as well as the electrical panel and pipe rack). This should be free of piping, guy wires, and electrical connections. Equipment spacing requirements are also given in the *Fire Protection Manual*. A minimum 36-inch operator access clearance should be maintained between piping in congested areas.

Shell-and-tube heat exchangers should have enough room around them to allow pulling the tube bundle. Compressors and pumps require enough room to remove cylinders, shafts, bearings, and other parts without removing piping.

## Tips for Good Piping Arrangement

Piping components should be standardized whenever possible, based on what is available in the area and on operations and maintenance preferences.

If piping is buried, burying a strip of **Terra-Tape** warning tape directly over the pipe will help prevent accidental damage during excavation, and will aid in locating the line. Another approach is to survey and sketch pipe routes. Buried piping may be covered with concrete or steel cables for additional protection. Metal foil Terra Tape should be used with buried plastic lines to allow easy detection with a pipe locator.

Pipe should not be run under foundations. It will be difficult to dig up if it leaks, it can become a gas collection pocket if abandoned, and it may allow differential foundation settlement.

In cold climates, lines should be buried below the frost line to avoid freezing and the expense of steam or electrical line tracing. Risers may require tracing and insulation above the frost line.

Burial of steam lines should be avoided because of the difficulty in collecting condensate from traps. When steam lines (or other process lines prone to condensation, such as wet gas) must be installed below grade (e.g., at road crossings), the line should slope towards a steam trap or other dump trap.

Equipment and valves that require periodic testing should not be isolated, even if actual use is minimal. The classic examples are pressure/safety valves. These should be installed close to grade (without creating liquid traps) or with platform access, so that the equipment inspector can get at them. Install equipment within direct reach of the operator, except when the process allows no other choice; the operator may be tempted to neglect equipment that is difficult to service. Ensure that relief valves discharge to lower elevation, self-draining relief piping.

Do not install equipment where poisonous H₂S or flammable LPG vapors may accumulate. See Sections 1000 and 1100.

Because of the increased possibility of corrosion, aboveground pipe should not be placed directly on soil. In many field facilities, such pipe is normally installed on 4-inch by 4-inch wooden, concrete or steel sleepers spaced at 10-foot intervals. Large plants use dedicated pipe racks. See Section 320 for pipeway design.

Piping that penetrates reinforced concrete structures should avoid contact with rebar and temporary forming lumber, which may cause abrasion or corrosion. Such piping should be installed in a sleeve for easy replacement.

Flanged spools should be included in internally coated and critical lines to monitor corrosion. The spools should be short enough, especially in large diameter lines, to be removed by a small crew with an "A-frame" truck. Spools may also be necessary for equipment and startup strainer removal.

When a new connection is made to an existing piping system, the electrical potential between the new and the old must be checked. Differences in potential are caused by galvanic action and by cathodic protection systems. Flange insulation kits are used if the difference is significant. This particularly applies to flow lines, casing gas collection lines, and gas gathering lines directly connected to wellheads. Weld end insulating kits are also available. Refer to the *Corrosion Prevention Manual* for more information.

See Sections 320 and 710 for more on layout.

## Fire Protection

Fire protection is primarily afforded by access to equipment, spacing of equipment, ability to contain spills, and availability of fire extinguishing agents. See the *Fire Protection Manual* and Section 320 of this manual.

Extreme fire hazards should be 50 to 150 feet from escape routes, fire water piping, and other fire hazards. Consult the Company Health, Environmental and Loss Prevention (HE&LP) staff for specific recommendations. Fire water piping should have a minimum of mechanical connections and absolutely no wafer valves. Stud bolts stretch in a fire (especially the long ones used with wafer valves) and could allow leaks, reducing fire water supply and pressure. To minimize flanges consider top-entry butt-weld end valves. Screwed fire water piping is acceptable but may be impractical because of flow rate limitations.

Fire water piping, fittings and valves should be steel throughout. Do not use cast iron, aluminum, or bronze; cast iron may fracture and the other materials could melt.

Producing facilities use flexible hoses to transfer products between tanks, remove hydrocarbons from pits, and load tank trucks. Such hoses can be a fire hazard unless properly grounded. As a rule, hoses used to transfer flammable liquids must be bonded to ground. Bonding must be checked before every use because kinks may permanently damage the bonding wire. This is done by testing the resistance across the hose grounding connections. Any hose exceeding about 200 ohms total resistance should be rejected.

Hydrants should be placed from 20 to 50 feet from major hazards and fitted with at least two connections, each with a capacity of 250 GPM. Hoses and reels should be installed so that hoses are long enough to reach all hazards in the area. Standard Drawings GD-S99621 and GB-S1007 in the *Fire Protection Manual* give typical hydrant designs.

Piping flexibility is a major concern. See Section 330.

## 732 Skid Layout

A skid fabricator may not use the same materials and construction techniques normally used in field construction. It is very important for the engineer to qualify weld procedures, piping materials, connection and support methods, and pressure ratings of all components. This is commonly done by providing the fabricator with a piping specification.

Skids are generally much more congested than onsite construction because every square foot of footprint costs the fabricator money. He may therefore be tempted to use shortcuts such as allowing insufficient valve clearance, surrounding equipment with piping in such a way that it is difficult to remove equipment without removing piping, providing poor operator access, and stacking components so that they are out of reach. To avoid these problems, the fabricator should be required to submit shop drawings for review and Company approval before fabrication starts.

Once the skid is painted, construction materials cannot be traced. Fabricators may try to conceal use of foreign pipe and fittings in this manner. Valves can usually be traced, even if painted, by tagged data or casting marks. If warranted, a certificate can be obtained from the manufacturer, usually at considerable cost, that certifies that pipe and components meet the piping specification. This can cost up to 10% extra depending on location and manufacturer.

Skid fireproofing is generally required in gas plants and other fire hazard areas. It is important to provide a means to fireproof the skid.

Skid equipment is generally installed on a structural steel base for lifting support and structural rigidity in operation. The base, not the piping, must support the equipment. The skid should have lifting eyes for easy placement. Piping should not be used for lifting.

Avoid skid bases over 30 inches high; if they are higher, handrails will be required at extra cost. See the *Safety in Designs Manual*.

If the skid will eventually be shipped by truck, the footprint must be small enough to fit on a truck bed, and the module must clear overhead obstructions.

Skids should be electrically grounded.

## 733 Pipeways and Racks

Pipeways are now used in almost all field process plants. This section discusses rack design for accessibility and future expansion, but not structural design, which is covered in detail in Section 320.

Pipe racks consist of a series of bents (inverted U-shaped structures). The bents are structural frames consisting of two columns (stanchions) and connecting horizontal cross members where the pipe lines rest. Pipe racks are used only for multiple,

parallel pipe runs that require a common support. They also support other equipment, such as cable trays, instrument lines, utility lines, and electrical conduit. The area under a pipe rack is usually a general service space or aisle for installation, maintenance and operational functions.

## Pipe Racks

The following guidelines should be followed when placing piping on field pipe racks:

- Mount insulated pipes on hangers (preferred) or shoes.
- Insulate hot pipes (usually over 140°F). Insulation thickness and location depends on ambient conditions, cost of energy, process requirements, fluid type, and the need for personnel protection.
- Electrical and instrument trays for conduit and cables are best placed on outriggers or brackets to minimize interference with pipes leaving the pipeway. Alternatively, trays may be attached to stanchions.
- Elevations for pipe racks should be set to avoid pockets and flat turns in the piping. Lines should drain into equipment or other lines that also can be drained.
- Pipes sized NPS 1½ and smaller may require support by trays, grating or other continuous supports to minimize sag. Insulating an entire tray may be less expensive than insulating a portion of the contents separately.

## Supports

Pipe supports must be designed to support not only the weight of the pipe and its contents but also the insulation and other loading factors. Climatic conditions may introduce external loading such as snow or ice, and dynamic effects and occasional loads must be allowed for, such as hydrotests, impact, hydraulic shock, wind loads, earthquake, mechanical vibrations, and discharge reactions.

Pipe support systems must accommodate displacement caused by thermal expansion and contraction of the pipe under normal and abnormal operating conditions. Hangers and supporting equipment are designed to allow piping free movement for thermal expansion, which is independent of pipe schedule and size. Pipe must not be immobilized in a manner that adversely transfers loads from one support to another or from the supports to the piping. If held immobile, a 6-inch diameter steel pipe with ⅝-inch wall thickness generates 50 tons of force if its temperature changes by 50°F. Larger pipes are capable of generating much larger thermal forces.

Pipe systems should have sloping lines for adequate line drainage. Some areas of the line may sag, such as heavily loaded sections where valves or other components increase the weight. Locating supports close to heavy valves and equipment reduces the loads that cause traps.

Piping attached to equipment must be securely braced near the piece of equipment to avoid undue stress on its connections. The best way to accomplish this is to install a support in the equipment foundation.

Pipe supports should be designed with a generous safety factor, usually a 3:1 ratio of designed capability to actual load.

## 734 Piping Connected to Pressure Vessels

Piping connected to pressure vessels includes relief devices, gage and sample cocks, sight glasses, instrumentation connections, process piping, and water jet lines.

Section VIII of the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code stipulates vessel and piping design up to the vessel discharge flange. Downstream of this point, conventional ANSI piping specifications apply: ANSI/ASME B31.3 for most nonboiler applications and ANSI/ASME B31.1 for power boiler piping (see Section 100 and the *Pressure Vessel Manual*).

Pressure vessels cannot be drilled and tapped, and saddle attachments that do not meet code requirements cannot be installed. Connections require a full penetration weld between the connection fitting and the vessel. Postweld heat treatment may also be required. No field changes can be made without a hydrotest. Depending on the type of vessel and degree of inspection, pressure vessels may have to be certified by a state inspector.

Small (NPS 1½ inch and less) connections typically have screwed or socket weld ends. Socket welded or screwed and completely seal welded connections are required for services involving highly toxic or flammable products such as LPG or H₂S-laden streams. Larger connections are flanged. The design of piping connected to these penetrations must be adequate to prevent leaks, environmental damage, and health threats.

**Pressure Relief Devices.** Pressure relief devices are the most critical piping component connected to a vessel. Relief devices are typically either safety valves, relief valves, or rupture disks. Sizing criteria are given in the *Instrumentation and Control Manual*.

A **safety valve** is an automatic, spring-loaded valve actuated by static pressure upstream of the valve. It is characterized by rapid full opening (pop). It is normally used in vapor or gas service.

A **relief valve** is an automatic spring-loaded valve actuated by static pressure upstream of the valve. Unlike a safety valve, it opens in proportion to the upstream pressure (once opening has started). It is used primarily for liquid service, and to prevent product loss is designed to reclose after normal conditions have been restored.

A **rupture disk** is a nonreclosing pressure-relief device that is actuated by inlet static pressure and functions by bursting. The rupture disk itself is both the pressure-containing and pressure-sensitive element of the device. The rupture disk holder and knife hold the disk in place. Rupture disks come in a variety of configurations, such as plain flat, prebulged, or reverse buckling. The configuration affects the operating point and the capability of the disk to protect against vacuum conditions. Disks are usually either a thin stainless steel sheet or a brittle carbon material. Disk material is not required to conform to an ASME specification. Material for the rupture disk.

holder is listed in Section II and Division UG of the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code, Section VIII.

Rupture disks are used for low pressure, high volume relief. They are also used under PSVs in dirty or corrosive service to protect the valve, but a properly trimmed PSV is recommended.

Pressure relief devices are generally installed vertically on pressure vessels without a block valve between the relief device and the vessel; a stop valve could be inadvertently closed, isolating the relief valve from the vessel. Block valves are sometimes approved (on an individual basis) for high maintenance or frequently inspected relief devices and are always locked open, with the Equipment Inspector or Area Foreman retaining the key.

The piping between the vessel and relief device must have the same cross-sectional area available as the relief device. However, discharge piping may be oversized to reduce backpressure on the relief system; this is an engineering decision that must be made based on the capacity of the relief system or flare stack.

Finally, relief devices must be tested periodically. Intervals between examination are usually one to four years, depending on the service, location, air quality emission restrictions, and local restrictions. See the *Instrumentation and Control Manual* for more information. Since testing is mandatory, it is of great benefit to the equipment inspector if the valve or test lines are readily accessible. Relief devices should generally be installed as close to grade as possible without creating liquid traps.

## Instrument Piping

Instruments may be attached to pressure vessels to measure pressure, temperature, viscosity, level, or other process variables. Attachment is usually by means of a *nozzle* penetration through the vessel shell or head.

Because the probe may be damaged, penetration-type instruments should not be installed on vibrating vessels (such as reciprocating compressor suction and discharge bottles). Instead, a sensor should be installed on the vessel, with the instrument installed on a nonvibrating support.

Minimize vessel connections by using bridle-connected instruments. The instrument can then be isolated with block valves on the bridle piping and removed without taking the vessel out of service. Also, several instruments can be installed on the bridle, sharing a single set of vessel connections. This allows retrofits without additional vessel penetrations. Heavy bridles should not depend on vessel connections for support; provide additional support.

**Sight glasses** display the liquid level in a vessel. The simplest type is a glass tube between block valves. Block valves are necessary because the tube breaks easily, and because frequent cleaning is necessary to remove oil, foam, and other buildup within the glass. More advanced and damage-resistant sight glasses use armored glass windows or floats with magnetic followers on the outside that are connected to a scale.

**Gage cocks** (sample valves) are small valves used to draw a sample from a vessel for analysis or determination of level. Depending on the application, ball valves (for noncritical services), and globe or needle valves (for critical services) are used. In either case, a tight shutoff is required. Gage cocks should be plugged with a bar stock plug when they are not in use.

**Transducers, capillaries, pressure sensor lines**, and other instruments may be attached to pressure vessels using small-diameter screwed connections. Typically, connection size should not be less than NPS 1, and the pipe installed not less than NPS ¾. When a smaller line is required, the NPS 1 connection can be swaged down to the requisite size. Bushing reducers must not be used. Stainless steel tubing can be used for lines smaller than NPS ¾. Avoid copper tubing. See Sections 200 and 710.

## Process Piping

Generally, process piping size is at least NPS 2. Process piping is commonly flanged at the vessel connections and has a block valve (see following). All flanged vessel connections should have a spectacle blind so the vessel can be positively isolated.

Vessels are very sensitive to thermal expansion of the process piping. Expanding piping can shear foundation anchor bolts, bend nozzles and buckle vessel walls. Expansion loops and flexible layouts are used to minimize this problem. It is important to prevent excessive stress from being transferred into the vessels.

Steel gate valves, globe valves, and unlined, firesafe plug valves are approved as primary vessel block valves. Butterfly valves are not. Ball valves are acceptable subject to some restrictions:

- The ball valve must be rated firesafe for flammable services.
- The ball valve body can be safely drained.
- The stem must be blowout-proof.

Ball valves that seal both sides of the ball can trap a quantity of product between the seats, within the body cavity. If this product cannot be safely discharged through a relief valve, rising temperatures (e.g., in a fire) can rupture the body, allowing a leak to occur. Some split wedge gate valves also fail in this way.

The minimum size of vessel drains is usually NPS 2; except on very small or noncritical vessels. Large drains allow the vessel to be quickly discharged if necessary and are resistant to plugging and damage. Drain valves are usually gate or ball valves; it is usually unnecessary to throttle drains with a globe valve. Gates are safer because they can't fully open all at once. Also, globe drain valves cannot be rodded out if they plug. Make sure vessel is vented to avoid pulling a vacuum while draining.

## Water Jet Lines

Water jet lines are used to blast sand and other debris from a heater/treater vessel during cleaning. They consist of an external header and an internal water diffuser. The diffuser is generally designed as part of the vessel package, and is outside the scope of this manual. The header is generally sized 2 inches or larger, and is

connected to the vessel by a gate valve. Potable water should not be used for jet wash.

## 735 Steam Flood Piping

Piping for steam flood requires special attention. It should be designed to ANSI/ ASME B31.3 with seamless pipe only. Velocities must be kept low to avoid erosion from the water in the typical 80% quality steam. Expansion/contraction must be accounted for with adequate piping flexibility (pipe loops and non-fixed supports).

## 736 High Pressure Piping

Thick wall pipe and the proper ANSI class flanges and other components are commonly available for pressures in excess of 4000 psi. The problem is to adequately seal joints to prevent high velocity leaks through or around the sealing medium. A high pressure leak can be lethal, acting like a "water lance."

Invisible leaks (e.g., noncondensed steam) are particularly dangerous.

The recommended approach is to use welded connections wherever possible. Otherwise, screwed connections are allowed in small diameters and are commonly used in high pressure gas well piping. Octagonal ring joint (ORJ) flanges are also commonly specified, but may be harder to assemble. If raised face (RF) flanges are used for ANSI Class 900 and above, spiral-wound gasket with an additional inner blowout ring is used to make the confined joint.

See Appendix B for a discussion of hub-and-clamp connectors and installation practices.

ERW pipe is not recommended for high pressure (above about 700 psig, or ANSI Class 300) systems. Use seamless pipe instead.

Problems associated with high pressure lines as opposed to low pressure lines include the following:

- Sulfide stress cracking occurs more readily. See Section 737.
- Leaks discharge in greater quantity.
- Sudden release of energy contained in a high pressure fluid could cause substantial damage.
- Valve shutoff may be difficult against a high differential pressure. Excessive wear in rubbing surfaces may occur sooner than anticipated.
- Welding of thicker wall pipe is more critical and requires more passes. Heat treatment may be necessary. The number of radiographic weld tests generally increases from a 5% minimum to 20+% minimum (see PIM-MS-2505). Test interpretation is more difficult because the pipe wall shadow is darker.
- Pipe, valves, and other components are much more expensive. The relationship of cost to pressure rating is exponential.

- High pressure piping and components are much heavier. Special care is required to provide adequate support, particularly for valves.

## 737 Compressor and Pump Piping

Piping for rotating machinery must resist vibration and convey products in a streamlined manner.

### Vibration

Failures from vibration occur most often at points of stress concentration such as pipe threads, poor weld penetrations, supports welded to pipe, and rapid changes in wall thickness such as reinforcing saddles and pipe of different wall thickness welded without beveling.

A large unsupported mass on a relatively small pipe stub, such as a relief valve, bleeder valve, high pressure blind flange, or pressure gage, could also cause vibrational failure.

Fatigue failures can be reduced by the following measures:

- Use rotating rather than reciprocating machines.
- Install pulsation dampeners. See Appendix C and the *Compressor Manual*.
- Change the natural frequency of piping with bracing, by changing lengths and wall thicknesses, etc., or change the machinery speed so that the exciting frequency does not match the natural frequency of the piping.
- Hire a consultant to make an analog study of the piping layout and locate possible problem areas. Contact Materials and Equipment Engineering at CRTC for guidance. See also the discussion on vibration studies in the *Compressor Manual*.
- Remove stress concentrations by seal or bridge welding threaded pipe; use smooth transitions (Sweepolets, tees, etc.) instead of reinforcing saddles; and use full penetration welds.
- Reduce the pendulum effect with small, lightweight, close-coupled fittings; use hub-and-clamp connections and stainless steel tubing instead of rigid screwed pipe; use reduced port valves for vents, drains, and pressure taps; and locate branch line valves and gages as close as possible to the header. Note that reduced port valves are more prone to plugging problems than full port valves.
- Use NPS ¾ Schedule 80 pipe as the minimum pipe size.
- Minimize the number of connections.
- Use pipe clamps instead of U-bolts.

## Layout

Piping should be designed to minimize turbulence. This can be done by avoiding sharp changes in direction close to machine connections (using long radius elbows or bends), and using full port/high C_v valves and oversize suction lines.

Machinery should have block valves, suction and discharge piping vents and drains, and a discharge-to-suction bypass (to reduce backpressure at startup).

Compressor piping should slope up at 8% from the scrubber to a suction bottle to allow any liquids to drain back to the scrubber. Reducers should be bottom flat to avoid condensation traps.

Pump piping should generally slope up from suction to vessel or tank. Reducers in suction piping should be top flat to avoid air pockets. Suction lift piping should slope up continuously from the liquid source to the pump, and the pump casing should have a vent line so air can be purged. Suction lift piping should have a foot valve and strainer.

When machines are operated in parallel, uneven flow splitting may occur. Identical or mirror image piping arrangements will help avoid the problem.

Suction piping should be as short as possible. Pump suction piping is generally one or two pipe sizes larger than the pump connection. Often, a vortex breaker and a startup strainer are installed (see Section 200).

Compressors and positive displacement pumps require a pressure relief or safety valve, often included as part of the discharge piping. The relief valve must be sized to pass full capacity at 110% of the design pressure. Consult the *Instrumentation and Control Manual* for sizing practices.

Relief piping should be routed back to a suction vessel or tank, not to the suction piping, or overheating may occur. A bypass is usually needed so that the machine can start up under minimum load, and is also used to recirculate fluid for flow control.

Pump discharge piping is often larger than the pump connections. Compressor discharge piping may include a short length of small diameter piping between the discharge flange and the pulsation bottle. This is called a choke tube and is used to reduce vibration and pressure variations. Choke tubes are often integral with the pulsation bottle. They can be easily sized with PC computer program GLTW7100. Information on this program and other sizing methods (SWRI for example) can be found in the *Compressor Manual*.

Block and check valves should be the same size as the pump connection, not the suction line. This reduces cost because smaller valves can be used. Compressor block valves, however, may be larger because they may be installed some distance away, outboard of the pulsation bottles, choke tubes, or knockout vessels.

Machinery lubrication lines should be of stainless steel tubing and installed in rigidly mounted trays to reduce vibration damage. Connections should be ferrule compression joints such as Swagelok.

Instrument air lines should also be stainless steel tubing. Do not use Teflon tape or pipe thread compound on the threads; it may clog instruments. Instrument air lines should have periodic traps to remove condensation. Do not use copper tubing in natural gas service, including gas-operated control valves. Do not use carbon steel tubing; corrosion can clog instruments. The minimum practical tubing size is ¼ inch.

Compressor interstage and discharge piping may be hot, to 250°F. All piping over 140°F must be insulated if it is a personnel hazard.

Piping around machinery may be congested. A 3-inch clearance is required around valve handles, and room should be left to disconnect the machine and remove it. Valves and instruments requiring frequent attention should be placed where it is convenient for the operator to read and adjust them, generally close to grade.

Piping and emergency block valves around machinery should always be conspicuous. Piping should be marked with flow arrows, color coding (per the *Safety in Designs Manual*), and labeled to indicate the contents.

## 738 Sour Service

Those responsible for design and construction **must** be aware of the poisonous and corrosive hazards of H₂S (hydrogen sulfide) and design accordingly. Hazards and design information are provided in Material Safety Data Sheet No. 301, Hydrogen Sulfide. Consult Section 1000 for more information on designing for H₂S service.

### Safety Aspects of H₂S

H₂S is a colorless gas commonly found in crude oil and natural gas products from oil wells. It is also found in water produced from geothermal wells. H₂S is highly toxic. Over an extended exposure period concentrations of 100 to 150 ppm can be lethal.

High concentrations of H₂S can be emitted from gage settings, sulfur removal plants, casing collection systems, and gas plants. Concentrations in the 7000 ppm range are not unusual. H₂S is heavier than air and tends to collect in low spots such as valve boxes, well cellars, sumps, and tank cleanout cellars.

In lines containing H₂S, connections should be minimized. If possible, all piping should be butt welded. If this is not possible, piping NPS 1½ and smaller should be either socket welded or seal welded. Socket weld valves are preferred. Piping NPS 2 and larger should be butt welded. Weld end valves with top entry bonnets should be considered as an alternative to flanged connections.

High pressure sour piping is more susceptible to sulfide stress cracking than lower pressure piping. At 5000 psi, NACE MR-75-01 limits H₂S concentration to 10 PPM, while at 500 PSI it is 100 PPM. Above the NACE limits, special material procedures should be followed to prevent sulfide stress cracking.

Other guidelines for layout and materials selection are as follows:

- Screwed unions are unsuitable for H₂S service because they cannot be seal welded at the union ring.
- Cast iron valves may not be used; thermal expansion or fire exposure may cause the valve to rupture.
- All gaskets must be confined.
- In general, H₂S piping and equipment should be located so that prevailing winds sweep gas leaks off the plant location, not across it.
- H₂S piping layouts should minimize congestion, especially around safety showers. Piping should be arranged so that workers can find escape routes, breathing apparatus and safety showers quickly (possibly while blinded). Piping should allow clear visibility into the plant or well area so that an over-come person can be easily seen.
- Valves must have bolted, welded, or pressure seal bonnets (with a secondary mechanical retention feature). Block-and-bleed valves with pressure seal bonnets may fail if excess pressure develops in the body, either from thermal expansion when closed or from the process. Such valves should be equipped with a pressure relieving device on the body. Union bonnets are unacceptable because they can back out of the body if packing sticks to the shaft. Screwed bonnets are acceptable only if welded closed.
- Piping runs must not have low spots or liquid traps where water can collect. H₂S and water form sulfurous acid, which accelerates corrosion.
- Pipelines containing H₂S should be clearly labeled with signs or decals.

## Corrosion

H₂S can cause rapid, catastrophic failure of ferrous metals through stress corrosion cracking. This occurs in high tensile strength steel, and its effect can be minimized by purchasing pipe, valves, and fittings with hardness not exceeding Rockwell C22. Galvanic-cell corrosion also occurs if sulfurous acid is present.

The National Association of Corrosion Engineers (NACE) guidelines for piping in H₂S service are given in Specification MR-01-75. Valves should generally be ordered to this specification for H₂S concentrations above 125 ppm. This will ensure that hardness limitations are not exceeded and provide valve materials certification. Consult the *Corrosion Prevention Manual* for more detailed information.

## 739 Emergency Shutdown (ESD) Systems

Emergency shutdown (ESD) valves are an important part of any process safety system. They must operate quickly to isolate and/or depressure the process system upon the initiation of a shutdown. The term ESD valve generally includes the valve, an actuator, and switching accessories used to set the operating characteristics of the actuator.

## ESD Valves

ESD valves commonly used at producing facilities are:

- Gate (direct action and reverse gates)
- Quarter-turn (butterfly and ball)

See Section 200 for more information on these valves. Note that globe and plug valves are not commonly used as ESD valves.

Shutdown valves should have metal-to-metal seats, which maintain the shutoff capability of the valve in a fire. To qualify as firesafe, soft seated quarter-turn valves must have been independently tested under the fire conditions outlined in the latest edition of API STD 607 or API SPEC 6FA. The Company requires that quarter-turn valves in hydrocarbon service have secondary metal-to-metal seats.

When the valve is used for isolating purposes, usually the valve/actuator combination must have a fail-closed (FC) action. This means that the signal to the actuator will open the valve and removal of the signal will automatically close the valve. In the case of depressurizing or blowdown valves, the action is reversed and the valve/actuator combination must be fail-open (FO). Shutdown valves are either fully open or closed; they are not used as throttling valves. Shutdown valve stations often include block valves and bypasses to allow startup and blowdown.

## ESD Valve Actuators

Actuators should be mounted, tested, and bench-set (including limit switches) by the valve manufacturer. Actuators for ESD valves should be firesafe.

ESD actuators should be single-acting with spring return. A single-acting spring return actuator requires power only to drive it in one direction. If the power is removed, a spring will drive the piston in the opposite direction.

Fail-closed activation is achieved by locating a spring above the piston to send the valve closed upon removal of power supply. For fail-open activation the spring is located under the piston and the power applied above the piston. For a reverse-acting gate the opposite is true for each.

Gate valves require linear piston actuators, which consist of a piston mounted vertically above the valve body. A shaft attached to the piston connects to the valve stem. Power applied to the piston causes a vertical linear movement which raises or lowers the shaft.

Rotary piston actuators are used on quarter-turn ball, butterfly, and Orbit valves. The actuator is mounted horizontally on the valve body at 90 degrees to the valve stem. The orientation of the actuator is usually parallel with the flow path through the valve but can be perpendicular if piping configurations so dictate.

Diaphragm actuators have larger power application areas than piston actuators. The location of the spring determines the fail action. Diaphragm actuators are not firesafe unless insulated.

A rotary vane actuator consists of a cylinder which is partitioned into two 180-degree segments. A central shaft has two vanes mounted at 180 degrees to each other, with the vanes located in opposite partitions. Pressure is applied into each cylinder segment on opposite sides of the vanes, causing a rotary motion of the shaft. Rotary actuators generally have no fail response.

#### Accessories

Quick-exhaust solenoid valves are used with actuators to increase the speed of action by filling or exhausting the actuator rapidly.

Interconnecting devices are required to tie the shutdown valve into the ESD/alarm system. For an electrical shutdown system, solenoid valves are used. For a pneumatic shutdown system, pneumatically operated valves are used. Valve positioners and controllers are sometimes required to regulate valve performance. Consult the *Instrumentation and Control Manual* for more information.

Speed controls are available for adjusting the opening or closing time of a valve/actuator combination. They consist of a variable restrictor that is located in the exhaust port of the actuator.

Both electrical and pneumatic limit switches are available to provide remote indication of the valve position. Also, limit switches can be used to ensure that valves are opened or closed in a prescribed sequence.

ESD valves in fuel lines are often supplied with a lockout and manual reset device. This keeps the valve from automatically reopening once the shutdown signal has cleared, forcing the operator to go to the valve and consciously put it in service after a shutdown.

## 740 Piping Specifications

### 741 Piping Practices Unique to Producing Organizations

Some producing organizations have developed their own piping specifications for field production facilities used to collect, treat, and convey well fluids between the wellhead and point of custody transfer. These specifications were developed as an adjunct to the Corporation Piping Specification because the specification was designed primarily for refinery operations and contained more stringent restrictions than those needed for remote or low hazard field operations.

The current Corporation Piping Specification may differ from Operating Company Specifications in several ways. The most important differences are:

- Production organizations use more ERW pipe for certain noncritical applications, such as:
  - Air and fresh water systems
  - General and hydrocarbon services to 740 psig (onplot) or 2220 psig (offplot)

- Moderate H₂S service in casing vapor collection plants
- Soft water, for steam floods
- Glycol
- Up to 1600 psig offplot steam distribution systems

- ERW pipe is acceptable provided it is purchased in accordance with Company Specification PPL-MS-1050. Off-the-shelf ERW pipe is often not acceptable, so ERW purchases are usually limited to mill runs where inspection is possible. CUSA Western Region uses ANSI/ASME B31.8 instead of B31.4 for all offplot piping up to 450°F, even though the scope of the two specifications is different. Above 450°F, B31.3 is used. B31.4 is designed to be used for offplot liquid transmission lines, while B31.8 is for offplot gas transmission lines. CUSA Western Region decided to use B31.8 because of the close similarity between the two specifications.

- NPS 2 screwed wellhead and galvanized utility piping is allowed. The Corporation Piping Specification restricts screwed piping to NPS 1½ inch and less (except galvanized). This NPS 2 piping is generally installed in remote locations away from any urban development.

- Some producing organizations allow use of raised face flanges through ANSI Class 2500. When a confined joint gasket is necessary (i.e., above ANSI Class 600) spiral-wound gaskets are used.

- Corporation piping class sheets tend to be general, while producing class sheets are specifically designed for use in production fields. However, many specific Corporation class sheets have been developed over the years. Before using them, oil class sheets ensure that they are up-to-date.

- Plastic pipe is sometimes used to convey noncondensable vapors from casing gas collection systems to an incinerator. This is done because steel pipe corrodes too quickly. Such piping is always buried, labeled and routed to avoid accidental excavation.

## 742 Piping Specification Development

Specification development is covered in Section 100. Existing Corporate classification sheets should be examined to see if one will suffice for the new application. Be aware that if one is found, it may not have been updated in some time. Contact the ETD piping consultants in the Materials and Engineering Analysis Division to see if revision is needed.

Producing facility class sheets can also be examined for suitability. Contact operating companies directly. A good example is the CUSA Production Department Piping Guide. It lists many class sheets tailored for onshore field facilities. Similarly, Chevron Canada Resources piping classes may be adaptable to Alaska.
