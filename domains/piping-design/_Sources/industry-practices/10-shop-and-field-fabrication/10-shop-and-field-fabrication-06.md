## 612 Shop Fabrication

Economy and consistent quality are achieved by shop fabrication of piping into subassembly units called spools which are then transported to the jobsite, erected, and joined. The fabrication shop may be a permanent facility operated by a piping fabrication company and equipped for fabrication, quality control inspection and examination, and testing of pipe spools. Such shops usually have a wide range of qualified welding procedures and a core of regularly employed production and quality control personnel.

Shop fabrication allows better control of quality and project schedules by minimizing problems owing to bad weather, labor unavailability, and shipping delays. Commercial arrangements are normally handled by Company Purchasing and shop inspection by Purchasing Quality Assurance. Piping materials are usually procured

---

by the fabricator, but long-lead items may be ordered by the Company and shipped to the fabricator. Prime-coat painting may be included in the purchase order.

Alternatively, a jobsite weld bay may be set up by the plant construction contractor. Material procurement and radiographic or other NDT inspection can be handled by the contractor or the Company, as established in the construction contract. Welding procedures, welder qualifications, and site inspection are handled by Company construction engineers and inspectors.

Generally, union labor at the jobsite will refuse to erect spools fabricated by nonunion fabrication shop workers. A satisfactory resolution should be reached before placing the fabrication purchase order.

## 613 Equipment and Manifold Modules

Modularizing complete equipment assemblies or complex piping manifolds may be economical, particularly when labor costs at the field site are very high or where extreme weather conditions make field erection difficult. Examples are offshore and arctic projects. Also, packaged units such as refrigeration equipment, compressors, fired heaters, and metering and pressure control manifolds are available from vendors as standardized packages. The modules must be within transportable limits of size and weight, structurally designed for lifting, and able to support loads with minimal deflection and stressing of the components. Often temporary provisions are needed to avoid damage in shipment such as supports to limit vibration of instruments and small piping, guards to protect delicate items, and rust-preventive coatings or fluids to prevent corrosion of machinery parts.

Design and specifications for module piping and equipment may be done by the Company, or by the fabricator/erector to Company performance specifications, with either minimal or detailed piping specifications supplied by the Company. Procurement of the module is handled by Company Purchasing. Company-engineered equipment is often procured by the Company and shipped to the module fabricator/erector.

As with fabricated spools, union jobsite labor may refuse to erect modules from a nonunion shop.

## 620 Welding and PWHT

### 621 Welding Procedure and Welder Qualifications

#### Specifications

Welding procedures and welders are required by ASME/ANSI B31.3 to be qualified per ASME Section IX. Additional Company requirements for welding of pipe are contained in Specifications PIM-MS-2505, 4770, 4772.

---

Acceptable welding processes, discussed in the *Welding Manual*, are:

- Shielded metal arc welding (SMAW)

- Gas tungsten arc welding (GTAW)

- Gas metal arc welding (GMAW), interrupted arc. Interrupted arc GMAW is restricted to butt welds and is not used for weldolets and other attachments. Root passes may be made in any direction, but fill and cap passes are not permissible in the downhill direction below the two o'clock position.

- Flux cored arc welding (FCAW). Both gas-shielded and self-shielded electrodes are permitted. The types of self-shielded electrodes are restricted.

For the gas-shielded arc welding processes, the wind velocities in the weld area should not exceed 5 mph. Wind screens and enclosures should be used to shield the weld area if necessary.

Contractors must qualify their welding procedures except where they are provided Company qualified procedures. Qualification of welders by an organization other than the organization doing the welding is not acceptable.

## Seal Welding

Seal welding of threaded joints eliminates the stress risers caused by exposed threads. Secondarily, it eliminates leakage at threaded joints. Seal welding is normally used in piping services involving cyclic loads (vibrational or thermal) or flammable or dangerous fluids.

Seal welding should cover all exposed threads with weld metal and blend smoothly into the pipe (see Figure 600-1). Threaded joints must be made up dry, without thread compound or Teflon tape, and with proper thread engagement.

## Bridge Welding

In severe cyclic service valves at branch connections should be made with short nipples or male ends and the seal weld built up to the full hub diameter of the valve for the full length of the nipple. See Figure 600-2 for typical bridge welding details.

## Galvanized Pipe

Galvanized pipe should not be welded unless the galvanizing is ground off. The zinc from the galvanizing is a contaminant and if picked up by the weld can result in poor quality. Stainless steel welds are particularly sensitive to zinc and even minute pickup in the weld can result in severe cracking.

## Minimum Weld Spacing

The minimum spacing between circumferential weld joints should be 2 inches or 6 times the wall thickness of the pipe, whichever is greater.

## 622 Inspection and Testing of Welds

### Extent of Examination

As a minimum, butt-welded piping should be examined per ASME/ANSI B31.3 and Company requirements. In addition, any of the following factors may necessitate increasing the level of examination.

- Nature of the fluid being transported
- Operating temperature and pressure
- Material (low alloy vs. carbon steel)
- Welding process(es)
- Shop or field fabrication
- Fabricator experience
- Welder proficiency
- Environment

The decision to increase the level of examination should be made at the project level.

The Company requires the following minimum levels of examination:

- 100% radiography for piping in severe cyclic service
- 5% minimum radiography for all other piping applications

Some operating companies have different requirements.

ASME/ANSI B31.3 requires the following minimum examination:

- For **severe cyclic service**, visual examination of all fabrication and 100% radiography.
- For **hydrocarbon service**, both visual examination and radiography or ultrasonic examination of not less than 5% of the welds for each welder (or welding operator)
- For **Category D fluid service**, visual examination only

In addition to the Code B31.3 provisions for Category D fluid service, the Company normally employs radiography and ultrasonic examination to detect internal weld defects on **utility lines**, because failure could result in the shutdown of multiple process units. However, in organizing the requirements for field inspection, it should be recognized that visual examination is a most effective method for finding weld defects. Also, when visual examination is done in-process, it can help discover defects that would be more costly to repair after completion of welding.

Although Code B31.3 allows visual or ultrasonic inspection as a replacement for radiography, the Company's preference is to use radiography because it provides

---

exact documentation of the inspection. However, radiography is sensitive to the surface condition of the weld and preliminary visual examination can eliminate the need for reshots due to surface conditions such as external undercut or high cap.

## Guide to Weld Inspection

Section 520 of the *Welding Manual* is a comprehensive discussion of welding inspection for field foremen, engineers, and other Company representatives who are not specialists in welding and quality control but are responsible for obtaining good fabrication. Section 520 is also published separately in a pocket edition. Both the Manual and the pocket guide are available from the Engineering Technology Department.

## Hardness Testing

Company requirements for hardness testing differ from the requirements in Table 331.1.1 of ASME/ANSI B31.3 for maximum hardness and also for the frequency of inspection, as follows:

- For carbon steel, at least 5% of all welds shall be hardness tested (one test per weld and at least one weld per spool). Final hardness shall not exceed 200 Brinell. Carbon steel SMAW welds may be exempted from testing if made with a brand and type of electrode approved by the Company in writing.

- For carbon-molybdenum steels, at least 10% of all welds and hot bends shall be hardness tested (one test per weld and hot-formed bend and at least one test per spool. Final hardness of welds and hot-formed bends shall not exceed 215 Brinell.

- For chromium-molybdenum steels, a minimum of 10% of welds and of hot-formed and hot-bent materials in each heat treatment batch that are furnace heat-treated, and 100% of those that are locally heat-treated shall be hardness-tested (one per weld and bend). Final hardness of welds and hot bends shall not exceed 215 Brinell.

## 624 PWHT of Austenitic (300-Series) Stainless Steels

Company practice is to require stress relief of 300-series stainless steel piping, unless otherwise specified, to reduce the susceptibility of stainless steel to stress corrosion cracking (SCC). Cracking of stainless steel is primarily due to chlorides. Chlorides can be present in the process stream resulting in internal SCC, or may be present in air, wash water, or insulation, resulting in external SCC. Considerable cost savings can be realized by waiving the stress relief requirement for services that are unlikely to promote cracking; however, a case-by-case review of specific services is needed.

The decision tree in Figure 600-4 summarizes Company guidelines on the stress relief of 300-series stainless steel piping. Where stress relief is required, stress relieve all 2" nominal pipe size (NPS) and larger butt welds. For piping less than 2" NPS, stress relieve all welds up to and including block valve.

Heat treatment shall be done at 1550°F to 1650°F for 1 hr/inch of nominal wall thickness, but for not less than 1 hr. Cooling from heat treatment temperature shall be in still air with the heat source removed.

### Intergranular Corrosion Concerns

Types 304, 316, and 317 stainless steel are unstabilized grades of 300-series stainless steels. Their specifications allow carbon contents up to 0.08%, so they are considered non-L grades. L-grades (low carbon grades) are limited to 0.03% carbon. Since non-L grades will suffer severe sensitization during welding and stress relief, L-grades or stabilized grades should be used when welding is involved.

Stabilized grades such as Types 321 and 347, are typically used when operating temperatures exceed 700°F, since they are more resistant to sensitization than the L-grades. The stabilized grades also retain more strength than the L-grades above 850°F. Refer to Section 310 of the *Corrosion Prevention Manual* for more information on the effects of stabilization and low carbon on sensitization resistance.

Stress relief of all grades of 300-series stainless steel piping should be waived for services where intergranular corrosion is of greater concern than SCC (such as acid service). In such services L-grades should be used with no stress relief, to minimize sensitization and susceptibility to intergranular corrosion attack. Types 304 and 316 stainless steel have been historically used in chemical plants. These alloys should not be stress relieved because the heat treatment would cause sensitization.

### High Pressure Hydrogen Service

Stress relief should be done in high pressure hydrogen service (greater than 100 psia hydrogen partial pressure) due to the consequences of a leak. Examples of high pressure hydrogen service include hydrotreaters, catalytic reforming units, and hydrogen manufacturing plants.

### Internal Stress Corrosion Cracking

Stress relief should be done for known stress corrosion cracking environments when temperatures are above 140°F, including aqueous streams with greater than 50 ppm

# 630 Piping Fabrication

## 631 Fabrication of Piping Spools

Information required for fabrication of piping spools includes:

- Piping design and materials, as defined by isometric drawings (often CAD-produced) and Piping Class Sheets (see the Corporation Piping Specification in Volume 2 of this manual). The fabricator or contractor may choose to make individual spool drawings in his format and system
- Fabrication specifications appropriate for the material category:
    - PIM-MS-2505 for carbon steel piping
    - PIM-MS-4770 for stainless steel piping
    - PIM-MS-4772 for alloy steel piping

    See Volume II of this manual for guidelines on the use of these specifications.

    The purchase order list sheets or supplemental specifications, or the plant construction specifications may give particular requirements for a project that modify or supplement the standard specifications. Also, when appropriate, include specifications for radiographic inspection and painting.

---

- Dimensional limits for individual spools dictated by shipping or handling restrictions, if the fabrication shop is not on the job site. Spools longer than 40 feet are difficult to move by truck

- Required production schedule, indicating priorities (if any) for certain groups of spools. Scheduling is important:

    - Spools hung on a particular piece of equipment (column, vessel, pump) must be on site when the equipment is set. Thus, equipment delivery timing may set the priorities for spool production

    - All spools for a run of piping should be on hand, so they can be set sequentially for an efficient erection program

When spools are fabricated by a plant construction contractor, dimensional limits and spool production schedule are the contractor's responsibility but, to avoid delays, should be monitored by Company representatives to assure that the contractor has a satisfactory spool fabrication program.

## 632 Spools for Lined Piping

Design of spools for internally lined pipe should take into account the method of joining pipe, fittings, flanges, and individual spool pieces, and also provide a feasible procedure for modifying spools in the field.

- Welded or flanged joints can be used for cement-lined piping; see Section 400 of this manual and Section 300 of the *Pipeline Manual*

- Plastic-lined piping requires flanged pipe sections (fabricated to length) and fittings; see Section 200 of the *Coatings Manual*, and Section 400 of this manual

- Fusion-bonded epoxy-lined piping requires cleaning and coating of the internal surface at girth welds. Where this cannot be done, flanged or other mechanical joints must be used. See Section 200 of the *Coatings Manual*; the internal crawlers described there are suitable for straight pipe, NPS 8 and larger

## 633 Field Fitup of Spools

Piping isometric drawings are based on exact drawing dimensions for location of piping connections and line routing. In reality, the accumulation of construction tolerances often results in deviations from the drawing dimensions, and provision must be made for field fitup of piping in all three dimensions. The flexibility of smaller sizes or long runs provides some adjustment allowance for fitup, but where piping between fixed connections is relatively stiff, random allowances for field fitup must be provided at suitable places. In this way, the pipe can be cut and trimmed to fit and a field weld made without straining or deforming the pipe, and with minimal forces at the connections to equipment.

---

If adjustments must be made for satisfactory fitup, but provision has not been allowed for fitup field welds, the alternative is to cut the spool as needed to achieve proper fitup; this may be expensive.

A minimum distance between butt welds of 6 times the pipe wall thickness, or 2 inches, whichever is greater, is recommended.

Before bolting up flanged connections, flange faces must be parallel within 1/16 inch per foot measured across any diameter; flange bolt holes must have less than 1/8 inch maximum offset. This conforms to ASME/ANSI B31.3, Section 335.1.1(c).

## 640 Pressure Testing of Piping Systems

### 641 General

Proof testing of completed piping systems is required by ASME/ANSI B31.3 and Company Practice. Small pressure vessels and similar equipment are often included in the pressure test systems even though such equipment has already been strength tested to Code requirements in the shop (except for the cases of field fabrication, alterations, and repairs). Proof testing of completed systems tests the assembled system for strength and leakage.

ASME/ANSI B31.3 Section 345 covers testing of piping. Code testing of equipment, if performed in conjunction with field testing of piping systems, must also meet the requirements of the appropriate specifications and code (ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code Section VIII, UG-99 to UG-101, for example, cover testing of pressure vessels). Guidelines for field testing are given in the *Pressure Vessel, Fired Heater and Waste Heat Recovery*, and *Heat Exchanger and Cooling Tower Manuals*.

A piping system should be tested:

- Before initial operation

- After alterations or repairs that affect its strength. Alternatives such as increased nondestructive examination may be substituted after appropriate approval

- When rerated for higher pressure or temperature service, such that previous tests do not satisfy Code requirements for the rerated conditions

Hydrostatic testing is done with a liquid, normally water. See Section 642. Service testing of low-pressure (not exceeding 150 psig) utility lines with their normal operating fluid (air, water, steam) is permitted. See Section 643. Pneumatic testing with air or inert gas may be necessary for large lines designed for gas or vapor service where the weight of water would overstress the piping or pipe supports, or for refractory-lined piping. See Section 644.

---

ASME/ANSI B31.3 allows an **alternative leak test** when both hydrostatic and pneumatic testing are impractical. See Code Sections 345.1(c), 345.8, and 345.9.

For completion testing of pipelines under ASME/ANSI Codes B31.4 and B31.8, see the *Pipeline Manual*, Section 700.

## 642 Hydrostatic Testing of Piping Systems

A test program should normally be prepared by the design organization, using Standard Form PIM-EF-121, Field Hydrotest Schedule, accompanied by test diagrams (markups of Piping and Instrument Diagrams). These diagrams define the separate systems for test and are coded to designate the test pressures.

Assembled piping systems are often tested with connected small pressure vessels and similar equipment to simplify testing procedures and minimize testing time and construction interference. Form PIM-EF-121 provides for test data for drums, vessels, columns, furnaces and heat exchangers, as well as for piping systems. For a small plant, however, it may be possible to fully specify testing in a few paragraphs in the construction specifications.

Model Specification PIM-MS-3541, Field Hydrostatic Testing of Piping Systems, should be used in conjunction with the Field Hydrotest Schedule. See Section 3 of this specification for limitations on use of valves to isolate instruments, equipment, and sections of piping having different test pressures.

When a system contains one or more subsystems at different test pressures, it may save testing time to first test the subsystem subject to the highest test pressure, then those with progressively lower test pressures, each together with the previously tested subsystems. Some piping and equipment is tested more than once, but filling and draining time and test equipment setup time is saved.

### Determining Test Pressure

ASME/ANSI B31.3 Section 345.4.2 specifies the piping hydrostatic test pressure at any point in the piping system to be not less than 1.5 times the design pressure. When the design temperature is above the test temperature, the minimum test pressure P_T is:

$$P_T = 1.5P \left(\frac{S_T}{S}\right)$$

where:

- P = design pressure, psi
- $S_T$ = stress value at test temperature, psi
- S = stress value at design temperature

$S_T$ and S are basic allowable stresses per Table A-1 of ASME/ANSI B31.3. The value of $S_T$/S shall not exceed 6.5. If testing at P_T would produce a hoop stress in excess of the specified minimum yield strength (SMYS) at test temperature, the test pressure must be reduced so as not to exceed the SMYS at test temperature.

---

Although ASME/ANSI B31.3 Section 345.4.3(b) permits a reduced piping test pressure where it is not practicable to isolate the piping from a vessel and where a vessel test pressure is not less than 77% of the calculated piping test pressure, it is preferable to design the facility and arrange testing so that the piping can be tested at 1.5 times the design pressure.

The Company prefers to test a piping system at the test pressure for the flanges per ANSI Specification B16.5 as long as this does not overstress the pipe or piping components. However, if test pressures for pipe, valves, expansion joints or other components in the system are lower, the test pressure should be 1.5 times the cold working pressure rating for the limiting components, but not less than 1.5 times the system design pressure. ASME/ANSI B31.3 Section 345.3.3 has guidelines on testing expansion joints.

ASME/ANSI B31.3 Section 345.2.4 requires externally pressured piping (vacuum piping) to be tested at an internal pressure of 1.5 times the external differential pressure, but not less than 15 psi.

## Testing Procedure and Precautions

All welds, joints, and connections should be left exposed (no paint, coating, or insulation) for examination during hydrostatic testing. The test pressure should be held for at least 10 minutes and maintained as long as needed to make a thorough examination of the system. Once tested and accepted, welds and joints may be insulated or covered.

Temperature of the test fluid and, thus, the metal temperature of piping and equipment, should be safely above the ductile-brittle transition temperature (or any metal component of the system). Section 400 of the *Pressure Vessel Manual* gives guidance on the minimum pressurizing temperature (MPT); see also ASME Section VIII, Figure UCS 66.

If the pressure test is to be maintained for a long period of time and the test fluid is subject to thermal expansion, precautions must be taken to avoid excessive pressure buildup by providing a relief valve set at 105% of the test pressure and assuring that the relief valve is never blocked off from the system under test. Most Company field organizations require a relief valve on the test pump discharge regardless of holding time. This is a good general practice.

## Testing Through Equipment

Some guidelines for testing through equipment are as follows.

- **Pressure Vessels.** Vessel strength test pressures are available from design drawings but should be checked against manufacturer's data reports. These pressure limits should be listed on the Field Hydrotest Schedule.

- **Furnaces.** The limiting hydrotest pressure for furnaces should be the maximum pressure permitted by the weakest component (tubes, flanges, headers or extrusions of the furnace coil). This test pressure should be verified against the manufacturer's test pressure

---

- **Heat Exchangers.** Shop code test pressures should be obtained from the manufacturer's data reports. Also, exchangers are usually retested after maintenance and retested if corroded. Hydrostatic testing of a heat exchanger whose design differential pressure is less than the pressure rating of either side must take into account the allowable differential pressure. Upon releasing the test pressure from such a system, the pressure on one side of the tube sheet drops faster than on the other side. If the resulting differential pressure becomes large enough, the tube sheet or another common part may fail. The pressure must be released slowly enough to avoid a large momentary differential pressure

- **Steam Service Coils.** Steam service coils that qualify as boiler code piping should be hydrostatically tested in accordance with the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code and ASME/ANSI B31.1, Power Piping.

- **Equipment Supports.** Adequacy of equipment supports, including column and vessel skirts, should be checked if designs are not based on water-filled contents

- **Mechanical Equipment.** It is not good practice to hydrotest through pumps, compressors, and other mechanical equipment.

## 643 Service Testing of Low Pressure Utility Systems

Utility piping within ASME/ANSI B31.3 Category D fluid service (nonflammable, nontoxic fluid not exceeding 150 psig, with a temperature from -20°F to 366°F) may be tested with the service fluid. Pressure should be gradually increased in steps, holding the pressure at each step long enough to equalize piping strains. For piping in gas or vapor service, a preliminary check for leaks should be made at 15 psig for lines NPS 14 and larger, and not more than 25 psig for other lines.

Utility piping designed for pressures over 150 psig, including nominal 150-lb steam lines, must be hydrostatically tested as described in Section 642.

## 644 Pneumatic Testing

Pneumatic testing involves the hazard of a sudden release of the energy stored in the compressed gas. It should be avoided except where conditions do not allow hydrostatic testing, for instance, large relief lines that cannot withstand loading when water-filled, and refractory-lined piping. A testing specification with safety procedures should be prepared for each line to be pneumatically tested. Company practice requires approval by the designated level of local operating management, as well as by the engineering manager for the project.

To minimize the chance of brittle failure during a pneumatic test, the testing should only be made well above the metal ductile-brittle transition temperature. Pneumatic testing should not be done on nonmetallic piping systems.

The procedure for pneumatic testing should have the following provisions:

- A test pressure limit of 110% of design pressure

---

- A pressure relief device set no higher than the test pressure plus either 10% of the test pressure or 50 psi, whichever is less

- Test fluid consisting of air or inert gas

- Using soap solution or equivalent liquid, a preliminary examination for leaks at 25 psig or one-half the test pressure, whichever is lower, of all weld seams, joints and connections

- Subsequently, the pressure is gradually increased in steps until the test pressure is reached. Pressure is held at each step long enough to equalize piping strains

- Pressure shall be reduced from the test pressure slowly to design pressure before examining for leakage at weld seams, joints and connections

To avoid creating an explosive hydrocarbon-air mixture, air must not be used as a test media for a system that has been in hydrocarbon service without first properly cleaning and purging the system in order.

Because static buildup in solid CO₂ particles in the discharge jet is a source of ignition, carbon dioxide from a high-pressure source should not be discharged into a flammable atmosphere.

If steam or other condensible fluid is used as the test fluid, precautions shall be taken to avoid condensation before system depressurization. Steam tests should only be performed on equipment verified as adequate for vacuum.

Large relief systems designed to operate at low pressures—less than 15 psig—are often tested for leaks with air or steam. Testing must conform with the above precautions and restrictions.

## 645 Corrosion or Contamination by Test Fluids

Clean potable water is normally specified as the test fluid. Precautions include:

- For chrome-nickel 300 Series austenitic stainless steel, chloride content of the water should be less than 100 ppm to avoid chloride stress corrosion cracking. For 300 Series stainless steels other than 321 and 347 that have been in service above 700°F, the test fluid should be a 1½% soda ash solution to prevent polythionic stress corrosion of the sensitized steel

- For carbon steel piping in 98% sulfuric acid service, where severe corrosion would result from diluting the concentrated acid with any water left in the piping, diesel oil, kerosene or another clean hydrocarbon with a flash point above 120°F can be used. Extreme caution should be exercised to prevent spills or fires when using hydrocarbons as the test fluid. Wherever feasible, it is better to test with water, then drain and dry the system with dehydrated air

- Use of antifreeze solutions, or brackish or salt water should be discussed with specialists of the Materials and Equipment Engineering Unit of CRTC to determine that these fluids, or residues from them, will not be detrimental to the

---

equipment and piping (including valve seals and packing), the process, or to any catalyst that may be used in the process

After hydrotesting, piping and equipment should be drained as completely as is practical. For stainless steel, it is critical to sponge or blow with air and dry well to avoid the buildup of deposits that could promote stress corrosion cracking.

## 650 Assembly and Installation: Special Techniques

### 651 Field Layout of Small Piping

Whenever possible, small piping should be designed and laid out with the major piping and included on the drawings, isometrics, and models. Doing so will usually give lower overall cost and less confusion in the field. However, many contractors will leave the routing of lines and layout of manifolds for small piping (NPS 1½ and smaller) to be handled in the field. Therefore, construction contractor field forces should be given clear instructions regarding requirements for valve access, and clearances for walkways and equipment access (including removal and replacement of equipment); see the *Safety in Designs Manual*.

Pump auxiliary piping, cooling water piping, steam trap manifolds, steam tracing, seal oil piping, etc., should not block access for removal from their foundations of pumps, compressors, drivers, exchangers and similar equipment. Where this is not practical, the piping should be readily removable to provide access without cutting. Pump auxiliary piping should allow access to drive couplings from both sides of the pump and to bearings, packings and seals without pipe or tubing interference.

Small piping and electrical conduits must not be attached to the top or sides of handrails (1½-inch minimum clearance must be maintained around handrails).

---

Failure to properly route small piping will result in costly rework.

## 652 Small Piping Connections Subject to Vibration or in High-Pressure Service

Small piping connections such as vents, drains, instrument connections, auxiliary piping, etc., subject to vibration at equipment or on larger piping must be designed to prevent failure. Construction forces must ensure that the design purpose is achieved.

Vibrations can arise from:

- Cyclic pressures from reciprocating compressors and pumps
- High frequency vibrations at centrifugal pumps and compressors, and high-energy dissipation at control valves and relief valves

To avoid fatigue failure, designs for these connections should have:

- Short, heavy-wall pipe nipples, or equivalent, with bridge welding between the first valve and the equipment or larger piping; see Standard Drawing GD-L1057. Minimum recommended connection size is NPS 1; NPS ¾ is the minimum accepted by some organizations
- Sturdy, lightweight root valves, as appropriate for the service: see Standard Drawing GD-L1057. For instrument connections, suggested valves include Kerotest 110N or 200N, Dragon 10F, or reduced port Rockwell (Edward) Univalve
- Adequate bracing of the root valve and downstream piping or instruments

## 653 Socket Welding and Seal Welding of Fittings and Valves

Heat produced by socket welding can generate thermal expansion stresses that may cause cracking in the weld. As a general practice, there should be a nominal 1/16-inch clearance between the end of the pipe and the bottom of the fitting recess, as shown in ASME/ANSI B31.3 Figures 328.5.2 B and C. This is achieved by pulling the pipe back 1/16 inch after contacting the bottom of the recess. This procedure is a prudent precautionary measure for carbon steel piping. For stainless and alloy materials, and for lines in high temperature service (over 800°F), it is critical.

Heat produced by socket welding or seal welding at connections to valves may distort seating faces so that it is difficult to get tight valve shutoff in service. To minimize this risk, gate and globe valves should be closed and then backed open a half turn for welding. Prevent overheating of valve packing, gaskets and seal rings if weld heat input is high or if special materials for packing, gaskets or seal rings are not suitable for usual service maximums (650°F to 800°F); see manufacturer's catalog and installation instructions.

## 654 Pipe Thread Compounds

Thread compounds should not contain lead or copper. Thread compound or Teflon tape should not be used on screwed connections that will be seal-welded, because these contaminate the weld metal and produce defective welds. Recommended compounds for carbon steel and stainless steel screwed connections in services other than drinking water and instrument air are:

- Dow Corning "Molykote G-N"
- Cooper Cameron "Key-Tite Pipe Joint Compound"
- Oil Center Research "Liquid-O-Ring #404"

For drinking water and instrument air use:

- Federal Process Company "Thread Sealant JC30"
- Garlock "Type A Pipe Thread Compound"

Use of Teflon tape is not recommended on instrument air piping, because there is a risk that loose pieces will plug small downstream connections. If used, the tape should not cover the first two threads at the end of the pipe.

## 655 Bolting-up Practice

Flange faces, and grooves on ring and tongue-and-groove flanges, should be thoroughly cleaned with a wire brush, with care taken not to damage the surface finish. Nonmetallic brushes should be used on stainless steel flanges. After cleaning, flange faces and grooves should be inspected for pits and defects.

Bolts, studs and nuts must be of materials specified in design drawings and specifications. Bolts and studs should be long enough so that the nuts are fully engaged, with at least one full thread exposed at each end. Bolts, studs and nuts should be cleaned and lubricated with an approved thread paste. Lubrication of the contact surface between nut and flange prevents seizure when tightening the nut. Suggested lubricant is Bostik, Inc. "Never-Seez Anti-seize and Lubricating Compound." (This lubricant has also been used to coat valve packing gland bolts and valve stems.)

Gaskets should be inserted dry, and are normally self-centered by the flange bolts. Metal-jacketed gaskets for tongue-and-groove or male-and-female face joints should be placed with the seam in the bottom of the groove or on the bottom of the female facing.

When flanges are in close horizontal and vertical alignment and the faces are parallel, all bolts are installed except one or two and the nuts tightened hand-tight. A spud bar is inserted in one of the holes left open and used as a lever to raise or lower either flange to achieve proper alignment. Ensure that the tongue enters the groove on tongue-and-groove flanges or that the ring seats into the groove on ring joints; tighten one bolt sufficiently to keep the tongue and groove engaged (or the ring seated) and then tighten the opposite bolt a like amount. This will result in a parallel facement. Remove the spud bar and install the remaining bolts, tightening opposites and gradually working around the flange until all bolts are tight, with equal tension

---

on each bolt. With spiral-wound gaskets, the flanges should be evenly seated on the gasket centering ring.

If carbon steel flanges are in alignment but the faces are not parallel, and if a run of line sufficient for deflection exists on either side of the flanges, the first bolts to be tightened should be on the side of the flange where the greatest opening exists. When flanges are drawn up parallel, all bolts can be tightened to the same approximate degree of tension, and then gradually increased around the bolt circle until all are tight.

If the flanges cannot be drawn parallel without severely straining the bolts, the pipe, if carbon steel, may be heated to a temperature sufficient for bending. At this temperature bolt the flanges together as outlined above, then, if the piping class requires, PWHT to between 1100°F and 1200°F. After the pipe has been allowed to cool, unbolt the flanges for inspection and if strain still exists repeat the procedure. This procedure should not be used for alloy or stainless steels without advice from Company metallurgy specialists.

If lines cannot be lined up without excessive strain or by heating the pipe, the piping must be cut and rewelded so that flange buildup is possible without strain. Flange misalignment at machinery must always be corrected to avoid strain on the machinery. (Piping design should provide adequate flexibility to prevent excessive forces on equipment under high temperature operating conditions.)

Standard length wrenches should be used. If hammer wrenches are used, ten-pound hammers are the heaviest permissible.

Good practice for tightening bolted flanges is to draw up progressively on bolts in the 12 o'clock, 6 o'clock, 9 o'clock, and 3 o'clock positions, progressing in this fashion around the flange until all bolts are brought up snugly to ensure sufficient compression to seat the gasket. The larger the flange the more important this procedure becomes. When using stud bolts, ensure all threads on each nut are fully engaged.

Flanged joints in high-pressure or high-temperature services often require precision loading of studs, and special tools must be used. Torque wrenches are available with various torque ranges (foot-pounds). In some cases bolt tensioners are used to stretch the bolts or studs to obtain required bolt tension, such as Biach Tensioners. Use of an extensometer, such as the ultrasonic Maintenance Monitor Model 633, is recommended for accurate control.

For more complete guidelines on bolting up large piping flanges and equipment flanges, see the *Heat Exchanger and Cooling Tower Manual* Sections 532 and 1000, which also discuss bolting equipment usage and development of bolting procedures.

See Figures 600-5 and 600-6 of this manual for recommended bolt torques and stresses.

## 656 Vents in Reinforcing Saddles at Branch Connections

Where reinforcing saddles are used at branch connections, a 1/8-inch minimum diameter vent hole should be provided in the saddle to reveal any leakage during hydrotesting in the weld between the branch and main run and to provide venting during welding and heat treating operations. Plug the vent hole after hydrostatic testing with a stiff grease sealant to prevent moisture from entering.

## 657 Nonconductive Insulating Flange and Joint Assemblies

Insulating flanges or insulated joint assemblies are used to electrically isolate sections of cathodically protected underground piping from aboveground piping systems, and to separate pipe of different materials where galvanic action could cause corrosion. The nonconductive assemblies are normally specified and indicated on design drawings, but field engineers should know their purpose.

The nonconductive gasket, sleeves and washers should not be painted, since the paint film might be of sufficiently low resistance to allow current across the insulating flange. Similarly, dust and dirt settling between flange faces can affect the insulating effectiveness. This can be prevented by wrapping nonconductive tape around the flange circumferences to cover the gap between flange faces.

## 658 Cleanup of Piping

Internal pipe cleaning is needed to remove dirt, debris, weld spatter, and foreign material from the piping. Usually cleaning of process and utility piping is done by the operating organization as part of precommissioning, often with assistance of a construction contractor crew. Cleaning methods available are:

- Chemical cleaning (see Section 660)
- Oil flushing
- Steam blowing
- Mechanical cleaning and pigging
- Water flushing
- Air blowing

The first four methods will usually be specified by the design organization for particular systems, dependent on service requirements. The following guidelines pertain to these methods:

- Purchase order specifications for pipe and piping components in systems requiring chemical cleaning should specifically state that no internal coatings (lacquer, oil) be applied

- To protect the compressor valves, suction lines to reciprocating compressors should be cleaned by abrasive blasting or pickling. See Section 660

- Lube and seal oil systems for centrifugal compressors require extreme cleanliness; see Model Specification MAC-MS-2983. Avoid socket-welded and screwed connections

- Water velocity should be somewhat higher than the design flowrate. Velocities from 8 to 12 feet/second are suggested for lines up to 10 inches in diameter. - Instrument and utility air lines

---

- Large off-plot blowdown, flare and relief lines, and any other vapor service lines that are not designed to support a full hydrotest load

- Lines upstream of catalyst-packed vessels

- Large diameter water lines in which water flush velocity is insufficient to properly clean the system

- Halon system lines

The most effective means of air blowing is the surge method, in which pressurized air is stored in vessels and piping upstream of the controlling point of the blow. This air is then released as quickly as possible into the section of piping to be blown. This method requires quick-opening valves and takes full advantage of the large pressure differential between the pressurized and atmospheric sections of a piping system.

In addition to being very loud, high velocity air releases may contain hazardous flying debris. Piping should be secured so that the jet thrust generated during blowing is not hazardous to personnel or equipment. Prior to air blowing, control valves, globe and check valves, flow meters, orifice plates, transmitters and other instruments or equipment that may either be damaged by air blowing or interfere with the performance of the air blow should be removed, isolated or bypassed. Pipe and vessels that have not been pressure-tested should not be used to store pressurized air.

Strainers should be provided at pump and machinery inlets and ahead of other equipment (vessels, etc.) from which removal of debris accumulated during cleanup is not practical. Permanent process strainers with properly sized mesh for line cleanup can be utilized, and temporary strainers should be installed at other locations. The maximum particle size that a particular machinery item can safely handle without damage should be considered when specifying screen mesh size. Strainers are described in Section 290.

After cleanup, ferrous piping systems that will not promptly be put in service should be properly protected against corrosion. Methods should be suited to the particular pipe material (including valve packing and elastomers), operating service, and length of time before going into service. These methods include:

- Water fill (oxygen scavenger and/or biocide is rarely used)
- Dry air or nitrogen fill
- Oil fill

Corrosion specialists in the Materials and Equipment Engineering Unit of CRTC can advise on corrosion protection of piping and connected equipment awaiting entry into service.

## 660 Descaling, Cleaning, and Pickling

### 661 What These Terms Mean

The terms descaling, cleaning, and pickling refer to procedures for removing oil, rust and mill scale from the interior of piping.

### 662 When Cleaning Is Necessary

Most piping contains internal oil, rust, weld spatter, and mill scale when it is first placed in service. Cleaning prior to startup generally involves nothing more than liquid circulation and flushing to remove loose debris. However, there are services in which this degree of cleanliness is inadequate. Lube and seal oil systems for machinery, and compressor suction lines, for example, must be scrupulously clean to protect downstream equipment from damage by solid particles. There are also occasional fluid services where product purity requirements demand more than the usual cleanliness.

Older lines may also require periodic cleaning to reduce excessive pressure drop resulting from scale accumulation during service. Examples are closed cooling systems with untreated makeup water, and boiler feedwater piping. This work is often deferred until the entire system needs cleaning.

### 663 How Cleaning Is Accomplished

By far the most common technique for internally cleaning piping is chemical cleaning. Abrasive blasting is also used occasionally. Another method is called vapor phase descaling, a technique in which the Company has little experience.

Chemical cleaning dissolves the rust via either an acid solution or a chelating agent. In either case, a corrosion inhibitor must be added to the solution to minimize corrosion of the metal being cleaned.

---

Chemical cleaning is normally done by a specialist contractor using proven methods, experienced personnel, and equipment designed for the purpose. Chemical cleaning typically involves several steps:

1. The metal is degreased with a hot alkaline solution. Oil or grease prevent the acid from contacting the rust and mill scale, so they must be washed out before the actual scale removal can begin.

2. A warm solution of inhibited acid is circulated for several hours to dissolve the rust and scale. Acids commonly used include hydrochloric and citric.

3. The acid solution is dumped, the system is rinsed with water and final traces of acid are neutralized with a dilute alkali such as sodium carbonate (soda ash).

4. The final step is a passivation treatment, usually involving either phosphoric acid or a phosphate. Freshly cleaned steel will rerust rapidly. To prevent this, the passivation treatment forms an iron phosphate surface layer on the metal that inhibits the rerusting. Because water will destroy the passive film and cause rusting, the piping should not be rinsed with water after being passivated.

Chelating agents are often used in place of acids for rust removal. They function by chemically complexing the rust rather than actually dissolving it. Chelating agents work more slowly and are generally less effective in removing heavy rust scale. However, they are easier to use because they save several steps in the cleaning procedure.

Before using a chelating agent the piping is degreased. It is then filled with an acidic chelating agent, which is circulated. Next alkali, usually ammonia, is added to the solution to bring the pH to near-neutral. Circulation is continued. This final circulation accomplishes neutralization and passivation in one step. It isn't necessary to follow it with the phosphate passivation from step 4 for acid cleaning. A similar technique uses citric acid and ammonia, and also eliminates the need for subsequent passivation.

Which of the two procedures to use is usually decided by the chemical cleaning contractor. This is a safe approach when using a contractor with an established local reputation, because our cleaning specifications are basically performance specs.

A conventional acid-cleaning procedure is typified by use of a strong acid, such as hydrochloric, for the cleaning, with phosphoric acid or a phosphate for passivation. Chelating can be recognized by the trade name of the chelating agent; "Vertan" is the most common.

## 664 References

The *Corrosion Prevention Manual* contains a detailed discussion of the fundamentals and personnel safety requirements of chemical cleaning. It is worth reading before attempting to specify, contract for, or supervise the cleaning of a piping system.

---

Model Specification MAC-MS-2983, Cleaning Lube and Seal Oil Systems, gives a step-by-step procedure for field inspection, cleaning, assembly, flushing and inspection of compressor lube oil and seal oil systems.

Model Specification PIM-MS-2411, Descaling and Cleaning Steel Piping, is a general specification for the chemical cleaning of piping systems. It supplements MAC-MS-2983 but can also stand alone when necessary for cleaning other than compressor lube and seal oil piping.

## 665 Cleanliness Standards

Specifications MAC-MS-2983 and PIM-MS-2411 provide different degrees of cleanliness for steel surfaces. MAC-MS-2983 requires that the cleaned surface be a uniform gray-white in color, free from any visible rust, scale, paint, or other contaminants. PIM-MS-2411 requires conformance to the Steel Structures Painting Council (SSPC) standard SP-8 for piping cleaned by acid pickling and to SSPC SP-6 for piping cleaned by abrasive blasting. Because SP-6 allows very small quantities of tightly adhering rust, blasting to PIM-MS-2411 is not as strict as MAC-MS-2983. Section 300 of the *Coatings Manual* discusses common surface preparation standards such as SP-6.

It is important that the contractor understand the performance standards to be used on the job. The cleaning method chosen must be capable of achieving these standards. The SSPC standards are generally recognized throughout the industry and can minimize debate as to whether the completed cleaning job is satisfactory or not.

## 666 Additional Requirements

The best way to obtain a thoroughly clean system is to clean and passivate all component parts prior to fabrication. The parts should then be wrapped in plastic to help preserve the passivation. Subsequent fabrication should be done in a clean, dry environment. For best results, MIG or TIG welding should be used for root passes. Hydrotesting should be done with oil—not water—to preserve the passive film.

If the piping is cleaned after fabrication rather than before, hydrotesting should be done before the chemical cleaning to avoid rerusting the surface.

## 671 Primary Cautions During Hot Tapping

Following are three principal safety concerns relating to both hot taps and repair welding on pressurized lines or vessels [1-8]. References, in brackets, are given in Section 679. See Section 340 of the *Welding Manual* for more detailed information regarding the welding concerns associated with hot tap welding and recommendations for selecting welding procedures appropriate for the carbon equivalent of the piping material and weld cooling rate as a function of wall thickness and pipe contents.

**Burn-through.** A local burn-through (blowout) at the time of welding occurs because the heat of welding reduces the capacity of the pipe to contain pressure. A burn-through permits leakage of the pressurized contents, which is dangerous in itself. In addition, the contents may be flammable or toxic. A burn-through usually requires shutdown of the line for repair. Catastrophic rupture is not likely [2].

Published test data shows that limiting hot taps to a wall thickness of 0.188 inch and above is conservative for welding done with low-hydrogen electrodes (Exx18 or Exx16). It is not conservative for welds made with cellulosic electrodes (Exx10) [3] [7] [8].

**Weld Crack.** A weld crack on the run or branch line is caused by an underbead (or toe) crack in the weld heat-affected zone (HAZ). This type of weld crack is a result of hydrogen from the welding process and occurs at the hard zone in the weld heat affected zone. This type of crack will occur when the weld cools to ambient temperature, and may not occur until the next day.

**Thermal Degradation of the Contents.** Thermal degradation of the pipe contents can result in undesirable chemical and physical reactions, accelerated corrosion, or environmental cracking of the steel.

## 672 Fixed Parameters

It is vital to have a full understanding of line conditions that may be encountered during a hot tap. Those conditions cannot readily be changed and may determine how or when a hot tap can be undertaken or otherwise influence the success of the procedure.

### General Conditions

**Internal Linings.** Lines or vessels lined with cladding or with glass, cement, lead, refractory, plastic or strip linings, should not be hot tapped.

**Line Selection.** Be sure to select the right line. There have been many instances, particularly with buried lines, where the wrong line has been tapped.

**Encased Lines.** Where line is encased, make sure that the main line and not the casing is hot tapped. The system might have to be shut down if the casing is hot tapped and the drill penetrates the main line.

---

## Suitability of Line Contents for Hot Tapping

Hot taps are **routinely made** on lines containing water, nitrogen, steam, natural gas, crude oil, and petroleum products. Hot taps that involve welding fittings on the line **should not be made** on lines containing oxygen, chlorine, compressed air, acids, or nitrates. Welding on lines containing these products can result in explosive reactions or weld cracks. Saddle attachments are available from the Pipeline Development Company (Plido) that permit a flange or coupling to be fastened to the line without welding. See Section 675.

Certain line contents require **special consideration**. Successful hot taps involving welded fittings on lines containing caustic, sour water or hydrocarbons, high pressure hydrogen, or ethylene and unsaturated hydrocarbons; and on LPG or refrigeration lines require special consideration.

**Sour Service.** Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) corrosion of carbon and low alloy steels can charge hydrogen into the steel. Hot tap welds can have hard heat-affected zones that are susceptible to this sulfide stress cracking in a sour environment. Sulfide stress cracking can be avoided by either controlling the weld procedure to avoid hard welds, or by avoiding hot taps on lines defined as sour by NACE Standard MR-01-75, Sulfide Stress Cracking Resistant Metallic Material for Oil Field Equipment. The weld procedure hardness can be controlled by preheating to 200°F (when possible) and by using a temper bead welding sequence (see Section 673).

Sour crude pipelines are routinely hot tapped without special precautions during welding. However, sulfide cracking of these welds has not been a problem, presumably because the line does not undergo significant sour corrosion. Wet sour gas and sour water lines, on the other hand, can undergo significant sour corrosion and hydrogen charging. Therefore, weld hardness should be controlled to avoid sulfide cracking.

**Caustic.** High concentrations of hot caustic can cause stress corrosion cracking of welds that have not been postweld heat treated. To avoid stress corrosion cracking in caustic service, carbon and low alloy steel should be PWHT'd if the line is heat traced, or if the operating temperature is above 140°F with caustic concentration less than 30%; or above 110°F with caustic concentration above 30%. Welds on caustic lines should not be made if PWHT would be required even if the caustic concentration does not require PWHT. On thin lines at no flow, the heat of welding could cause a local increase in the caustic concentration, which could result in caustic cracks.

Ethylene and unsaturated hydrocarbons can undergo self-sustaining decomposition initiated by welding at high internal temperatures and pressures. The temperature/pressure limits for hot tap and repair welding on ethylene lines are available in published sources [10, 11]. The oxygen content of the ethylene should be limited to 1000 ppm.

**Low Temperature.** When hot-tap and repair welding on steel lines operating below 32°F, such as LPG and refrigeration lines, condensation and icing are likely. The risk of weld cracking is higher because of this moisture, and attempts to preheat the

---

weld area may be ineffective. The Materials and Equipment Engineering Unit of CRTC should be consulted.

## Pipe Wall Thickness

The risks of burn-through and weld cracking resulting from hot tapping vary for different pipe wall thicknesses, as follows:

**Very Thin Wall (0.172 inch and less).** The risk of burn-through is significant; hydrogen cracking is secondary. Fluid flow conditions greatly influence both. Most Company locations prohibit hot tapping of pipes in this thickness range. Risk of burn-through (and pipe wall distortion) can be substantially reduced by maintaining flow. Welding heat input and pressure conditions must be carefully controlled. Consult the CRTC Materials and Equipment Engineering Unit.

**Thin Wall (0.188 inch to 0.250 inch).** The risk of burn-through is limited, that of weld cracking significant. The welding heat input should be maximized and flow reduced or stopped to minimize the risk of cracking.

**Intermediate Wall Thickness (0.25 inch to 0.500 inch).** There is no risk of burn-through. To minimize the risk of cracking, heat input should be maximized and the flow reduced or stopped.

**Thick Wall (greater than 0.500 inch).** Burn-through is not possible. Weld cracking susceptibility is independent of the fluid heat capacity, since the heat sink created by the material itself controls weldability. The risk of cracking increases with thickness.

## Equipment Limitations

Hot tap equipment is typically limited to 1440 psi at 100°F or 700 psi at 700°F. A hot tapping contractor should be consulted in the early planning stages for intermediate combinations and conditions exceeding those stated. Marbo, Inc., has made hot taps on steam lines at higher temperatures and pressures at Company locations.

## Temperature

If the operating temperature of the line is above 200°F then the weld has effectively been preheated and, as long as the weld is above 200°F, the chance of weld cracking is very low. Some risk of cracking still exists in sour or hydrogen service when the weld cools to ambient during a shutdown. For all wall thicknesses except "very thin" (defined previously), the operating temperature of the line has no effect on whether burn-through will occur.

## 673 Controllable Parameters

### Welding Procedure

**Low-hydrogen Electrodes.** Low-hydrogen electrodes (Exx16 or Exx18) are superior to cellulosic electrodes (Exx10, Exx13, Exx24, etc.) for hot tap and repair welding for two reasons:

- They have a lower risk of weld cracking
- They are less penetrating and therefore reduce the risk of burn-through

**Temper Bead Welding Sequence.** The most practical way of avoiding both burn-throughs and weld cracks is by using either 3/32 or 1/8-inch diameter electrodes with a temper bead weld pass sequence (see Figure 600-8). The basic principle of the temper bead technique is to avoid completing the weld (cap pass) on the base metal of the pipe. The temper bead technique is effective in reducing the weld HAZ hardness and can significantly reduce the weld cracking risk.

The temper bead technique is often not well understood by welders and craft foremen, but the need to do it conscientiously should be emphasized.

**Longitudinal Welds on Full Encirclement Sleeves.** The two halves of a full encirclement sleeve should be joined using a butt weld rather than lap welded with a butt strap. This longitudinal butt weld should be a full penetration weld. Fusing the weld to the main run pipe is acceptable. When a backing strip is not used, penetration of the first pass into the run pipe is usually negligible and is not of concern. For very thin wall thicknesses, use a backing strip to avoid fusing to the main run and to minimize the risk of burn-through.

### Flow Rate and Pressure

**Burn-through.** For thin, intermediate, and thick wall, the chance of burn-through is independent of the flow conditions, and hot tapping lines with no flow will not result in burn-through. Some flow is desirable when hot tapping very thin lines, to dissipate the heat of welding and decrease the risk of burn-through. There is no need

**Weld Cracking.** Decreasing the flow rate for liquids and for low pressure and low velocity gases will decrease the probability of weld cracks. This is because the slower weld cooling rate will result in lower hardness welds and less susceptibility to delayed hydrogen cracking. This benefit will decrease as the pipe wall thickness increases.

Cracking probability is independent of the fluid heat capacity in thick wall pipes. Decreasing or stopping the flow is desirable for intermediate wall thicknesses, and recommended for thin wall thicknesses. Flow should be maintained for very thin wall thicknesses.

- **Liquids:** The heat removal is independent of pressure for liquids, but is substantial even if the fluid is not flowing. #### Preheating

Preheating of all hot tap and repair welds is desirable but typically cannot be done. Unfortunately, a line with flowing product represents a very large heat sink. Preheat will be most effectively maintained by electrical resistance heating, but the required heat input may be very large. Experience has shown it difficult, if not impossible, to maintain preheat on liquid or high velocity gas lines.

When possible, carbon steels (P1) should be preheated to a minimum of 200°F. Preheat is best measured using a temperature indicating crayon.

## 674 Safety Considerations

### General Safety Considerations

The general provisions of the Corporation *Fire Protection Manual* should be followed. Specifically, standby fire extinguishers and/or water should be provided in the work area. All personnel concerned with use or operation of the lines or equipment being hot tapped must be advised of the proposed job plan. Use of a hot work permit and an accident prevention tagging system is recommended. In this procedure, the supervisor in charge of the work will tag all valves or blinds controlling flow through lines in the work area, and will remove his tags only after completion of the job.

### Special Precautions for Liquids Above Flash Point

The following special precautions should be followed to avoid a fire hazard when draining the tapping machine after a tap into a line containing a liquid at a temperature above its flash point.

- If the hot tap is on the bottom of a line or horizontal, install stainless steel tubing to the tapping machine drain connection. The tubing will be used to drain and cool the hot stock from the tapping machine. Route the tubing through a container of water or provide sufficient length to cool the liquid below its flash point
- If the hot tap is made into the top of a line, the hot liquid held in the space above the gate valve wedge and below the bleeder of the tapping machine

---

cannot be bled away. The following procedure must be used to remove this liquid:

1. Install an additional ⅝-inch minimum bleeder valve on the tapping machine adapting stub as near to the gate valve end of the machine as is practical.

2. Install a valve on the top vent connection at the head crank end of the tapping machine. Install stainless steel tubing at the vent connection. Route the tubing through a container of water, or provide sufficient length to cool the liquid below its flash point.

3. Install the hot tapping machine on the gate valve, pressure test, and tap.

4. Retract boring bar, close gate valve, open top vent, and sample stock from the tubing.

5. Pump glycerin into the bottom bleeder. Glycerin has a higher specific gravity than most petroleum fractions and will displace the hot stock above the valve wedge upward and out the top vent connection. Pump at approximately 1 gpm for long enough to ensure that the hot stock has been pumped out.

6. Drain the glycerin through the bottom bleeder and remove the machine.

## 675 Procedure and Equipment

### Selecting the Hot Tap Location

The line location for the hot tap should not be near a longitudinal or circumferential weld seam. Several pipe diameters' spacing should be maintained between the hot tap and existing welds. A hot tap should also not be made in the area of a bend. The proposed hot tap location should be thoroughly inspected for actual wall thickness and out-of-roundness before proceeding. Avoid taps on the bottoms of lines; cuttings will fall toward the packing gland and drive shaft of the tapping machine, creating the possibility of damage or jamming.

### Welded Attachments

Hot tap connections should be designed for internal pressure and external loads (e.g., the weight of the valve and the hot tap machine) using the area replacement method in accordance with the appropriate specification (e.g., ASME, ASME/ANSI B31.3, B31.4, B31.8, etc.).

For plant piping, the branch reinforcement may be selected from the piping class. Note that the branch reinforcement tables in the Corporation Piping Specification only consider the internal pressure; additional reinforcement may be required for the external loads.

## Full Port Gate Valves

Full port straight-through gate valves are normally used for hot taps. The Company normally purchases reduced port valves so care should be taken either to obtain the full port straight-through style or confirm that the cutting machine will not damage the valve seats. Occasionally, the lugs on the valve seat rings will jam the cutter and may require grinding off. ## Hydrotesting

To avoid pressure testing the entire system, the branch connection should be hydrotested prior to cutting into the main line. Although desirable, hydrotesting the hot tap connection is not commonly done unless required by the operating code.

**Hydrotest Pressure.** Hot tap connections should be hydrotested at the pressure required in the appropriate code, typically 1.25 or 1.5 times the design pressure of the line or maximum rated operating pressure. To prevent temperature-induced pressure increase, the test pressure should be released as soon as possible after completion of the test and the connection vented.

**Test Media.** Water may be used to test any line operating under 200°F. At temperatures greater than 200°F, oil, glycerine, or commercially available products (e.g., Dowtherm), may be used, but the problem of disposal should be considered. In some instances, the pipe contents can be bled off the line and used to pressure test the fitting. If air or inert gas is used, the pressure should be limited to 1.1 times the design pressure.

**Collapse.** When testing full encirclement fittings such as stopple fittings (covered by Section 676) confirm that the test pressure will not collapse the main run line. The collapse pressure of the main run may be calculated using the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code, Section VIII, Division 1, UG-28. Note that the ASME/ANSI piping and pipeline codes do not contain design details for externally pressurized pipes.

**Brittle Fracture.** When the hydrotest fluid temperature is below 70°F, brittle fracture during hydrotesting becomes a possibility. Equipment should not be pressure tested when the hydrotest fluid temperature is below the minimum pressurizing temperature specified by ASME Section VIII, Figure UCS 66; see also Section 400 of the *Pressure Vessel Manual*.

## 677 Considerations for Other Materials

**High Strength Line Pipe (API 5L X56 & above).** These steels are covered in the *Pipeline Manual*, Section 700.

**Low Alloy Steel.** Because postweld heat treatment (PWHT) is required, ambient temperature hot taps of steels with P-numbers 4 and 5 should not be considered. For an explanation of P-numbers see ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code Section IX, QW-420. If the line is operating at above 300°F, a hot tap may be considered without PWHT with limited risk. The weld will become susceptible to hydrogen cracking if the line is allowed to cool below 300°F prior to PWHT. If this happens the weld should be replaced and heat treated or undergo thorough ultrasonic examination before and after heat treatment.

**Austenitic Stainless Steels (P-number 8).** Only "L" grade (304L, 316L, etc.) or stabilized (321 or 347) stainless steels should be welded. Caution is advised when welding on stainless steels in environments that may contain chlorides. Particular care is needed on insulated line. Prior to welding, insulation and any deposits should be removed and the line inspected both visually and via dye penetrant. Chlorides can cause stress corrosion cracking of stainless steels at temperatures above 150°F.

Unlike carbon and low alloy steels, stainless steels are not subject to hydrogen cracking at welds. Therefore, reducing the flow is not necessary. Flow is recommended for making hot taps on very thin wall. Preheat and temper bead welding of stainless steel are not required.

**Other Materials.** Other materials such as aluminum, copper alloys, and cast iron may also be hot tapped, but may require special procedures. Consult CRTC's Materials and Equipment Engineering group.

# 680 Storage and Handling

## 681 Identification and Segregation

### Marking and Identification

Typically the pipe manufacturer stencils the grade, diameter, wall thickness, heat number, pipe number, manufacturer, etc., on the pipe. For 10-inch and larger pipes the marks are usually painted on the inside of the pipe. For 8-inch and smaller they are on the outside.

The pipe can be color-coded to distinguish the grade and/or wall thickness.

Valves and fittings should be tagged with the purchase order number, size, ANSI class, item number, and manufacturer.

### Segregation

Pipe of different lengths, grades, outside diameters or wall thicknesses should be racked separately. Adequate access to individual racks should be provided.

## 682 Storage of Valves and Fittings During Construction

Valves and fittings should be segregated by size, class, etc. They should be stored on pallets or a slab to keep them out of mud and water. In wet or cold climates, a storage tent may be appropriate. Exposed flange faces should be protected against corrosion and damage by applying suitable grease and bolting on wood or steel

---

covers with at least four bolts. Threaded connections should be protected by thread protectors. Beveled ends and valve openings should be protected by snug fitting end protectors. Large valves (4-inch and larger) should not be lifted by the handwheel or valve stem. Use slings or cradles around the body to lift.

## 683 Pipe Handling and Storage During Construction

### Short-Term Stockpiling of Pipe

**Mechanical Damage Prevention.** Pipe should be stacked in a staggered pattern so that each joint in the stack is supported for its full length by the two joints in the lower row (see Figure 600-12). Pipe should be restrained at all times against rolling by a sufficient number of chocks or stanchions. The stacking height of coated pipe (except fusion bonded epoxy) should be limited to prevent coating damage.

**Fig. 600-12** Staggered Stacking Arrangement for Pipe Storage

[FIGURE: Fig. 600-12 Staggered Stacking Arrangement for Pipe Storage]

A pipe stack should be supported along its length to prevent sagging. The ground should have adequate bearing strength so that no settling occurs that would affect the stacking arrangement. Each tier should be level-checked before and after additional tiers are added.

**Corrosion Protection.** The bottom row in a stack should be elevated at least 4 inches above ground level on earthen berms covered with polyethylene sheeting or on wooden skids. The storage area should be on relatively high ground and have drainage adequate to keep the pipes out of standing water. Pipes should be left open—not covered with plastic—to allow free flow of air through the pipes.

**Coated Pipe.** Coated pipe should not be lifted with cable slings or clamps lest they damage the coating. Coated pipe should be racked on padded supports or earthen berms. Pipes should not touch the stanchions or other pipes unless sufficient padding is provided. Stacking height of coated pipe should be limited to the number of tiers specified by the coating manufacturer. Weight-coated pipe requires special attention.

Some coatings (e.g., coal tar enamel, Pritec, Extrucoat, and most tape wraps) become soft and are more easily damaged when warmed by the sun. ## 684 Long Term Storage of Pipe

Pipe that will be stored for more than six months should receive additional attention, as follows.

### Uncoated Pipe

To avoid pitting corrosion, uncoated pipe should be racked together so that no pipe surface directly touches any adjacent steel surface (pipe, support, stanchion, etc.). Rubber, plastic sheet, plastic pipe, and rope are often used. This separation material should be checked to avoid corrosion underneath the separation material. For long-term storage absorbent materials such as wood and fiberboard should be avoided. Grease and plastic pipe caps should be considered.

**Avoiding Pitting Corrosion.** Except in very dry climates, water absorption should be a primary consideration for selection of the separation material. Corrosion pitting of the pipe under supports, at stanchions or chocks, and under rope used to separate the pipes can be a significant problem. The corrosion pits occur at locations where the steel surface is wet for extended periods. The pits can become so deep that even abrasive blasting cannot clean the pipe sufficiently for corrosion coating application.

Supports covered with plastic are generally good, except where the plastic is punctured (consider double sheets) or where the water collects in folds in the plastic sheet. Avoid using wooden supports and chocks. If used, hard woods, which absorb less water, are preferred over softer woods.

Pitting under ropes can be minimized by using plastic rope or PVC tubing, which retains little or no water.

**Storage Location.** For long-term storage, uncoated pipe should be stored away from coastal areas subject to salt spray. In salt spray areas, heavy corrosion scales can form that are difficult to remove, particularly for over-the-ditch cleaning machines. Soil underneath the stacks can be treated to prevent plants from growing.

### Coated Pipe

In addition to the precautions for short-term storage of coated pipe, the recommendations of the pipe coating manufacturer concerning ultraviolet damage of the coating should be followed. The most susceptible coatings are polyethylene, polyethylene (Pritec, Mapec, & Extrucoat) and tape wraps with polyethylene backing. Tarpaulin coverings may be required. Only the top layers of the stack are exposed to damage by ultraviolet light.

Extended storage can also affect the bending properties of fusion bonded epoxy. Chalking of the fusion bond epoxy is not detrimental.

# 690 Safety

## 691 Construction, Commissioning and Repair Procedures

To ensure the safety of personnel and operating facilities, installation, repair, testing, flushing and commissioning of piping require strict conformance to safe practices and procedures. The following activities and others may be hazardous:

- Erection and rigging. Properly select and control erection equipment, and properly use scaffolds. See the *Safety in Designs Manual*

- Torch cutting, welding, and postweld heating (stress relieving). Prevent ignition and combustion of flammables

- Radiographic inspection. Protect personnel in the area from exposure to radiation

- Hydrostatic and pneumatic testing. Protect personnel and equipment against failure of piping and equipment under test and unforeseen high-energy release of line contents. See Section 640 and Model Specification PIM-MS-3541

- Flushing, pre-commissioning and commissioning. Protect personnel and facilities from exposure to hot, corrosive, toxic, or high-pressure fluids

- Tie-ins and repairs on existing piping and equipment. Prevent ignition of flammables, exposure to toxic or corrosive fluids, loss of product, etc., and protect personnel and facilities from these hazards. Before cutting into an existing line or unbolting flanged joints or valve bonnets, ensure that the line is depressured at that point

## 692 Safety Checklists (Work Permits)

Company operating organizations normally have an established and systematic procedure for ensuring safety while working in or on existing operating facilities. The procedure typically includes checklists covering site conditions, work methods, potential hazards, and notifications to other plant organizations that may be affected by the work.

If the work involves risk of ignition and fire, the checklist is called a hot work permit. It is appropriately initialled and posted at the work site. Valves, blinds, machinery motors, etc., are tagged in "safe" positions until work is completed and the tags have been removed by the responsible personnel involved. As a minimum, Operations, Maintenance, and Fire and Safety must sign for hot work and tie-ins. (See Figures 600-13 and 600-14.)

## 694 Emergency Pipe Repairs

Leaks that occur in lines critical to the operation of a plant sometimes must be controlled without shutting down the plant. Several methods are available for controlling leaks. All are temporary measures at best, and permanent repairs must be made as soon as possible.

### Pipe Repair Fittings

Several suppliers, such as the Pipeline Development Company (Plidco), can provide specialized pipe repair fittings. These fittings are either off-the-shelf or specially engineered. In either case they have a specific pressure rating and blowout-proof seal designs, and they can be made into permanent repairs by external welding. Plidco-type sleeves are recommended over pipe repair clamps (discussed below).

The most commonly used Plidco fittings are the "Weld + Ends" and the "Split + Sleeve". The former requires a depressured and cut pipe. The latter can be installed on an operating pipe as a seal welded after flow is resumed. For more information on these and similar fittings, see Section 300 of the *Pipeline Manual*.

## Emergency Pipe Repair Clamps

Small leaks, especially in less hazardous services such as steam, can sometimes be temporarily controlled with a pipe clamp. Figure 600-15 shows a simple clamp.

Clamps are installed with A307 machine bolts or A193 stub bolts. Clamps up to and including 4-inch NPS require two bolts; large clamps require four bolts. A gasket must be used to contain the fluid. Use a composition sheet gasket, neoprene rubber or other material compatible with the fluid.

These clamps have no maximum pressure rating. They can, however, exert enough force on a section of corroded pipe to cause a general failure. Be certain the pipe wall is strong enough to withstand the clamping forces.
