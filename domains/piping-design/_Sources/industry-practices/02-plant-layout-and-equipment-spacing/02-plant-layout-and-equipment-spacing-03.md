# 1 Introduction

Layout refers to the location of plants and process/power units within the plants and associated utilities as well as the location of equipment items within those units. It also includes making provision for offsite areas such as storage areas, load out facilities, offices and access roads. In this context equipment includes buildings, control rooms, power generators, switch gear and motor control centers, workshops, store rooms etc. Also included are overall arrangements of the support structure and consideration of construction.

## 1.1 Philosophy

These guidelines provide guidance on all aspects of the layout for Oil Search Alaska North Slope Facilities. This guideline should be used for units constructed on site with modules fabricated offsite. This document is not retroactive, but as far as practicable, should be applied to green field and brownfield expansions and modifications on existing sites as well as the installation of temporary facilities.

## 1.2 Objectives

Safety-in-Design is a key factor associated with the layout design of any facility. The principal design criterion is to minimize or eliminate hazards in an environment where risk mitigation is critical to overall safety of the operating facilities, site personnel and the environment.

The design safety philosophy is intended to provide a basis for facility design that ensures all health, safety and environmental hazards with the potential to jeopardize the integrity of the project are kept at a minimum. Throughout the lifecycle of the installation, emphasis is placed on:

- Segregation of high-risk facilities or equipment from less hazardous operations and equipment.
- Minimum damage to equipment and prevention of long downtime or production deferment.
- Minimum involvement of adjacent facilities in a fire.
- Protection of the environment using best available technology not entailing excessive cost and achieving compliance with regulatory requirements.
- Facilities that are cost-effective to construct, transport, install, hook-up and commission, and operate.
- Facilities are accessible for all operations and maintenance activities.
- Critical emergency facilities are accessible for operators to perform emergency shutdown actions in the event of an abnormal operating condition, loss of containment, fire or explosion.
- Available access for fire-fighting.
- Site security.
- No danger or nuisance to persons or facilities beyond the adjacent property lines.

## 1.3 Exclusions

The focus of this guideline is on the consideration and development of layouts for plant facilities. The following exclusions apply:

- Specifications and allied design of process systems.
- Detailed pipe-work design and ergonomic/human factors aspects of pipe and valve positioning.
- Detailed engineering design of platforms, gratings, stairs and walkways.
- Design of support facilities, equipment, packages and modules.
- Overall safety systems design; other than the layout design for locations of safety equipment and allied facilities.

# 2 General

## 2.1 Purpose and Scope

The purpose of this document is to help maintain best current practices and requirements for plant layout and equipment spacing.

This guideline covers the general layout principles regarding OSA North Slope Facilities that will contribute towards a safe and operable plant. Aspects of determining the site location, such as environmental, regulatory and infrastructure are excluded.

Where there is Federal, Provincial or Local government legislation, regulation or practice applying to the plant layout and spacing, such requirements shall be complied.

Ideas to improve working methods are encouraged and anticipation that this document will be updated from time to time. Conflicts between this guideline and statutory requirements or referenced Codes and Standards shall be brought to the attention of the document custodian at the earliest opportunity.

This document represents the starting point for all projects. Project specific amendments will be issued to supplement this document where necessary.

## 2.2 Methodology

All recommendations made in this guideline are based on the following criteria:

- Safety & Fire Protection requirements.
- Human Factors, Engineering and Health requirements.
- Operating, Maintenance, and Construction requirements.
- Legal requirements.

This guideline outlines the acceptable standards, minimum technical requirements and recommended good practices. Often, fire protection and safety spacing requirements exceed maintenance accessibility requirements and legally allowable minimum distances. The relative location of the equipment or process unit depends on its potential release of flammable materials, its flammable liquid hold-up, and it's potential to be a source of ignition.

Recommended distances for different plants are compiled in the Appendices. These tables are supplemented by detailed notes in the following sections. If these guidelines cannot be followed, then additional loss control measures, such as fireproofing, water spray or blast hardening will be necessary.

## 5 Site Selection

Safety is a key consideration when deciding where to locate a plant. Other important factors include:

- Proximity of public and populated areas (particularly downwind).
- Access to raw materials and product markets.
- Availability of fuel supply.
- Availability of land, labor and cooling or process water.
- Means of effluent disposal.
- Interconnections with other plants and power grids.
- Risk to/from adjacent plants.
- Government policies (planning permission and investment incentives).

[Oil Search logo]

As far as safety is concerned, the most important factor is the distance between the site and populated areas. Providing this separation between a hazard and the public helps to mitigate the effects of a major incident.

The topography of the site is also an important feature. It is desirable to avoid terrain where hazardous fluids can flow down into populated areas. Potential contamination of water courses by liquid spills is also a consideration.

Site selection should also allow for availability of utilities and access for emergency services.

# 6 Plant Layout Design Criteria

Plant layouts shall maximize safety and exposure protection from the spread of fire, with ease of operation and maintenance consistent with economical design. Requirements for known future expansion shall be recognized and reasonable allowances made.

Space between units and equipment needs to be provided where necessary for safety, maintenance and personnel accessibility, but not wasted, as it usually adds to the capital and operating costs.

The main principles for safe facilities design and operation are:

- Minimizing the likelihood of uncontrollable releases of hydrocarbons and other hazardous materials
- Facilities and equipment are accessible for all operational and maintenance related tasks.
- Consideration for Area Classification
- Minimizing ignition sources
- Prevention of fire escalation
- Prevention of facility and equipment damage
- Providing for personnel protection and escape
- Available access for fire-fighting equipment and facilities including mobile (i.e., passive incipient protection or full fire-fighting)
- Consideration for Site Security
- Facilities that are safe and cost effective to construct, transport, install, hook-up & commission and operate.
- Balance of site manpower loading consideration.
- Critical emergency facilities are accessible for operators to perform emergency shutdown actions to prevent or mitigate potential fire explosion consequences.

The benefits of a good layout and design cannot be overemphasized. They provide an irreplaceable safety factor throughout the life of the plant, often at very little increase in initial cost. Good plant layout should achieve an economic balance of the requirements of safety, environmental, construction, operations, maintenance, vehicle movement, future expansion and process relations that meet the Company's standards and regulatory code requirements. Where new facilities are planned within an existing live plant, minimum disruption to the existing plant must be a priority. The spatial arrangement of plant items is a critical feature in the design of any facility and has a significant impact on safety and both the installation and operating costs.

These following elements will start to define the site:

- Location of main entrance and egress requirements and to access to new and existing roads.
- Location of flare and process areas to be crosswind of one another and downwind of personnel areas based on prevailing wind.
- Location of pipelines for import and export facilities.
- The situation of power lines entering the site will suggest the location for the substation.
- Interfaces with associated drill sites, process and utility plants.
- Topographical data that affects cut and fill requirement; tankage and storage areas for example may be located on naturally lower ground.

Using the hazard assessment of each plant operation will help establish the layout or orientation of process units within the plant. Review the possible loss events and the consequences for each proposal and then select a layout which will minimize the overall property damage and related business interruption should an incident occur.

Access should be provided for fire-fighting and damage control. Main access roads within the site should run in a north-south, east-west direction, and perpendicular to one another, preferably around all sides of process and utility units, to afford good access for mobile equipment during operations, maintenance, and turnaround.

Truck movements through the plant should be routed away from the main personnel areas, and where possible, loading areas should be minimum distance from the main entrance.

The site should have at least one emergency access / egress in addition to the main entrance and be surrounded by an internal perimeter road. Dead end roads and personnel access ways should be avoided.

Environmental considerations should be incorporated into the layout. Effluent treatment plants should not be sited adjacent to residential or similar property. Toxic, non-toxic and surface water drainage systems should be separated and proper means of disposal built into the layout.

## 6.1 Plant Layout Considerations

This section outlines the key aspects to be considered when preparing preliminary layouts during pre-FEED phase. Preliminary layouts are influenced by the following factors:

- Type of plant, process units
- Design specifications
- Overall site size and shape, natural slope
- Topography
- Permitting constraints
- Site soil and local environment conditions
- The selected construction and module movement method

These should be used in conjunction with the project applicable codes and standards, regulatory consideration and Company requirements.

The following points must be considered for optimum plant layout:

- Accessibility for normal operation and maintenance.
- Accessibility for fire-fighting.
- Accessibility for emergency shutdown.
- Accessibility for evacuation of personnel.
- Prevailing wind direction and effect of vapor clouds from or to the adjacent operating units.
- Individual fire risk areas, with their hazard classification and required fire protection measures.
- Segregation of high-risk facilities and sources of ignition.
- Protection of critical emergency facilities.
- Segregation and protection against blast and overpressure scenarios.
- Protection of adjacent facilities in the event of a fire.
- Security of installations from outside hazards.
- Minimizing danger and inconvenience to personnel and property beyond the boundary fence.
- Ergonomic Factors.
- Escape routes should be as straight and obstruction free as possible.
- Hazardous area classification.

[Oil Search logo]

- Ensuring that flammable vapor generated from one facility will diffuse to a concentration well below the lower explosive level (LEL) before it reaches any other facility or area where a source of ignition may exist.

- Providing a reasonable degree of protection (distance) against heat radiation.

- Distribution of underground fire water, sea water, sewer piping and electrical and instrument cables.

- Providing access and equipment parts movement pathways during overhauls.

Other factors may require spacing greater than minimum recommendation of this Guideline. In this category consideration should be given to:

- Proximity of adjacent property and the consequential environmental and public relations effects.

- Special process hazards, which may necessitate increased spacing.

- Special plant maintenance or installation considerations.

- Future development plans both inside and outside the boundary fence.

- Topographical features of site and direction of prevailing winds.

- Political security factors.

- Special hazard zoning plans for existing plant areas.

- Drainage – natural surface drainage or man-made dikes/buried pipes.

## 6.2 Plant Space Considerations

There are particular spacing constraints which need particular care and attention when optimizing the layout area utilization in both new plants and brownfield modifications:

- Layout design requirements need to focus on achieving adequate safe spacing of equipment and hazardous elements in the space environment (e.g., minimum horizontal and vertical clearances and minimum fire separation allowances).

- Layout should allow sufficient separation of buildings and temporary buildings to account for hazardous inventories, especially those likely to arise during start-up, shut-down and emergencies (e.g., lessons learned from past major events).

- Ensure that safe areas are established away from potentially hazardous items and activities.

- The location of storage areas for hazardous materials needs to consider blast overpressure contours and sympathetic detonation effects.

- Equipment tends to be large and layout can have an impact on constructability and operability factors, including equipment maintenance access ways and lay-down areas.

- Any intentions for future expansion.

# 7 Plant Type, Process/Utility Units

Plant layout is influenced by the type of plant (both the industry and technology utilized) and the kind of process units and associated utility/support facilities it contains. Generally, process/utility units are laid out according to the process flow, except where certain ignition sources or hazardous materials need to be separated. This arrangement minimizes the uncontrolled transfer of flammable materials, which is desirable both for economics and safety.

Each process/utility unit must be sufficiently separated from the others to allow units to be shut down for maintenance and turnaround, while others are still operating. Separation distances also define fire risk areas and limit the maximum area reasonably expected to be totally involved in a single fire.

Consideration shall be given to avoidance of spurious trips initiated or caused by potential vapor release areas, high point vents, pig launcher/receiver, located in areas with gas detectors.

At this stage of the project, some nominal placement of major equipment items will occur within each process/utility unit module and other associated facilities. This placement will be further refined during Detailed Engineering phase, once the preferred site location and process have been selected.

## 7.1 Classifications

Processes and operations can be empirically classified into one of three classes according to their explosion and fire hazard – Moderate, Intermediate or High (as shown in Appendix B).

These classifications (Appendix B) do not substitute for a proper hazard identification or analysis method. They are intended only to be used in determining spacing requirements.

Many additional factors and judgments can still affect the class to which the process is assigned such as: vessel size, flammable liquid hold-up, unit size, gas vs. liquid phase, pressure, temperatures, loss history, interdependency, lead time to rebuild, and market share. Therefore, if any of the above features of a process are higher than normal, raise it to a higher class.

## 7.2 Application of this Guideline

Layout is concerned with the spatial arrangement of the modules and process equipment (and its interconnections) within the plant boundaries, the location of the plant area within the site boundaries, and the location of the site relative to the surrounding environment and residential communities. This guideline makes a clear distinction between the layout of the various plants/units on a site and the arrangement of process vessels or equipment etc. within the plant (or plot).

## 7.3 Legislative Regulations, Local Codes, and Standards

Local legislative regulations adopted by the State of Alaska must be used in conjunction with governing codes and standards.

Minimum distance of equipment from site property lines shall comply with national (e.g. federal), state, and local codes and safety regulations or deed restrictions. If there is a conflict, the greater distance shall govern.

If local codes and standards for plant layout conflict with guidance given in this guideline, the more stringent text shall govern upon approval by the Company.

## 7.4 Requirements

- Projects shall undergo a risk assessment to an appropriate level of detail.
  a) Calculations relevant to layout or concerning potential major releases of hazardous materials shall be made available for such a review.
  b) Less stringent exceptions to or variances from requirements specified in this guideline shall be supported and documented by a risk assessment.
- New sites shall undergo a Project Security Review (PSR).

a) A PSR should be considered for expansions, revamps, and temporary facilities.

b) This review ensures that security risks such as terrorist activity and vandalism are reviewed and mitigated by competent personnel.

- Projects shall undergo a Concept Safety Evaluation (CSE) for inherently safer design guidance.
- Environmental considerations shall be included in the plant layout design to ensure that projects and developments strive to achieve the corporate goal of "no damage" to the environment. Projects shall be in compliance with Health, Safety and Environment (HSE) in design and loss prevention.

  Damage can be caused by:

  a) Emissions to the air, water, and ground.

  b) Physical interactions (nuisance, e.g. visual impact, noise, footprint, odor, dust, etc.).

  c) Energy inefficiency.

  d) Material use and waste generation.

  e) Interference with other users of the local environment.

## 7.5 Design Philosophy

Plant layout is particularly important at the design stage because the potential for subsequent modification to the layout is very low. Plant layout is a major driver of the risk that must be managed throughout the lifetime of the facility.

Plant layout shall identify and adopt the safest and most cost-effective layout that meets the requirements of the process design, safe operation/maintenance, and emergency action.

- Minimum spacing within plant shall be in accordance with:

  a) Section 14 for aboveground storage tanks.

  b) API RP 752 Management of Hazards Associated with Location of Process Plant Buildings.

  c) International Building Code / Alaska Administrative Code

- Separation distances shall satisfy the following:

  a) Provide minimum access clearance for emergency response actions.

  b) Allow safe access for operation and emergency evacuation.

  c) Provide minimum access clearance for maintenance.

  d) Give an economic design.

  e) Fire ratings are based on building separation

[Oil Search logo]

- PIP PNC00003 offers separation distances for common items of equipment that satisfy the aforementioned criteria.

  a) These distances should be regarded as a maximum for economic layout purposes.

  b) Separation distance adjustments either way may be justifiable if a change could materially affect the hazard or risk or if unusual access or process reasons exist.

  c) Any proposal to increase these separation distances should be subject to a cost benefit analysis.

- Separation distances are flexible and should be judged on a hazard and risk base approach. Whilst a quantified risk base approach may be used for location of units, buildings etc. within a site, such an approach is, in general, is considered impracticable as a basis for equipment layout within a unit.

  Variations within practicable separation distances are likely to reduce the knock-on potential. However, risk reduction within a plot is, in general, more effectively achieved by measures such as inspection, maintenance, and protective systems (e.g. fire protection, shut down systems, drainage etc.).

## 7.6 Operation/Maintenance

Process units should have staggered access from at least two directions. Egress shall be based on requirement set forth in IBC. The space between battery limits of adjoining units should be kept clear and open. Do not consider the clear area between units as a future area for process expansion. It should be labelled on the drawing as 'Clear separation space not for expansion use'.

To prevent a single event from impairing the overall operation, equipment or structures common to multiple process units, such as large compressors and turbines, and central control rooms should be located carefully. (It should be based on hazard analysis and evaluation. Also refer to API RP 752 on 'Management of Hazards Associated with Location of Process Plant Buildings' for more details). Layout the equipment within a unit in one of two general ways:

- Use grouped layout, where similar equipment is grouped together to ease operation, maintenance and control.

- Use flow diagram, where equipment is arranged in sequence similar to the PFD.

Where ever it does not conflict with loss of control, consider accessibility for maintenance and operations in determining spacing and layout. Locate equipment needing frequent overhaul, maintenance or cleaning at unit boundaries. Locate large vessels or equipment close to unit boundaries to allow easy access of cranes.

Maintenance access shall consider the following:

a) Equipment shall be located with adequate access for mobile handling equipment during maintenance.

b) If elevated equipment requires crane handling, sufficient area to manipulate a boom, a manual operated travelling crane, or a monorail and trolley shall be provided.

c) Equipment layout shall avoid the need to lift heavy pieces of frequently maintained equipment during unit operation over in-service process lines or equipment or provide passive protection (such as concrete containment).

### 7.7 Future Equipment

Space for probable future equipment shall be designated and located adjacent to related equipment. The allocation of such space should be addressed during the initial project risk assessment in order to identify potential hazards associated with probable future equipment.

Sewers and other underground piping and electrical runs shall be routed clear of known future equipment foundations.

## 8 Hazard Identification, Assessment and Mitigation

Risks associated with identified hazards can be reduced to a reasonable level through the application of good design principles that are focused on integrating safety into the overall considerations of the design process. This is achieved by reducing the probability of occurrence of risk events, conditions and sources, and minimizing their consequences.

Risks are inherent to any human activity and cannot be totally eliminated. The risk management process is based on the systematic identification of hazards and their associated causes in order to define the extent and likelihood of the potential loss.

This is used to prioritize and assign appropriate actions or treatments appropriate to the level of risk. These treatments are then used to reduce the risks to the project.

The guiding principle shall be that the project shall be able to demonstrate that risks have been reduced to As Low As Reasonably Practicable (ALARP).

ALARP principles and Cost Benefit Analyses are used to judge the adequacy at each stage of the risk management process and to ensure that the design meets the criterion of maximum protection of the personnel, environment and assets.

The first step should be the elimination of potential hazards by improving the inherent safety of the design concept, subsequently relying on engineering controls, and finally on administrative controls where fundamental design mitigation is not technically or economically feasible.

Hazards in plant facilities can sometimes be exacerbated by the general requirements for the location of the facilities to be remote of inhabited areas. This can on occasions affect support activities due to the location of support services and the nature of the products being handled. These hazards impact on the total life cycle design requirements of a plant, including its construction, installation, its operation and maintenance, and ultimately the de-commissioning and disposal of the plant.

A preliminary risk assessment should be done to determine the level of importance for each potential hazard and associated risks. This should involve key project personnel, and where possible experienced plant operational and maintenance personnel.

## 8.1 Plant Hazards

Hazards associated with plants will typically include the following:

- Inherent fire hazards presented by the release of flammable gases or vapors, flammable liquids, combustible dust, or ignitable fibers/filings, whether during normal operations or as a result of any unusual or abnormal condition.
- Dust arising from handling, processing and storage of bulk solids.
- High-temperature and high-pressure fluids, and hot surfaces.
- Rotating equipment located in or near operating areas.
- The handling of hazardous materials at truck and rail loading facilities.
- Weather, environmental conditions.
- Site drainage and storm water treatment.
- Firewater system.
- Large inventories of hazardous materials (including liquid fuels) from storage compounds and pipelines.
- Storage and handling of hazardous chemicals.
- Potential releases of toxic vapors (e.g. hydrogen sulphide (H2S), ammonia (NH3)).

The possible consequences of hazardous situations that may occur on all plant facilities are similar:

- Air, land and/or water pollution.
- Fire or explosion.
- Damage to equipment and allied facilities.
- Loss or disruption of production or other activities.
- Failure of process systems and utilities.

- Injury or death of personnel.

## 8.2 Hazardous and Safe Areas

Plants implicitly involve identifiable hazards in respect to the environment, location and presence of potentially explosive materials and ignition sources that have an impact on the safety of personnel and the facility / plant. Areas within such facilities require designation in relation to the level of effective safety or absence of particular hazards, and subsequently spaces are allocated for design and operational purposes as being a "Safe Area" or "Hazardous / Explosive Area". The determination and classification of the latter can be formalized through the systematic application of hazardous area classification which is prescribed by a wide range of methods; many of which fall under the category of State of Alaska specific standards or regulations.

### 8.2.1 Hazardous Areas

A hazardous area is defined in the applicable standards as an area in which an explosive atmosphere is present, or may be expected to be present, in quantities such as to require special precautions for the construction, installation and use of potential ignition sources. The explosive atmosphere is caused by the presence of a flammable liquid, gas or vapor or by the presence of combustible dust in suspension or in layers or a combination of gas and dust atmospheres.

### 8.2.2 Hazardous Areas Classification

Hazardous Areas are formally ranked as being a designated hazardous area using the relevant regional or State of Alaska standards that are directly applicable to the plant design under consideration.

The extent of a hazardous area depends on the rate of release, ventilation conditions, and fluid properties and is defined by the relevant regional or State of Alaska standards.

The potential for large releases in operational situations (e.g. process vents) needs to be assessed separately. Dispersion analysis is used to determine the size of the hazardous zone.

Drain system vents also need to be considered with respect to the location of ignition sources.

### 8.2.3 Hazardous Fluids (Sources)

The relevant State of Alaska standards will define which fluids are considered as sources requiring area classification. These typically include fluids such as:

- Flammable gas or vapor.
- Flammable liquids which are handled at or above their flashpoint, or which could be heated to the flashpoint after release.
- Flammable liquid that could form a flammable mist.
- Fluids which satisfy the criteria above and which are present periodically within the plant for 100 hours per year or more (e.g. during start-up).
- Flammable liquids containing residual, volatile materials under confined storage.

### 8.2.4 Safe Areas

A Safe Area in this guideline is regarded as a space that is not ranked as being a designated hazardous area by the relevant Regional or State of Alaska standard directly applicable to the plant design under consideration. The following criteria generally apply:

- A safe distance away from hydrocarbon inventory and sources of ignition.
- A safe distance from potentially explosive dust.
- Located upwind of any processing, flare, vent and drain systems.

- Involving ventilation that excludes unsafe gases or material that might impact negatively on the personnel or items located in the Safe Area.

### 8.2.5 Location of Safe Areas

All equipment that is critical to the plant safety support should always be located in a Safe Area (e.g. fire pumps, instrument/electrical equipment and an Emergency Assembly Area).

Safe Areas can also provide a controlled environment where welding can be carried out.

On large plants or complexes it may be necessary to provide more than one safe area. An escape and evacuation plan review will determine if this is required.

Muster Area /Temporary Safe Refuges (TSR) can be an allocated space within an already functional room or could be a separate designated area solely for the purposes of accommodating personnel during an emergency. The size of the main Muster Area/TSR should be 5 ft² per person.

[Oil Search logo]

Safe areas should be placed with the following considerations:

- Upwind of hydrocarbon releases, and as far as practical away from high pressure hydrocarbon inventory.
- Where there is a quick and effective means of evacuation from the plant.

## 8.3 Access Considerations

Plant facilities shall be provided with adequate access and egress for personnel, materials, and equipment by using access ways, roads, stairways, stiles, and walking / working surfaces including decks, floors, grade, platforms, ramps and walkways as per the following guidelines.

### 8.3.1 Escape and Evacuation

There is a clear distinction between means of evacuation and means of escape.

- Escape refers to safe means of personnel movement from hazardous areas to designated safe areas.
- Evacuation means leaving the installation and its vicinity in a systematic manner.

The escape and evacuation arrangements should allow everyone to reach a place of safety.

Emergency Response' procedures for possible incidents at plant shall be identified and accommodated in the plant layout. Make sure local authorities are aware of the types of possible incidents.

#### 8.3.1.1 Escape

The plant will have designated Muster areas where personnel can go during an emergency and be accounted for and from which they can then be instructed to evacuate the plant.

The main escape routes from process areas will be the main egress provided to minimize travel exits.

Escape routes from multi-storey process units shall be by stairs. Ladders may be accepted as means of escape routes where it is clearly not practicable to install stairs. Two independent paths of escape should be provided wherever possible. See the Appendices A for minimum escape route dimensions.

#### 8.3.1.2 Evacuation

The requirements relating to safe evacuation will be met by using a combination of means of evacuation according to current practice (e.g. vehicle transport, foot).

The number, size and location of evacuation systems need to be established based on facility manning, risk exposure analyses and emergency preparedness analysis.

# 9 In-Plant Unit Layout

Generally, the process units will be positioned in process flow order, with common utility blocks positioned to provide economical and effective services. Provision is normally made for future expansion

[Oil Search logo]

without disruption to working units. Administration, storage and loading areas should be located on the periphery.

A well-designed plant should have a uniform, functional appearance. Elevations of the various units should be determined early to establish a common site datum and relative elevations between units.

## 9.1 Philosophy

- The site area shall be divided into plots, and if possible, the plots should be rectangular.
- A single plot may contain more than one process unit or section.
- Irrespective of whether process units are on the same or adjacent plots, spacing requirements outlined in this guideline shall be satisfied. Spacing between process units shall take into account the following factors:
  - a) Exposure to possible fire radiation.
  - b) Exposure to possible explosion overpressure.
  - c) Maintenance requirements, including unrestricted hot work, of one unit while adjacent units are in service.
  - d) Business interruption potential and relative value of the units involved.
  - e) Prevailing wind direction.
  - f) Access by emergency response equipment.
  - g) Future expansion.
- Plant separation should consider the following:

  Plant separation distances depend on the size and congestion of the plant unit. The key issue is to limit escalation.

  - a) Maximum size of flammable gas cloud from one plant area does not significantly intrude into adjacent areas.
  - b) The magnitude of a gas explosion is not significantly increased by proximity of adjacent plant units.
  - c) Damage caused in one area does not lead to escalation in adjacent areas.
- If there is flow between individual units, the layout of the units should be, as far as economical, in a logical order of flow.

  The increased cost of wide spacing of process units must be weighed against the implied safety benefits in the event of a fire or explosion.

- Process units with hazardous inventories shall be located to minimize the risk of any inadvertent release of fluid to personnel within the site, and also on activities outside the site boundary. Topography, prevailing winds, personnel safety, and nearby population centers shall be considered in selecting the most suitable location.

  Project layout philosophy should be developed at an early stage to ensure its consideration in the safety assurance reviews for guidance on assessing the risk of such a release.

- Flammable material storage should be located on separate plots from process units. Flammable material may only be stored on a process unit plot provided all other options for separate plot location have been ruled out, additional risk reduction measures are incorporated, and a comprehensive risk assessment has been conducted.

  Though an inherently safer design of separate plot location is recommended for flammable material storage and process units, it is recognized it may not always be possible. In such situation, possible risk reduction measures to consider include storage deluge system, remote emergency shutdown (ESD), passive fire protection, blast protection, storage vessel orientation, etc.

- Field fabricated towers, fired heaters, etc., should be located at unit limits for construction convenience, if practicable. Exceptions may be taken in consideration of process design or hazard management benefits.

- Plant layout studies/ reviews should establish credible fire scenarios and the extent of direct flame impingement/ thermal radiation produced by the fire.

  a) This should be taken into account when finalizing layouts.

  b) Spacing between tanks and other items of plant can be relaxed with a higher degree of fire protection.

     For example, if passive fire protection (i.e., a fire wall) is provided between two critical product pumps then greater separation may not be required. In some cases, appropriate fire detection backed up by a rapid-fire response (whether by fixed fire-fighting systems or by manual means) can allow relaxation.

  c) In all cases, criticality of plant and equipment and implications of loss for asset damage, business interruption and reputation should be considered, as well as those for life safety.

## 9.2 Site Infrastructures

### 9.2.1 Roads

Roads are an important part of the design of a plant, as they provide access/egress and separation during normal operation as well as emergency access.

Consideration to the following needs to be given:

- Site roads shall separate plots. Refer to OGE-CICRT-000001 (Civil Design Criteria) for minimum width for site roads.
- Equipment shall be located so that site roads are not classified as a hazardous area.
  a) Minor site roads shall not be in an area classified as Class I Division 1.
  b) Any minor site road or in-plant road classified as Class I Division 2 shall have controlled access.
- Where applicable, plant layout including roadway design shall take into account the safe removal and stockpiling of snow in an operating facility.

## 9.2.2 Utilities

Site utility services shall be grouped together in a non-hazardous area with exceptions being utility services integral to process unit operation.

Site utility services should be located so that essential utility supplies can be maintained under emergency conditions (i.e., in the event of a flammable vapor release). The separation distances should be determined on a risk-based approach such that essential utility supply functions can be maintained within an agreed reliability.

## 9.2.3 Firewater Pumps

Firewater pumps shall be sufficiently remote from processing, storage, and loading areas to minimize the risk of fire or blast damage to the pumps given protective measures installed (e.g. fire/blast resisting enclosure).

The pumping facilities shall be capable of operation during the reasonable worst-case fire scenario and during primary power supply outage.

## 9.2.4 Flares

Flares and/or burner booms shall be located to avoid ignition of normal or accidental gas releases. Flares may become potential sources of toxic vapors. The potential danger from radiant heat from a flare should be taken into account for all potential wind directions on flare locations. The flare and vent systems shall comply with API Std. 521/ ISO 53521 or equivalent. The radiant heat intensities or emissions from flares and vent systems shall not exceed the limits above the abnormal conditions (e.g. flame out of flare system and accidental ignition of vent).

The location, spacing, orientation, and height of flares shall be determined, for the particular site, by a full assessment of the factors involved. Use of dispersion calculations should be considered. The height of elevated flares is also determined by the radiant heat calculations.

[Oil Search logo]

The following principles on siting should be applied:

- The flare(s) should, ideally, be located as close as practicable to the process units served, so as to allow the shortest, most direct, route(s) for the closed relief header(s), whilst avoiding passage through special fire risk areas.

  In practice, because they are a source of thermal radiation, and a possible source of ignition, flares are generally located remote from process facilities, storage areas, utility areas, and service/office areas, but well inside the property line.

- They should also be as far as practicable from public occupied areas that are, or may be in the future, located adjacent to the site boundaries.

- Design of the flare location shall consider thermal radiation, noise, luminosity, potential odor, potential ground level emissions, and ignition source related to the flare.

- A side-wind location (relative to the prevailing wind direction) from process facilities and similar sources of major release of flammable vapor is preferred.

- If more than one flare is provided, the location of each shall be based on operational requirements. The locations of all flares should be determined by any need for independent operation or maintenance of individual flares. This may require suitable spacing of separate flare stacks, or a tower-mounted multi-flare stack system. The latter should have facilities for lowering the stacks separately, to ensure that maintenance on any one flare stack can be carried out while the remaining flare stack system remains in operation.

## 9.2.5 Bulk Loading and Unloading Racks

Loading and unloading facilities handling flammable commodities shall be kept at a safe distance from accommodation spaces and control station. The loading and unloading facilities should be downwind or crosswind from process units and sources of ignition based on the prevailing wind. If this is impracticable, the loading/unloading area may be placed upwind at a prudent distance deemed by a thorough hazard analysis.

The following shall be considered in the layout:

- Loading/unloading areas for road transport shall have adequate space for access for filling, parking, and manoeuvring. A drive through rack arrangement is preferred.

- Loading/unloading areas shall include adequate spur tracks for standing, filling, shunting, and may require a siding for unserviceable rolling stock.

- Loading/unloading areas for container transport (e.g. bags, drums, crates) shall have adequate space for lifting and manoeuvring. Space may also be required for temporary placement of multiple containers during transfer.

[Oil Search logo]

- Loading/unloading areas for marine transport shall include adequate space for accessing transfer lines, docking, and positioning.
- For loading and unloading areas with potentially high risk to life, there shall be at least two independent escape routes for emergency egress.
- For additional guidance on the layout of material transfer facilities, refer to API Std 2610.

### 9.2.6 Security

Sites shall be within a restricted access perimeter. Site access will be monitored with CCTV. Access will be granted via the control room.

Location of units on a site relative to buildings, the boundary, and surrounding areas may be governed by local regulations and require agreement with the local authorities. Buildings used by site personnel during normal daytime working hours should normally be located within the pad entrance to minimize the number of people leaving and entering the site.

## 9.3 Buildings

All occupied buildings need to be located away from process areas, upwind of flare, vent and fired equipment, and outside of blast overpressure contours to protect personnel in the event of vapor release, fire or explosion.

Since ventilation in a building is generally less than that outdoors, a leak of flammable or toxic material tends to disperse more slowly and a hazardous concentration is more likely to build up. Also, if an explosion occurs, the blast pressure generated tends to be much higher and result in more damage. Ventilation and/or dust collection systems are required in enclosed storage buildings as well to assist with removal of mobile equipment exhaust and any off-gassing from the product and minimize dust.

Building design and orientation should also address environmental considerations such as prevailing weather direction (wind, dust, rain, ice and snow) and provide adequate protection from falling ice at building entrances.

### 9.3.1 Location

- A central control building (with or without satellite control buildings) for process and safety control may be used for all units in a process complex. Alternatively, dedicated control buildings may be required for individual process units or for parts of the site remote from the main processing area, e.g. a main tank farm or product loading facilities.
- Occupied buildings shall be provided with access ways and ideally should be located in an area classified as electrically safe.
  a) Occupied buildings shall not be located in any area classified as Class I Division 1.

b) If it is unavoidable that an occupied building be located in an area classified as Class I Division 2, it should conform to the requirements of API RP 500 Area Classification.

c) Control building shall be located away from noise sources and on prevailing upwind side of process and ignition/open flame areas, taking into consideration location of existing units.

d) Control buildings shall be located to allow unobstructed emergency pedestrian escape off the plot. The escape route shall not cross any special fire risk area and should not pass through an area containing process plant.

e) For further location guidance for process plant buildings, refer to API 752 or equivalent industry standard(s).

### 9.3.2 Blast Resistance

Control buildings and other occupied site buildings whether permanent or temporary shall be designed and located to minimize the risk to occupants.

### 9.3.3 Toxic and Hazard Consideration

If a unit has potential to produce a major toxic release, the location of the building should be specified after taking into account the following:

- Pressurization and ventilation of the building.
- Possible safe haven designation (i.e. possible shelter in place requirements) of the building.
- Prevailing wind direction.
- Escape routes.
- Operator involvement in safe shut down of the plant.
- Other design factors.

# 10 Plot Layout

## 10.1 General

The plot layout shall be an economical design to meet the process objective reflecting the need for safety to life and property, and allowing adequate access for maintenance, operation, and emergency action.

A plot may contain a process unit that can be subdivided into several sections. If such sections can process flammable and/or toxic fluids and may be operated independently (i.e. one section may be shut down with the other in commission), the spacing between equipment shall be sufficient to permit simultaneous normal maintenance on the shutdown section. The design should consider areas required

[Oil Search logo]

## 10.2 Plant Sections

The meaning of 'sections' in this context must be clearly understood.

- It is not intended to imply that there should be additional spacing between equipment.
- This does not apply to fired heaters with a common stack. It is acceptable to group together the heaters from more than one section, provided that individual heaters can be positively isolated from each other and any common stack.

This layout also has the advantage of collecting the permanent ignition sources together in one part of a plot.

If equipment is grouped together, the arrangement should permit, if possible, the use of common structures and maintenance equipment, e.g. a lifting beam.

## 10.3 Separation Distance

In determining adequate separation distances between the sections of a unit it is necessary to define probable maintenance and access needs and the effect of such activities on adjacent equipment.

Within the plot separation distances between individual items of equipment are generally determined by maintenance and emergency access requirements.

To meet this objective PIP PNC00003 provides data on recommended separation distances between process sections. Annex A provides data on clearances around equipment.

Recommended separation distances listed in external industrial standards should be treated as default values that the designer may reduce or extend based upon specific assessment of the risks involved. If possible, the specific risk assessment should:

- Address risks external to individual sections of a unit. For example, an external risk analysis might consider hazards to traffic around process facilities and buildings that need to be accessible in the event of an emergency.
- Consider risks within individual sections of a unit. For example, an internal risk analysis might assess the degree of confinement and congestion and the potential impact upon blast overpressure during an explosion.

## 10.4 Special Fire Risk Areas

The need for special fire risk areas shall be reviewed to decide if improved equipment design can justify their reduction or elimination. See also API 2218, for normative guidance on special fire risk areas.

[Oil Search logo]

## 10.5 Plant Scale Model

Layout design may be aided using 3-dimensional drafting software models. The cost benefit of using these tools should be assessed.

## 10.6 Construction Consideration

- If a plot is being developed on (or major modification work is being carried out on an existing unit), or adjacent to, existing units, any interaction of the construction and commissioning of the unit(s) of the new plot and interconnections between the existing units shall be considered. Measures shall be taken to achieve safe construction and commissioning of new plant, and to minimize disruption to, or stoppage of, existing units if this is necessary. Risks from construction activity should be considered and included in the formal risk assessment to compare the relative risk levels of location options.

- During the construction activities, consideration needs to be given to temporary facilities, these may include the following:

  a) Vehicle parking and bull rail access.

  b) Temporary construction offices and temporary construction facilities.

  c) Construction laydown areas.

  d) Drilling activities.

  e) Warehousing and storage areas.

  f) A suitable access route to the construction site should be provided for the workforce and construction equipment (e.g., cranes). To avoid possible damage by bulky equipment, the route should avoid roads with overhead pipe crossings or obstructions. Because of the number of vehicles likely to be involved, the route should be away from areas with appreciable pedestrian traffic.

  g) The layout and plant location should allow adequate access for operating, maintenance and fire-fighting to the units, and provide for escape routes for emergency evacuation.

# 11 Access Ways and In-Plant Roads

## 11.1 Philosophy

- Access ways within each unit shall be provided for maintenance and for fire-fighting from the roads around the plot. The design of the road layout should consider the plant complexity and the type(s) of fire-fighting appliances (i.e. fire-fighting trucks) likely to be utilized during an emergency.

Roads or access over firm ground should be provided to allow fire appliances to approach within reasonable operating distance of the hazard. Access should be kept free of obstruction.

- Emergency vehicle access points should be reviewed, including means of gaining entry where unattended or remotely-operated secure entry systems exist. There shall be at least pedestrian access for fire-fighting from all roads around the plot. At least two road approaches shall be wide enough to allow access by emergency equipment to process areas. If access is only possible from two sides, these should, wherever possible, be the longest opposite sides. Normally access ways should be arranged in a rectangular grid pattern so that fire-fighting can take place from two opposite sides.

## 11.2 Recommended Widths and Clearances

Minimum widths for personnel access ways, stairways, elevated walkways, platforms, shall comply with the Structural Design Criteria (OGE-STCRT-000001).

See Appendix A for minimum clearance for maintenance access around individual items of equipment.

- Access to modules or pre-assembled units should be considered at an early stage in the project development.

The use of height clearance indicators/barriers to indicate/prevent potential conflict between vulnerable stationary process equipment/piping above access ways and mobile equipment (e.g. crane jib) should be considered, where necessary, based on a review of the hazards considering type of traffic on the access way, type of fluid and inventory in the equipment/piperack, and safeguards in place to prevent contact.

## 11.3 Routing

- Access ways may be run longitudinally under pipe racks, provided that all relevant clearance factors have been considered. However, this shall not apply to roads or an access way from a road to a unit.
- Vehicular access ways shall be as straight as practicable and free of overhead structures except where pipe racks, ducts, are located, and should not cross main cable trenches. Equipment shall not protrude into access ways.
- Site-run piping and cable trays shall be installed in such a way that they do not affect access.
- Process equipment shall be located so that the access ways are not classified as being in Class I Division 1 areas.
- Access should take account of the clearance required by the proposed cranes or mobile handling equipment or if there is an economic advantage in a layout which necessitates the use of special equipment.

[Oil Search logo]

- If practicable and economical, the layout shall permit unrestricted access over minor roads and vehicular access ways for deployment of the largest crane and any other mobile equipment required for maintenance purposes and emergency services. Overhead pipe racks, ducts, and obstructions crossing these roads and access ways shall not interfere with such access.

- Where overhead pipe racks, ducts, cables, conveyors, and other obstructions cross a road or access way, the space available horizontally at grade shall permit access of the largest crane as required for maintenance purposes, so that the crane can be maneuvered to the required position. When the crane is being used for maintenance work, it shall be possible to raise and extend the jib to the required position without obstruction from overhead pipe racks, cables ducts, or equipment. Minimum vertical clearances are given in Appendix A.

- Should the above requirements be considered not economical and practicable, details of the equipment required to be removed for maintenance access shall be reviewed.

## 11.4 Access to Platform

- Access to platforms shall meet the requirements of Structural Design Criteria (OGE-STCRT-000001).

- Platform escape routes shall not be adjacent to each other.

- Platform escape routes shall not be adjacent to sources of hazard.

# 12 Piping

## 12.1 References

Requirements for process piping systems are covered in Plant Piping Design Specification (OGE-PISPE-000005) that includes information on layout, access, pipe clearances, and routing.

## 12.2 Routing

- Within a process unit, pipe runs shall be grouped in overhead pipe racks.

  a) The pipe racks should be as straight as practicable.

  b) Crossings of access ways should be at right angles to the access way and as far as possible from junctions and bends in the access way.

  c) Separate, but consistent elevations shall be maintained for both north-south and east- west pipeway routing.

- If pipe leakage during a fire could lead to a major increase in the fire intensity, such pipes should not be located in any special fire risk area.

a) Such pipes shall include flare lines and pipes connected to 353 ft³ or more of flammable liquid which cannot be remotely isolated.

b) If this is not practicable, the location of such pipes in the special fire risk area shall have external passive fire protection.

- Flammable or toxic liquid/gas piping that remains in service during a process unit outage shall not be routed through that process unit.

- Access and space accommodations shall be provided for:

  a) Designated removable pipe sections (e.g. for cleaning).

  b) Inspection needs.

  c) Complying with environmental requirements (e.g., for obtaining Volatile Organic Compound measurements).

## 12.3 Minimum Clearances

- Piping systems shall be designed, and equipment shall be laid out, to provide access by mobile equipment.

- Access ways shall be planned accordingly, and minimum clearances for this purpose shall be as described in Appendix A.

## 12.4 Piping Supports and Pipe Tracks

- Piping supports shall not block access ways nor interfere with access.

- Piping support layout should allow for removal of pipe spool if expected to be necessary for maintenance of equipment.

- Outside the plot and in tankage areas, pipes should normally be in pipe tracks.

- The minimum distance between the nearest edge of a pipe track and the toe of a bund (dike) shall be provided.

## 12.5 Pipe Racks

The following types of pipe racks may be required:

- Interconnecting pipe rack (elevated).

- Unit pipe rack (elevated).

- Pipe rack or sleeper way (at grade).

[Oil Search logo]

Pipe racks shall be single level whenever possible. On single level pipe racks, utility and service piping shall be placed in the center of the rack, with process piping on the outsides. Where multilevel racks are required, utility and service piping shall be placed on the upper rack level. Generally, process lines shall be placed on the lower rack levels; however, draining considerations shall be taken into account when placing process lines on multilevel racks. Piping that needs to be free draining or 'no pocket' will often dictate the levels for elevated structures. The highest level is often used for flare lines, non-pocket lines, or to support aerial coolers. Generally, it is cheaper to run pipes at grade, but overhead racks offer much improved access for operation and maintenance.

Interconnecting pipe racks carry the main process inlet, export and transfer lines and utility distribution lines to and from process and utility units. They should not run through process or utility units, thus avoiding the hazard of live lines within unit shutdowns. Unit racks connect to the interconnecting rack taking lines into the respective units via a battery limit station that usually will have a set of isolation valves.

Unit pipe racks are the main arteries carrying the pipes and cables into the units, and as such should be centrally located. They are usually elevated and are of sufficient height to allow vehicle access beneath. The width of rack will be determined by the number of lines and cable trays with an allowance for future requirements.

Piping should enter/leave from above or below the rack level. Piping should not flat turn on or off the rack closing out space for installation of future lines. Lines requiring expansion loops should be located close to pipe rack columns and should be grouped together to enable use of common supports. Space between levels must be sufficient to allow the largest lines to leave or enter the rack.

Electrical and instrument cable trays are normally run in or off the side of the pipe racks. The Piping Designer shall liaise with these disciplines to allocate these areas. Cable trays systems shall be kept away from hot lines. When there are large numbers of cable trays, they should be installed at the highest levels of the rack and process pipes should not be crossing or running above them.

## 12.6 Valve Operation

- Valve actuators and hand wheels shall be located so that they may be conveniently reached when standing at ground level or from a permanent platform.
- Extended spindles or chain-operated valves may be used but should be avoided if practicable. Chains or extended spindles shall not hang nor be in the path of moving equipment, e.g. cranes.
- Refer to (OGE-ICSPE-000004) Control Valve Specification for more specific valve guidance.

[Oil Search logo]

## 12.7 Spades (Blinds)

Spading (blinding) positions or other means of positive isolation, e.g. removable spool pieces, that require access during routine operation, shall be accessible without any requirement for scaffolding. This includes:

- Spades (Blinds) that require swinging where normal start-up or shutdown routine operations occur frequently, e.g. catalyst regeneration.
- Other spading (blinding) positions where economically practicable.

## 12.8 Battery Limit Isolation

Battery Limit stations are often provided at process unit boundaries to facilitate the quick isolation of interconnecting pipeline in an emergency. If all boundary isolations can be initiated from the one location, this limits the escalation risk of an event.

Battery limits are also utilized to designate separation of ownership.

- It shall be possible to isolate hydrocarbon and utility systems, including instrument air, plant air, nitrogen, process water, and cooling water at each plot without affecting the supply to any other plot.
- If the plot contains more than one section, the isolation shall be provided for each section.
- The isolating valves shall be readily accessible, and not in an area of special fire risk. They would be isolated in a fire situation to limit hydrocarbon release to the fire and to prevent any loss of containment in a utility system depleting supplies essential to adjacent sections.

# 13 Equipment

## 13.1 Philosophy

Equipment shall be located or oriented in such a way as to minimize congestion and confinement that could increase potential overpressure in the event of an explosion. Location of equipment shall take into account the predominant dispersion characteristics of potential leaks, in order to safeguard areas of importance for safety. Equipment with potential high energy failure (e.g. gas turbines) shall be located such that possible damage or escalation effects will be minimized. Safe separation may be achieved by ensuring sufficient distance between areas or by installation of physical barriers (e.g. fire/blast walls) to prevent escalation from one area to another.

If available, published equipment spacing tables (such as PIP PNC00003) may be used for guidance, but should be regarded as default values subject to thorough risk and hazard assessments. Further considerations for equipment spacing risk assessments may be found in Guidelines for Facility Siting and Layout by CCPS an AIChE industry technology alliance.

## 13.2 Detailed Equipment Layout Considerations

This section outlines the key aspects to be considered when developing detailed equipment layouts during the define phase. As well as reviewing all the preliminary layout considerations, equipment-specific spacing and arrangements are considered.

Equipment spacing guidelines are provided in the Appendix A. These should be used in conjunction with any project applicable regulatory codes and Company requirements.

In general, equipment shall be arranged with a view to providing:

- Separation of ignition sources from hazardous areas.
- Minimal explosion overpressure in case of ignited flammable release.
- All special process requirements (gravity flow, self-draining, critical NPSH, etc.).
- Access for fire-fighting and emergency response.
- Prevention of serious consequences from dropped objects.
- Facilitation of plant operations and control in normal and emergency situations.
- Avoidance of confined spaces wherever possible.
- Minimal possibility for escalation of fires and other failures or accidents.
- Safe containment of accidental release of hazardous liquids.
- Planned simultaneous operations (e.g. drilling and production, construction and production).

Spacing for equipment handling non-flammable materials is only that required for operations and maintenance. Special consideration should be given to equipment handling toxic or corrosive materials.

Similar equipment should be grouped together where practical (except firewater pumps). Parallel equipment should be located symmetrically, wherever possible to aid flow distribution.

Equipment containing large inventories of combustibles (e.g. oil/gas separators) should be separated to reduce the risk of escalating events.

Equipment should occupy the minimum of space; consistent with its function, maintenance, operational inspection and access requirements.

Equipment stacking is often necessary to achieve a reasonable utilization of plot space, however, to keep the fire involvement as low as possible, the following guidelines should be observed:

- Avoid stacking more than three levels of equipment.
- Do not locate equipment over any of the following:
  a) Pumps or compressors handling flammable materials.

b) Air coolers (fin-fans).

c) Heat exchangers or drums containing flammable materials above 600 °F or above its auto-ignition temperature (whichever is lower).

In order to minimize the length of piping / conveyor runs; process equipment should be arranged and oriented in a manner that is consistent with the main process flow and meet with piping / conveyor flexibility requirements.

To avoid unexpected interferences, clashes and congestion; the requirements for ancillary equipment should be established before finalizing the location of major equipment. These include:

- Access platforms to remote valves, instruments, Safety Relief Valves.
- Protective frames around equipment.
- Safety systems (e.g. eyewash stations, safety showers adjacent to any dangerous chemical handling units).
- Operator control stations and local instrument panels.
- Monorails required to assists dismantling work.
- Enclosures around engines.
- Drains and vents.
- Equipment auxiliaries such as pump seal systems.

## 13.3 Rotating Equipment

The layout of all rotating equipment should provide space for maintenance, removal and replacement of rotors and other major components. Rotating equipment should not be located below pipe racks.

Pumps should be grouped together with good maintenance access to the motor, and good personnel access to seals and suction / discharge valves. Adequate space for lifting and handling facilities for maintenance shall be provided. Maintenance access should allow for removal of the motor or any components over 50 lbs which include seals, coupling, rotors and bundles, either by 'A' frame, motorize forklift or monorail.

Spacing separates pumps with higher fire potential and provides access for fire-fighting. When a pump and its spare are exposed to a common fire hazard, additional separation between them may be justified to minimize the risk of losing both of them during a fire.

The following shall be considered in locating the equipment:

- Pumps should be located in the open area where it is practicable to access for operation and maintenance. If unavoidable, fire protection measures such as passive fire protection or water sprays are required for equipment and structures above the pumps.

- Adequately ventilated shelters shall be provided for large machines requiring in situ maintenance. Adequate space for lifting and handling facilities for maintenance shall be provided.

- Due to asphyxiation risk, nitrogen compressors shall be located in the open or in adequately ventilated houses.

- Pumps should be located and specified so that an acceptable NPSH can be obtained without undue elevation of suction vessels or columns. Pumps in flammable or toxic service shall not be located in pits to meet this requirement.

- Recommended distances from other items of equipment are provided in PIP PNC00003.

- Pumps, compressors, and blowers in flammable fluid service should be located 49 feet or more horizontally from any part of a fired heater, or any process lines that may operate at a temperature of 1202°F or greater.

## 13.4 Fired Heaters and High-Temperature Lines

Fired heaters shall normally be located at a safe distance from hazardous areas as they provide potential sources of ignition. Where practical, they should be located on the upwind side of a process unit, near battery limits, with consideration given to the effects of adjacent units as well as the equipment in the same unit. Spacing also minimizes damage to other equipment in case of a fired heater fire.

Where hazardous areas cannot be avoided, the potential for an incident to escalate shall be minimized (e.g., through use of segregation and/or fire barriers). Locating the fired heater shall consider the following:

- A heater, or group of heaters, shall be located on the periphery of a plot and immediately adjacent to an unrestricted road.

- There shall be adequate access for fire-fighting from all sides of a heater and, in the case of a group of heaters they shall be separated from the remainder of the unit(s) by vehicular access ways on the other three sides of the group.

- The layout and design of heaters shall normally be such that the tube removal can be affected by mobile lifting equipment, for which there shall be proper access i.e. the crane location shall not obstruct emergency access roadways. Space requirements for operation and for withdrawal of tubes and burners shall meet the minimum clearance guidelines:

  a) Horizontal fired heater – tube length plus 10 feet.

[Oil Search logo]

b) Vertical fired heater – not less than 25 feet wide, for distance equal to 25% of fired heater circumference.

- The following equipment and fittings that handle flammable fluids shall be considered to be potential sources of hazard, and shall be located 49 feet [or less if justified by risk assessment] horizontally from any part of a heater and any associated electrical equipment housed in industrial type enclosures:

  a) Pumps and compressors in flammable fluid service.

  b) Air-cooled heat exchangers.

  c) Sample points.

  d) Hydrocarbon vents and drains open or opened to atmosphere during normal operation. This shall include drains from fuel and pilot gas knockout drums. However, outlets from vents and drains that are opened only during plant shutdown or infrequent maintenance may be less than 49 feet from a heater, provided they are closed and plugged during normal operation.

  e) Control valves, meters, and filters on hydrocarbon gas or low flash point hydrocarbon liquid duty.

- Process or utility lines that operate at 1202°F or more including transfer lines to or from heaters, and gas turbine exhaust should be considered as permanent ignition sources and taken account of in any risk assessment.

- Insulation of lines above 1202°F should cover the lines completely, including flanges and joints. The effect of the insulation on flange bolt temperatures should, however, be checked, and the appropriate grade of bolting selected.

- Surface drains shall not be located directly under a heater.

## 13.5 Air-Cooled Heat Exchanger

As air coolers (sometimes called aerial coolers, or fin-fans) have extensive heat transfer surfaces, they are more vulnerable to failure under fire conditions than shell and tube exchangers, so should be located well away from fired equipment.

- The location of air-cooled heat exchangers shall be specifically considered with respect to areas of special fire risk. Such consideration shall include:

  a) The effect of the exchanger on air movement and increased fire spread.

  b) The possibility of failure of exchanger tubes releasing more combustible fluid to the fire.

  c) The design and operating conditions of adjacent process equipment, in particular sealing arrangements of pumps.

d) The height above any surface that can support a pool fire. See API RP 521 section 3.15.

- Minimum distance requirements between air-cooled heat exchangers and pumps:

  a) Air-cooled heat exchangers may be located at a minimum distance from pumps in hydrocarbon service with seals meeting recommendation of ISO 21049.

  b) For pumps on hydrocarbon duty where seals do not meet the requirements of ISO 21049, a greater distance is required by calculation of the range of a likely flammable release.

  c) The horizontal distance shall be measured at the pump or motor.

- Pumps should be located after considering overall piping and plot layout, pump NPSH availability, pump isolation, upstream and downstream inventories, and the consequences of fire spread and air cooler failure.

- The use of flanged joints in pipe racks under, or adjacent to, air-cooled heat exchangers should be avoided.

- Air-cooled heat exchangers should be located so that the hot air emitted is not a hazard or an inconvenience to personnel, nor adversely affects the operation of adjacent equipment.

- Air-cooled heat exchangers should, if practicable, be at least 49 feet horizontally from fired heaters, to minimize the possibility of circulation of hot air.

Air cooler units should be located at a suitable elevation to ensure maximum air flow. Piping should be top inlet and bottom outlet. No equipment should be installed immediately under air cooled heat exchangers or other equipment that is part of the heat exchanger system. No equipment should impede the exhaust air stream above the exchanger.

Air coolers should be located away from sources of heat to minimize the possibility of hot air recirculation. Air coolers must be spaced to prevent hot air recirculation from one cooler to another. Location of air coolers must be considered early in the plant layout to allow space for erection, support of piping headers (normally located on a post erected between the air cooler cells), and bundle maintenance.

Space should be allowed between air cooled exchangers for extension of structural steel members to support piping headers above the aerial coolers. For this reason, structural steel supports are often excluded from the vendor's scope of supply. Check this philosophy early in the design phase.

## 13.6 Shell and Tube Heat Exchanger

Shell and tube exchanger piping shall be arranged to provide clearance overhead at both bonnet and channel ends. Piping shall be designed with breakout spools at both bonnet and channel ends to enable removal by overhead lifting equipment. Sufficient space should be provided for the removal of tube bundles from shell and tube heat exchangers for maintenance, inspection or replacement.

The following shall be considered in locating the equipment:

- Shell and tube heat exchangers shall be located so that, when their tube bundles are withdrawn, they do not project into an emergency escape route or any road with unrestricted vehicle access.
- Shell and tube heat exchangers shall be arranged so that they can readily be dismantled for cleaning and maintenance.
- The spacing between heat exchanger shells shall allow sufficient unobstructed clearance for access for the bundle withdrawal equipment, and to permit access for shell flange gasket renewal.
- Additional clearance guidelines follow:

  a) Minimum clearances for exchangers with removable bundles shall be as follows:

     > At channel end:

     - Longitudinal – tube length plus 10 feet.
     - Overhead – 12 feet for exchangers with center line within 15 feet of grade.
     - A clear overhead for exchangers with center line more than 15 feet above grade.

     > At bonnet end:

     - Longitudinal – 10 feet.
     - Overhead – 10 feet.

  b) Minimum clearances for exchangers with fixed tube sheets are the same as above, except that longitudinal clearance may be provided at either the channel end or the bonnet end.

  c) Shell and tube exchangers shall not be stacked more than two high, except small diameter exchangers if total height above grade/platform does not exceed 11 feet.

## 13.7 Vessels

Layout of vessels needs to allow sufficient spacing for instruments, piping, operations, maintenance and firefighting access.

Vessels operating above 600°F or above auto-ignition temperature represent an increase risk of fire. Equipment located above vessels containing hydrocarbons operating above 600°F or above auto-ignition temperature (whichever is lower) should be protected by measures such as passive fire protection or water sprays.

Internally insulated vessels are more vulnerable to overheating and potential failure when exposed to external fires, so increased spacing is justified.

[Oil Search logo]

Blowdown and water disengaging drums should be located in a separate unit area to avoid involvement in process unit fires.

Vessels containing catalyst, resins, filters, cartridges or packing require space for bins or pallets and lifting facilities for filling / emptying of materials. Where a series of beds are involved, access to each bed will be required. Reactors have a substantial top platform with lifting facilities for removal of reactor head and catalyst loading. Reactors can have platforms attached or have an independent access structure.

Towers and columns shall be located in a way that all working platforms and manholes face the access road. Manways shall be on or about a common center line.

Permanent access should be provided to manholes and instrumentation on all vessels. Access to platforms from ladders should be by side stepping wherever possible.

Vertical vessels shall be placed on a common outside diameter parallel to the main pipe way, unless design conditions or accessibility dictate otherwise.

## 13.8 Air Intakes and Discharges

- Air intakes, including intakes to heating and ventilating systems, air compressors for process, instrument, plant and breathing air, and to gas turbines, shall be located as far as is practicable away from areas where air contamination by dust, or by flammable or toxic material can occur.
- Air intakes shall not be located in any area classified nor located above or below an area classified as Class I Division 1.
- Air intakes other than for breathing air may be located:
  - a) Not less than 9.8 feet above a Class I Division 2 area if the flammable vapor release is heavier than air.
  - b) Not less than 9.8 feet below a Class I Division 2 area if the flammable release is lighter than air.
  - c) In a Class I Division 2 area where a dedicated flammable gas detection and protection system is provided.

    The density of the flammable vapor shall be assumed to be at the expected operating temperature.

    Special process conditions may mean lighter than air materials behave heavier than air and heavier than air materials behave lighter than air.

- The location of air intakes shall consider the effects of:
  - a) Possible air contamination with dust or with flammable or toxic material.

b) Additional safety systems, e.g. equipment trips on detection of flammable material in the air intakes.

c) Reduced pressure on personnel working in the area.

Some air intakes can be tolerant of contaminants in the air. In these cases, it may be acceptable to locate the intake closer to the boundaries specified above.

However, there are cases when even a low level of contamination is unacceptable. In particular, the air intake for breathing air compressors must be sited where contamination is unlikely. The siting of breathing and instrument air compressors should consider possible expansion of the site, i.e. they should not be located where future processing plant may be constructed.

- Intakes and discharges shall be separated to prevent cross contamination by recirculation, taking into account natural wind effects. The distance between intakes and discharges shall be not less than 19.7 feet unless design provisions can be demonstrated to prevent recirculation.
- Air discharges from heating and ventilating systems from enclosed hazardous areas shall be considered as a source of hazardous release and shall be sited accordingly.
- Air discharges from heating and ventilating systems, and blow-off facilities associated with air compressors and fans, shall be located so that they do not cause a hazard or inconvenience to personnel nor affect the access to, and operation of, adjacent equipment.
- Gas turbine intakes must be located at an elevation at least 30 feet above grade to minimize snow and icing of filters.
- Gas turbine air intakes must be located in consideration of exhaust stack elevation and prevailing winds regarding possibility of exhaust entry into the intake.

## 13.9 Pressure Relief Devices

If atmospheric discharge from relief devices has been permitted and approved by the Company (see Sec. 7.4 – 4th bullet), flammable and toxic atmospheric discharges from the pressure relief devices shall comply with the listed guidelines below.

- Vapor from other low boiling point material storage vessels remote from process areas shall discharge to atmosphere not less than 6.6 feet above the pressure relief devices, and not less than 6.6 feet above any platform within a 9.7 feet radius.

# 14 Above-Ground Bulk Storage and Transfer Areas

## 14.1 General

- Layout and spacing of tanks within a tank farm shall take into consideration the following:

a) Characteristics of stored product.

b) Size of tank.

c) Maximum potential for fire radiation.

d) Boil over potential.

e) Business interruption consequences.

f) Prevailing wind direction.

g) Access of fire-fighting equipment.

h) Distances from adjoining properties.

i) Future expansions.

- For additional guidance on the layout of storage facilities, refer to API Std. 2610 and IP 19.
- Tankage areas should be laid out to meet the requirements of clause 9.1. Any proposal for larger plot sizes shall be subject to Company approval.

With large storage tanks, it may not be practicable to keep within these plot sizes.

- Bulk tanks storing hydrocarbons shall be located 250 feet, minimum, from any process unit.
- IP 15 classifies liquids as follows based on the closed-cup flash point:

a) Class I – Liquids that have flash points below 70°F.

b) Class II (1) – Liquids that have flash points from 70°F up to and including 131°F, handled below flash point.

c) Class II (2) – Liquids that have flash points from 70°F up to and including 131°F, handled at or above flash point.

d) Class III (1) – Liquids that have flash points above 131°F up to and including 212°F, handled below flash point.

e) Class III (2) – Liquids that have flash points above 131°F up to and including 212°F, handled at or above flash point.

f) Unclassified – Liquids that have flash points above 212°F.

Class 0 includes other similar liquefied flammable gases. Class II and III liquids are subdivided in accordance with the conditions under which they are handled. Class II (1) or III (1) refers to flammable liquids in Class II or III handled or stored at a temperature below the flash point. This is based on the maximum operating temperature of storage. At a temperature above the

flash point they fall into Class II (2) or III (2) and shall be treated as a Class I liquid. Non-flammable liquids shall be regarded as unclassified.

- NFPA 497 classifies liquids as follows:

  a) Class IA – Flammable liquids having flashpoints below 73°F and having a boiling point below 100°F.

  b) Class IB – Flammable liquids having flashpoints below 73°F and having a boiling point at or above 100°F.

  c) Class IC – Flammable liquids having flashpoints at or above 73°F and below 100°F.

  d) Class II – Combustible liquids having flashpoints at or above 100°F and below 140°F.

  e) Class IIIA – Combustible liquids having flashpoints at or above 140°F and below 200°F.

  f) Class IIIB – Combustible liquids having flashpoints at or above 200°F.

  g) Unstable Liquid – A liquid that, in the pure state or as commercially produced or transported, vigorously polymerizes, decomposes, undergoes condensation reaction, or becomes self-reactive under conditions of shock, pressure, or temperature.

  h) Stable Liquid – Any liquid not defined as unstable.

- Tanks shall be laid out to provide access for fire-fighting. There should be no more than two rows of tanks between adjacent access roads. Tanks of diameter 131 feet or greater should be arranged in a single row.

  The figure of 131 feet diameter is somewhat arbitrary, because fire-fighting is easier with a single-row tank layout, it may be considered preferable to group tanks slightly smaller than 131 feet diameter in a single row.

- Pumps associated with tankage operations shall not be located inside a bunded (diked) tank compound and should preferably be grouped together outside the bund (dike) provided this is practicable and economical.

  Normally, the spacing between pumps is determined by access requirements. However, if the pumps are on critical service, and failure would result in total shutdown of a major facility, then additional spacing or separation may be specified. An example of this critical duty is the main oil line (MOL) pumps on an oil-producing site, or the booster pumps on a major pipeline.

## 14.2 Class I, II, III and Unclassified Liquids

- Tank spacing shall comply with whichever is more stringent: NFPA 30, applicable national standards, or the following guidelines:

a) A minimum spacing of 0.5 of the largest tank diameter (0.5D) shall be provided between any two tanks.

The recommended spacing should be regarded as a minimum, especially if the financial consequences of the fire affecting adjacent tanks would be especially severe, e.g. if loss of a second tank would result in a shutdown of production or processing operations. Greater spacing is also required on sloping ground. With a separate bunded (diked) compound for each large crude oil tank, the spacing between tanks is likely to be greater than the recommended minimum of 0.5 times tank diameter. With substantially symmetrical arrangements and typical bund (dike) heights, the tank spacing would probably be about 1 diameter or greater.

- Liquids defined by IP as Class III (1) and unclassified liquids (NFPA Class IIIA liquids stored/handled below their flash point and IIIB liquids) should be stored in a separate compound from IP Class I, II, or III (2) liquids (NFPA Class I and II as well as IIIA liquids stored above their flashpoint).

- Aboveground tanks for liquids defined by IP as Class I, II, and III (2) (NFPA Class I and II as well as IIIA liquids stored/handled above their flashpoint) shall be completely surrounded by a bund or bunds (dike or dikes).

  a) The height of the bund (dike) as measured from outside ground level shall be sufficient to afford protection for personnel when engaged in fire-fighting, and the bund (dike) shall be located so that approach can be made to a tank fire to allow use of mobile fire-fighting equipment. The bund (dike) shall not restrict ventilation in the tankage area.

  b) Intermediate walls of lesser height than the main bunds (dikes) may be provided to divide tankage into groups of a convenient size, to contain small spillages and act as firebreaks.

- In no case shall the number of tanks in any bunded (diked) compound exceed 6.

- The combined capacity of multiple tanks in a single bund (dike) should not exceed 2.1 E6 ft² regardless if the tanks are conventional fixed roof tanks in a single bund though at the maximum limit would be allowed). Early discussions with local safety and environmental regulatory agencies may further influence bund (dike) capacity calculations.

- The net capacity of a bunded (diked) tank compound shall be equivalent to the capacity of the largest tank in the compound plus 10%. The net capacity of a bunded (diked) tank compound shall be calculated by deducting from the total capacity:

  a) The volume of all tanks, other than the largest, below the level of the top of the bund (dike).

  b) The volume of all intermediate bunds (dikes).

- If there is an absolute requirement to deviate from the guidelines in clause 11.2, advice should be sought from the Company fire advisor.

# APPENDIX A:    Guidance on Clearances

## A.1:    General

- Items such as the following shall not block operating aisles or maintenance access.

  a) Projecting piping.

  b) Valve spindles (valve wheels).

  c) Instrumentation.

  d) Instrument air manifolds.

  e) Local panels.

  f) Transducer stations.

  g) Pneumatic and electrical junction boxes.

  h) Grouped steam tracing supply and condensate manifolds.

- A minimum of 3.3 feet clear working spaces shall be maintained around equipment that requires frequent servicing such as pumps, exchangers, control valves, instruments, tower manways, etc. Also refer to A.3. (3rd bullet) for more stringent minimum horizontal clearances for specific applications.

- Horizontal and vertical clearances shall meet or exceed those recommended by equipment suppliers for maintenance and operations.

## A.2:    Vertical

- Overhead clearances shall not be less than:

  a) 12 feet if carry deck crane access is required.

  b) 15 feet if cherry picker (13.6 to 16.3 t [15 to 18 ton] crane) access is required.

  c) 10 feet if 1.8 to 2.7 t (2 to 3 ton) motorized forklift access is required.

  d) 12 feet for main pipe racks.

  e) 7 feet from grade or elevated platform for personnel passageway.

  f) 10 feet from pavement for secondary roads (cars, pickups, no mobile equipment).

  g) 16 feet from pavement for main roadways without crane access.

  h) 18 feet from pavement for main roadways and, if necessary, over access ways for heavy equipment.

- Overhead clearance from top of rail for railroads to be confirmed by national (i.e. federal) and local railway clearances requirements as these vary from site to site.

## A.3: Horizontal

- Main access way horizontal clearance for mobile equipment shall not be less than:

  a) 20 feet.

  b) 10 feet if mobile equipment is to service a specific piece of process equipment and the mobile equipment can operate "straight-in" (no turns).

- Minimum horizontal clearance to piping, equipment, and instrumentation shall be as follows unless otherwise approved by the Company:

  a) 5 feet for aisles at equipment and machinery.

  b) 4 feet for operating and maintenance areas at grade.

  c) 4 feet for workspace on platforms servicing the heads of shell/tube exchangers mounted on a structure. Also refer to clause 13.5.

  d) 3 feet for workspace on platforms servicing manways, in addition, the center line of the manway should be at an elevation of 5 feet from the platform.

  e) 7 feet for workspace around compressors, turbines, and large motors shall be provided for operating and maintenance clearances to handle casings, rotors, crankshafts, flywheels, etc. This minimum spacing shall be validated by the equipment supplier and approved by the Company.

  Note: Clearances shall be measured from the furthest projection on equipment, including associated pipe work with its flanges and insulation boxes, filters, valves in their open position, drains, cabling, instruments, etc., between floor level and 6.6 feet above floor level.

# APPENDIX B: Hazard Classification of Process Operations for Spacing Requirements

## B.1: General

Processes and operations can be empirically classified into one of the three classes according to their explosion and fire hazards: Moderate, Intermediate or High.

These classifications do not substitute for proper hazard identification or analysis method. They are intended only to be used in determining spacing requirements. Many additional factors and judgments can still affect the class to which the process is assigned such as:

- Vessel and unit size
- Flammable liquid holdup
- Gas vs. liquid phase
- Pressures
- Temperatures
- Loss history
- Interdependency
- Lead time to rebuild
- Market share

Therefore, if any of the above features of a process are higher than normal, raise it to a higher class. Typical process examples are given for each classification.
