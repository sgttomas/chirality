# 1 Introduction

## 1.1 Objectives

The purpose of this philosophy is to define the minimum technical requirements for the piping design and layout of process and utility piping systems including associated equipment and structures within modular installation. It also includes making provision for areas such as construction laydown, temporary construction man camps, and access roads. In this context equipment includes power generation, switchgear and fire suppression equipment. It also covers overall arrangement of supporting structure and consideration of construction/installation methods plus hook-up and pre-start-up of the facility.

Safety in design is a key factor associated with the layout of the facility. The principal design criterion is to minimize or eliminate hazards in an environment where risk mitigation is critical to overall safety of the operating facilities, site personnel and the environment. The plant layout is to establish acceptable methods for conducting quantitative risk assessment associated with explosion, fire, or toxic releases that may affect personnel within occupied structures, due to their proximity to process facility. These criteria enable development of an initial design that will meet facility siting risk assessments to the requirements of API - RP 752/753 that is recognized as a valid approach for controlling the risk to building occupants. The piping general layout philosophy is to provide a framework and basis for the design of the facility.

This guideline covers the general plant layout principles that contribute towards a safe and operable plant. This document represents the starting point on the plant layout development. Project specific amendments will be issued to supplement this document where necessary.

## 1.2 Design Criteria

The facilities shall be designed to standards as indicated in Section 2.0, Regulations, Codes and Standards. Piping engineering and design shall be in accordance with In-Plant Piping Design Criteria (OGE-PICRT-000001). Deviations from these criteria shall be identified and communicated to the Company. Where the requirement is not specified in these criteria, the Contractor shall consult the Company.

Users of this document are strongly advised to review and investigate the possibility of applying the latest edition of all relevant regulatory requirements, and the supplied Company Specification and Standards. When there is a conflict between design documents, the Company shall be notified at the earliest opportunity for resolution.

## 1.4 Scope of Work

Plant layout shall identify and adopt the safest cost-effective layout that meets the requirements of the process design, safe operation/maintenance, emergency response action and evacuation.

### 1.4.1 Modularization

The Company recommends the design to adopt a modular approach aimed to construct and commission, to the maximum extent possible in the fabrication yard, prior to shipping to the site. By using this approach, the goal is to reduce the field construction costs and start-up time, while keeping the majority of fabrication in a controlled setting. Modules shall be designed taking into account the maximum practicable size and weight, often depending on the physical limitations of the transportation route to main site location and available capacity of heavy lift Self-Propelled Modular Transportation (SPMT) equipment. Additionally, the maximum number of Sealift Modules that can be offloaded and installed in a single year shall be continually monitored in order to ensure safe, successful barge access and unloading during limited timeframe provided for the project.

Once the extent of the plant to be modularized is defined, the size of the individual modules needs to be developed based on economic criteria and physical limitations. However, it is crucial that a decision on the detail of each module be made early on in the project to ensure the establishment of appropriate execution strategy throughout the project phase. The equipment arrangement on the module is complementary to the needs of segregation for the purpose of loss prevention, as high risk process facilities (i.e., separation/dehydration, gas compression) are grouped together and away from low risk but critical utility and occupied areas (i.e., control room and shop/offices). Utility and accommodation facilities are grouped remotely from these exposures.

## 3    Layout Design Principles

Plant layouts shall maximize safety and exposure protection from the spread of fire, with ease of operation and maintenance consistent with economical design. Requirements for future expansion shall be recognized and reasonable allowances made.

Space between units and equipment needs to be provided where necessary for safety, maintenance and personnel accessibility, but not wasted, as it usually adds to the capital and operating costs.

The main principles for safe facilities design and operation are:

- Minimizing the likelihood of uncontrollable releases of hydrocarbons and other hazardous materials.
- Facilities and equipment are accessible for all operational and maintenance related task.
- Consideration for Area Electrical Classification .
- Minimizing ignition sources.
- Prevention of fire escalation.
- Prevention of facility and equipment damage.
- Providing for personnel protection and escape.
- Available access for fire-fighting equipment and facilities including mobile (i.e., passive incipient protection or full fire-fighting).
- Consideration for Site Security.
- Facilities that are safe and cost effective to construct, transport, install, hook-up & commission and operate.
- Balance of site manpower loading consideration.
- Critical emergency facilities are accessible for operators to perform emergency shutdown actions to prevent or mitigate potential fire explosion consequences.

### 3.1    Layout Design Evolution

Layout design develops and evolves over the project phases as more detailed information becomes available.

Consideration shall be given, with respect to layout design, to periodic drilling activities and other simultaneous operations potentially occurring on the pad. Potential for future plant expansions should be identified and access/approach for those potential expansions considered as part of the plant layout design.

A detailed blast siting study shall be performed to provide a detailed analysis and verification report on safe distances between occupied modules and blast source.

## 3.2 Conceptual Layout

During the Pre-FEED phase of the project, the design considers broad information of the expected installation, and its basic criteria. These may include:

- Expected location of the facility.
- Overall facilities to be accommodated (stick build or modular)
- Basic positioning of key plants/units
- Linkages if any to existing facilities (including any simultaneous operations issues)
- Expected pipe-rack and incoming pipeline (elevated, ground level)
- Likely plant orientation.
- Predominant wind direction that impacts flare and snow drifting.

The objective for this project phase is to produce an overall conceptual layout.

Distinguishing between "stick build" and "modular design" early on will help in overall Facility Design and execution planning by providing insight to requirements for clearances, access, space allocation, etc., necessary to layout an efficient and maintainable arrangement of equipment, buildings, piperacks, loadout areas, etc.

The pre-FEED engineering design of the facilities is to generate permitting documents and information, develop initial cost estimates for the concepts, and form sound basis for the FEED stage of the project.

## 3.3 Preliminary Layout

The plant layout then progresses to more rigorous considerations of the proposed sites and plant requirements, taking into account the identified hazards and design specifications. A number of layout case study options may be developed for screening, site selection and preliminary costings as part of the pre-FEED phase.

## 3.4 Layout Definition

During FEED Phase, after the site has been selected, along with preferred process and layout options, layouts for equipment and modules are defined further as information on equipment sizes becomes available.

## 3.5 Detailed Layout

The full detailed layout developed during Detailed Design phase integrates the requirements of all engineering disciplines including structural, electrical and instrument and process. Detailed layout of piping design requirements shall adhere to Plant Piping Design Specification (OGE-PISPE-000005).

# 4 Layout Considerations

This section outlines the key aspects to be considered when preparing preliminary layouts during pre-FEED phase. Preliminary layouts are influenced by the following factors:

- Type of plant, process units.
- Design specifications.
- Overall site size and shape, natural slope.
- Topography.
- Permitting constraint.
- Site soil and local environment conditions.
- The selected construction and module movement method.

These should be used in conjunction with the project applicable codes and standards, regulatory consideration and customer requirements.

## 4.1 Layout Studies

Layout design development is supported by a number of studies and reviews, including:

- Process requirements.
- Hazard identification (HAZID Study) and risk assessments – both qualitative and quantitative.
- Operability reviews.
- Constructability review.
- Blast overpressure venting studies may be used in developing the plant layout.
- A maintainability and logistics evaluation for storage and materials handling requirements.
- Truck movement and materials handling study to determine best location for plant entries and exits.
- Periodic drilling activities.
- Site manning level for 24 hrs basis (i.e., ops, maintenance, regulatory testing/monitoring).
- Location trade-off studies that identify which equipment poses the higher potential risk.
- Layout design requirements need to focus on achieving adequate safe spacing of equipment and hazardous elements in the space environment (e.g., minimum horizontal and vertical clearances and minimum fire separation allowances).
- Layout should allow for sufficient separation of buildings and temporary buildings to account for hazardous inventories, especially those likely to arise during start-up, shut-down and emergencies.

- Locating emergency support services, (i.e. stand-by generators, communication module) outside or away from process equipment areas to ensure critical facilities are protected from possible fire or explosion.

- The location of storage areas for hazardous materials needs to consider blast overpressure contours and sympathetic detonation effects.

- Large equipment can have impact on layout constructability and operability factors, including equipment maintenance access ways and lay-down areas.

- Any intentions for future expansion.

## 4.2 Plant Type, Process/Utility Units

- Plant layout is influenced by the type of plant (both the industry and technology utilized) and the kind of process units and associated utility/support facilities it contains. Generally, process/utility units are laid out according to the process flow, except where certain ignition sources or hazardous materials need to be separated. This arrangement minimizes the uncontrolled transfer of flammable materials, which is desirable both for economics and safety.

- Each process/utility unit must be sufficiently separated from the others to allow units to be shut down for maintenance and turnaround, while others are still operating. Separation distances also define fire risk areas and limit the maximum area reasonably expected to be totally involved in a single fire.

- Consideration shall be given to avoidance of spurious trips initiated or caused by potential vapor release areas, high point vents, pig launcher/receiver, located in areas with gas detectors.

- At this stage of the project, some nominal placement of major equipment items will occur within each process/utility unit and other associated facilities. This placement will be further refined during FEED, once the preferred site location and process have been selected.

## 4.3 Prevailing Wind

Prevailing wind conditions influence the design considerations for the location of the flare or vent stack. It is vital that reliable wind data is obtained for the proposed location of the facility. In this respect, safe and effective design requirements will typically require the following:

- That the plant is safely ventilated.

- Safe areas and locations are provided that are up-wind of any potential hazardous releases.

- Air intake, including intakes to heating and ventilating system, air compressors for process, instrument, plant and breathing air, and gas turbines, shall be located as far as practicable away from areas where air contamination by dust or by flammable or toxic material can occur.

- Any plumes of airborne gas and/or particulates are dissipated away from critical areas of the plant and other occupied/public areas as effectively as possible by the prevailing wind.

- Radiant heat emission levels from flares, and/or accidental ignition of vents, do not exceed the limits outlined in Section 4.4.

Prevailing wind conditions also influence the placement of uncovered stockpiles of bulk solids which can be a source of dust. Avoid placing critical air intakes for both HVAC and turbines/engines in close proximity of heavy traffic areas.

Because prevailing wind direction impacts so many dependent design decisions, it shall be included on all site/plot plans.

## 4.4 Flare and Vents

A vent is a potential gas fuel source and a potential liquid fuel source due to carry-over from the vent. Vents shall be routed to flare for safe disposal unless otherwise stated by process engineer. This is also true for a flare, if the pilot fails. An adequate scrubber and knock-out drum should be provided for all flares and vents to minimize the possibility of liquid carryover.

Both flares and vents may become potential sources of toxic vapors. Dispersion of gases from vents must not create a problem with air intakes for turbines.

The potential danger from radiant heat from a flare, as well as from accidental ignition of a vent, should be taken into account for all potential wind directions on flare and vent locations.

Flares, engine exhausts and other similar ignition sources shall be located to avoid ignition of normal or accidental gas releases; downwind from safe areas. Use of dispersion calculations should be considered.

The height of elevated flares is also determined by the radiant heat calculations. Noise from, and visibility of the flare needs consideration when positioning the unit relative to the property line.

Flare and vent systems shall comply with API Std. 521 (Pressure-Relieving and De-pressuring Systems) or equivalent.

## 4.5 Storage Tanks

Large storage tanks are potential fire hazards and shall be located away from occupancies to prevent liquid and or gasses from flowing in the event of leak regardless of location. Normally, tanks are located downwind of other areas. For additional guidance on layout of storage facility, refer to API Std 2610 and IP 19 (Fire Precautions at Petroleum Refineries and Bulk Storage Installations). Tanks spacing shall comply with NFPA 30, and applicable national standards.

Layout and spacing of tanks within a tank farm shall take into consideration the following:

[Oil Search logo]

- Characteristics of stored products.
- Size of tank.
- Maximum potential for fire radiation.
- Boil over potential.
- Business interruption consequences.
- Prevailing wind direction.
- Access of fire-fighting equipment.
- Distances from adjoining facility.
- Future expansion.

Generally, tanks occupy large areas of the plot. Storage containing the same product shall be grouped together and spaced with a bund (dike) area containing any leakage. Sizing of bund (dike) areas shall be established as a priority early in the site layout. The height of the bund (dike) as measured from outside ground level shall be sufficient to afford protection for personnel when engaged in fire-fighting.

Separation from process areas and other occupied site facilities is important for minimizing the risk to people, assets and the environment. This separation prevents/mitigates potential fires and explosions from propagating to adjacent facilities and escalating in consequences.

## 4.6 Construction Method

Plant layout is influenced by the intended method of construction, whether the plant is being constructed on-site, or whether modules will be fabricated elsewhere and then assembled on-site. Modular design is popular when considering remote sites, severe weather and the availability of a skilled labor force. Project schedules can be shorter because site civil works can proceed in parallel with module fabrication, and site assembly can be done in more favorable weather conditions.

Modular construction requires a separate design approach; it's not effective to design by conventional methods and then divide it into modules.

## 4.7 Access Roads

Roads are an important part of the design of a plant, as they provide access/egress and separation during normal operation as well as emergency access.

Consideration to the following needs to be given:

- Primary roads shall have sufficient horizontal and vertical clearance to permit the unobstructed travel of major vehicles.

- Secondary roads are those between process units used mainly for maintenance and turnaround activities, firefighting access and daily surveillance (i.e., pad operator rounds).

- Vehicle parking areas including access both to and from the property onto the public service roadway system.

- Consider area/space allocation of dirty and contaminated snow in a designated stock-pile or storage area of frequent snow removal.

Some roads may require road movement of feedstock/materials (e.g. movement of bulk solids [sand removal]). These roads may either be part of the primary road system or a separate dedicated road system.

Additional access ways shall be provided for controlled traffic as follows:

To all heavy equipment that may require periodic removal for maintenance

- At large compressors, pumps and gas turbines as needed to allow vehicles such as flatbed trucks to enter a dropout area and remove components via mobile equipment or permanent handling facilities.

- Under pipe-racks as needed to permit crane access for routine maintenance and removal of equipment.

- To outlying areas, storage and load-out areas such as pig.

- Around the manned site perimeter with at least two access points to public roads. Drillsites only have one access ways.

- Around other equipment as required for maintenance.

- Personnel occupied buildings shall be near road access to the pad, to support safe evacuation without having to cross through a fire or explosion area.

## 4.8 Infrastructures and Other Buildings

Some equipment may need to be located inside buildings, but this needs to be justified as it incurs additional cost and can create additional hazards. Typically, buildings are used where the process, equipment, materials or activities are sensitive to exposure of various kinds (e.g. heat, cold, moisture, contamination, damage, noise, etc).

Since ventilation in a building is generally less than that outdoors, a leak of flammable or toxic material tends to disperse more slowly and a hazardous concentration is more likely to build up. Also, if an explosion occurs, the blast pressure generated tends to be much higher and results in more damage. Ventilation and/or dust collection systems are required in enclosed storage buildings as well to assist with removal of mobile equipment exhaust and any off-gassing from the product, and to minimize dust.

[Oil Search logo]

All occupied buildings need to be located away from process areas, upwind of flare, vent and fired equipment, and outside of blast overpressure contours (Blast Radius/Radiation Zone) to protect personnel in the event of vapor release, fire or explosion. Occupied building within the blast radius zone shall be design for this blast overpressure (i.e., blast resistance), reinforced and or equipped to achieve tolerable risk level within the distance of blast zone.

Building design and orientation should also address environmental considerations such as prevailing weather direction (wind, dust, rain, snow); as well as the ability to remove snow drifting from pad and provide adequate shelter for entrances.

## 4.9 Piperacks

Separation between piperacks and process equipment provides access for operations, maintenance and it reduces exposure from adjacent fires. Process equipment should never be located under onsite piperacks.

Separation distances from pumps are measured as the horizontal distance between the edge of a piperack and the nearest part of the pump (usually the stuffing box). There are no limitations on spacing pump drivers from piperacks.

Where practicable piping which interconnects modules, or multiple trains, it should be routed such that it will not remain in service through plant areas which are shut down for turnaround.

Above ground pipelines pipeways are designed in order to interconnect above ground piping between modules. The piperack sealift module or Pre-Assembled Units (PAU's) will also include interconnecting walkways to enable accessing modules at floor elevation.

## 4.10 Trenching

To enable clear access for day to day activities, plot maintenance and future development, trenches may be utilized to run interconnecting lines underground between the process/utility plant facilities and infrastructure facilities.

## 4.11 Secondary Spill Containment

A bund (or dike) is an embankment or wall of brick, stone, concrete or other impervious material, which surrounds the entire perimeter of a storage compound and provides a barrier to retain solids and liquids.

Bunds/dikes are designed to contain spillages and leaks of the substances being stored, prevent contact with incompatible materials, and to facilitate clean-up operations. As well as being used to prevent pollution of the receiving environment, bunds/dikes are also used for fire protection, product recovery and process isolation.

Facilities that should have bunded/diked areas include:

[Oil Search logo]

- Storage facilities for hazardous chemicals or petroleum.
- Electrical transformers containing oil.
- Facilities used to transfer stored liquids (such as transport facilities).
- Drum storage areas, either temporary or permanent.
- Processing areas.
- Any facilities that store or collect substances other than water or uncontaminated storm-water.
- Any location where spills are common, including transfer points, workshops, factories, service stations, wash bays, and other areas in which a material is transferred from its container.

When locating more than one storage container within the same bund/dike, the compatibility of their contents must be checked. Incompatible materials must not be contained within the same bund/dike. Refer to local codes and regulations.

While bunds/dikes are more commonly required for liquid storage compounds, they may also be required for solids storage compounds particularly if storm-water runoff from the storage container is classed as contaminated.

## 4.12 Loading and Unloading Facility

Loading and unloading facilities handling flammable commodities shall be kept at a safe distance from accommodation spaces and control station. Though an inherently safer design is recommended for flammable material storage and process units, options for separate plot have been ruled out. Additional risk reduction measures are incorporated, and a comprehensive risk assessment has been conducted.

The loading and unloading facilities should be downwind or crosswind from process units and sources of ignition based on the prevailing wind. If this is impracticable, the loading/unloading area may be placed upwind at a prudent distance deemed by a thorough hazard analysis.

Access from the site boundary to and from loading and unloading areas should be, preferably located at the pad entrance to minimize inter-plant traffic.

## 4.13 Laydown Areas

Designated Lay-down areas are areas specifically positioned to enable equipment maintenance to be carried out. These are normally assigned for maintenance work around machinery and vessels and are serviced by the fixed or mobile cranes.

Permanent overhead sling points or monorails should be provided to assist horizontal traversing of heavy items to cantilevered rigging beams movement to laydown areas for crane access.

Lay-down areas shall be located so as to minimize the risk of load handling or dropped object damage to systems and structures.

The need for load handling above pressurized hydrocarbon equipment, flammable inventories, and equipment important for safety shall be avoided as far as possible. Suitable impact protection shall be provided where such lifting cannot be avoided.

Lay-down areas should be located outside hazardous areas and should be provided with heavy-duty barriers to prevent damage to adjacent equipment.

## 4.14 Maintenance Consideration

Adequate space needs to be provided in layout designs to enable the following maintenance operations to be carried out when required:

- Removal of tubes from fired heaters and heat exchangers.
- Full maintenance of compressors, generators, cylinders, turbines etc.
- Replacing pump, pump internals, etc.
- Removal of vertical turbines or pumps.
- Opening and removing inspection plates and manways
- Pig insertion and removal and temporary pigging equipment.
- Catalyst removal and recharge.
- Replacing/cleaning filter elements.
- Overhead access to critical large control valves such that actuators can be removed and the valve(s) rebuilt in place.
- Platform or module deck access to control valves, transmitter, etc.

Equipment shall be located with adequate access for mobile handling equipment during maintenance. Equipment layout shall avoid the need to lift heavy pieces of frequently maintained equipment during unit operation over in-service process lines or equipment or provide passive protection.

During the construction and drilling activities, consideration needs to be given to site facilities; these may include the following:

- Temporary construction offices and temporary construction facilities.

## 4.16 Provision for Future Expansion

Some consideration is often given to the possibility of future expansion of the plant within the site. While these provisions can add costs, some minor, inexpensive adjustments can often be made to aid later expansion.

Space for probable future equipment shall be designated and located adjacent to the related equipment. The allocation of such space shall be addressed during the initial project risk assessment in order to identify potential hazards associated with probable future equipment.

## 4.17 Pipelines

The interconnection pipelines shall comply with the requirements of codes and standards as well as the required regulatory compliance. Pipelines shall be designed and routed and isolated at the battery limit or any location driven by safety for containment of isolation scenarios.

## 4.18 Battery Limit Isolation

Battery Limit stations are often provided at process unit boundaries to facilitate the quick isolation of interconnecting pipeline in an emergency. If all boundary isolations can be initiated from the one location, this limits the escalation risk of an event.

Battery limits are also utilized to designate separation of ownership.
