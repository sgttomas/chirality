# 1300 Cl₂ Guidelines

**Abstract**

This section establishes procedures for the safe handling and storage of liquid chlorine in commercial containers of one-ton capacity and smaller, and for the application of associated equipment, for both new and existing facilities. It also provides good engineering practices for the design and installation of piping in chlorine service.

## 1310 Introduction

This section provides the Company's minimum standards for the design and installation of new facilities using commercial liquid chlorine containers of one-ton capacity and smaller, chlorinators and associated equipment, and piping. In the Company, chlorine is used primarily as a biocide in cooling water treatment.

This section may also be used to upgrade existing facilities.

The Chlorine Institute's *Chlorine Manual*, the National Safety Council (NSC) Data Sheet I-207, and other references listed in Section 1380 were used in preparing this section. Refer to these resources as appropriate when applying the recommendations of this engineering guideline.

This section does not cover bulk chlorine shipping containers such as tank cars, tank trucks or barges.

## 1311 Nature of Chlorine

Chlorine is classified as a nonflammable gas that liquefies under pressure. The gas has a characteristic pungent and suffocating odor and a greenish-yellow color. It is about two-and-one-half times as heavy as air. The liquid is amber in color and is about one-and-one-half times as heavy as water.

Both liquid and gaseous chlorine will react vigorously and explosively with hydrogen to form hydrogen chloride. Reaction with hydrogen sulfide forms hydrogen chloride and sulfur; reaction with ammonia or ammonium compounds can form various mixtures of chloramines, such as NCl₃, which is an explosive compound. Reaction with hydrocarbons, alcohols, and ethers can be explosive. While dry chlorine does not corrode most metals, it can become very corrosive when moist or heated.

## 1312 Summary

Chlorine is a hazardous substance. When designing facilities for chlorine service, rigorous practices are warranted. A failure may release liquid chlorine, which can quickly vaporize and produce large volumes of chlorine gas. Because it is heavier than air, chlorine gas collects at low points and in poorly ventilated areas. Chlorine can be a serious hazard to personnel at low atmospheric concentrations. Minimizing the potential for release of chlorine requires safe, isolated storage and careful handling, tiedown and hookup of the containers by knowledgeable personnel.

## 1313 Key Emergency Actions—Leaking Container

- Alert downwind personnel and facilities.
- Rotate container so only gas leaks and, if possible, move the container to a safe location.
- Use emergency repair kit—obtain from storage.

- Use water to knock down and direct vapor. Do not spray water directly onto the leak or onto a liquid chlorine pool.

#### 1314 Key Emergency Actions—Fire

- Alert downwind personnel and facilities.
- Keep containers cool. Fusible plugs melt at 158°F.
- Remove containers from fire zone.

## 1330 Special Problems in Chlorine Systems

### 1331 Physiological and Health Effects

The Company's MSDS 646 should be consulted for the OSHA exposure standard, physiological and health effects, additional safety information and first aid procedures.

Chlorine gas is potentially lethal. The degree of danger depends upon the concentration in the air and on the duration of exposure. The physiological effect of gaseous

chlorine ranges from moderate irritation of mucous membranes to difficult breathing and death.

Chlorine as a liquid is also hazardous to personnel. Liquid chlorine in contact with the skin will produce severe burns and blisters. In contact with the eyes it can cause blindness.

## 1332 First Aid and Personnel Protection

Plant personnel who handle chlorine containers and operate chlorination equipment should be trained in first aid, and safe operating and emergency response procedures. Procedures should be included for such activities as:

- Changing containers
- Using container emergency repair kits
- Vapor control and fire fighting

For these and similar activities use impervious neoprene or rubber clothing and a breathing-air apparatus with a vapor-proof, full-facepiece mask. Never attempt a rescue without wearing proper respiratory protection.

Safety shower/eyewash units must be installed as stipulated in the Company's *Safety in Designs Manual* and maintained in good working order.

Safety shower/eyewash units, breathing-air apparatus and protective clothing should be within 50 feet of the chlorine containers. Backup breathing-air apparatus and protective clothing should be provided at other readily accessible locations.

When appropriate, atmospheric monitoring of chlorine releases and sounding of an alarm should be considered.

## 1333 Chlorine Flammability

Chlorine is not flammable but it will support combustion. Chlorine containers are very vulnerable in a fire; cylinders and one-ton containers are fitted with fusible metal plugs that have a melting point between 158°F and 165°F. The purpose of the fusible plug is to allow the pressure to release rather than have the container rupture at high temperature. Chlorine cylinders and one-ton containers do not have conventional pressure relief valves. Therefore, care must be taken in locating and storing chlorine containers to mitigate their risk to fire exposure and release of chlorine.

## 1334 Chlorine Leaks

Only specially trained and equipped personnel should ever attempt emergency repairs on chlorine containers. Emergency repair kits approved by the Chlorine Institute (kit A for cylinders and kit B for one-ton containers) should be available and personnel should be trained in their use. Emergency repair kits may be stored in a central location.

It is a good safety practice to check frequently for leaks. A mist of aqueous ammonia vapor, when mixed with chlorine gas, will form a white cloud (ammonium chloride). A commercial 26-degree Baumé aqueous ammonia solution in an atomizer may be used to snoop for leaks. Avoid getting aqueous ammonia directly on brass or copper.

Emergency repair kits should be used to handle container leaks. Other techniques used for leak mitigation follow.

## Container Valve Leaks

A container valve leak can usually be stopped by tightening the bonnet packing nut with a special wrench that is supplied by the chlorine shipper for this specific purpose. Tighten the bonnet packing nut just enough to stop the leakage. Note that chlorine container valves are designed to achieve either a full open or full closed position with a single turn of the stem. They can be damaged if turned beyond this point. The valve should unseat when the end of the special wrench is struck with the heel of the hand.

## Container Body Leaks

If a container body develops a leak, turn the container so that the opening discharges gas rather than liquid. This will reduce the amount of chlorine discharged to the environment. Never immerse a container in water.

Chlorine cylinders deliver gas in the vertical or upright position. One-ton containers positioned horizontally have two valves on one end that are normally vertically aligned; the upper valve delivers gas and the lower valve delivers liquid. When a one-ton container is rolled over with a valve inadvertently left open, the flow will cycle between gas and liquid according to the valve orientation on the container. The most common cause of chlorine leakage has been the mishandling of containers.

## Associated Piping Leaks

If a leak develops in the piping, isolate the containers by closing the container valve. Always purge chlorine lines before attempting any repair. Nitrogen may be used as a purge gas. Safely discharge the purge gas and chlorine into an alkaline solution through a pipe or weighted rubber hose.

Chlorine can be absorbed in aqueous solutions of caustic soda (NaOH) or soda ash (Na₂CO₃). The recommended mixtures in Figure 1300-1 are given by courtesy of the Chlorine Institute.

Avoid the discharge of chlorinated water into drainage systems where it may react with hydrocarbons and corrode the drainage system.

## 1335 Accidental Releases

Federal law (CERCLA) requires that the release to the environment of hazardous substances such as chlorine be immediately reported to allow necessary emergency response to protect the public and environment. Any chlorine release of 10 pounds (1.2 gallons, 49.9 SCF) or more within a 24-hour period must be reported to the National Response Center (NRC) in Washington, D.C. (telephone 1-800-424-8802) and, if exposure occurs to persons outside of the facility, to the state and local emergency response agencies required by Federal Law SARA—Title III. Additional state and local agencies may also require notification.

## 1336 Materials Considerations

Materials selection must take into account the possibility of mechanical damage as well as service conditions. Dry chlorine at room temperature is noncorrosive to carbon steel, copper and the nickel alloys. However, the frequent changing of chlorine containers exposes the piping internals to atmospheric moisture. The corrosion products resulting from the use of carbon steel piping will foul the downstream chlorination equipment.

Chlorine can be handled in Monel piping, tubing, and valves. Container "pigtails" can be cadmium plated copper tubing and should be rated at least 500 psig minimum working pressure. Container "pigtails" should be capped or plugged when out of service to mitigate the intrusion of moisture.

Few alloys can continuously resist wet chlorine. PVC may be considered for the parts of a chlorination system that are continuously wet with, for instance, chlorinated water.

Lead gaskets are used for the container isolating valves. A good practice is to use new lead gaskets when placing a container into service.

Be sure all packing, seals, and gasketing are compatible with chlorine service. Teflon and Viton are acceptable materials.

Consult with your materials engineer for guidance if the recommendations provided in this practice are not suitable for your application.

## 1337 Chlorine Storage

Chlorine containers should be stored outside in a secure, well ventilated area. Provide suitable platforms and supports to hold the containers, and keep them free of standing water. Platforms should be large enough for inspection and emergency repairs.

One-ton containers may require a monorail or crane with a special lifting beam. The containers can be set on roller trunnions to provide support, maintain fixed spacing, and facilitate container rotation in the event of a leak. All 150-lb cylinders should be secured with safety straps or chains and one-ton containers with positive hold-down clamps to prevent inadvertent movement during servicing or in an earthquake.

Minimize the number of chlorine containers that are distributed to the sites where chlorine is used. Spare containers with their associated valves tend to increase the possibility of a chlorine leak. Full and empty containers should be separated from each other as well as segregated from other compressed gas containers and hydrocarbon chemicals.

Usually a four-week supply at a consolidated storage area will be adequate; consult with purchasing regarding optimum delivery schedule to hold storage to a minimum. Chlorine containers should be used on a first-in, first-out basis.

# 1340 Design and Construction Requirements

## 1341 Dispersion Modeling and Risk Assessment

Dispersion modeling may be performed to determine potential chlorine concentrations in air in the event of an accidental release. Modeling results and risk assessment may be used to develop guidelines on design criteria needed to mitigate potential exposure to the community or plant personnel. Possible design criteria affected by the risk assessment are the quantity of chlorine stored and where the chlorine user should be located within the plant. Modeling results may also be used to develop emergency response plans for responding to accidental releases.

Concentrations resulting from a release are highly specific to release rates and meteorological conditions. Furthermore, releases of liquid chlorine forms fine liquid droplets (an aerosol) as well as vapor. The presence of the aerosol makes the chlorine cloud denser than air, which permits the cloud to travel a substantial distance downwind. Each situation should be modeled on a case-by-case basis. The Health, Environment and Safety Group at CRTC has a license to use a model developed by Energy Analysts Inc. called the Energy Analysts Hazards Analysis Package (EAHAP) which is specifically designed to predict concentrations from releases of hazardous chemicals. EAHAP can predict concentrations as a function of distance from the source.

Contact the Health, Environment and Safety Group at CRTC for assistance in dispersion modeling and CRTC Materials and Equipment Engineering for

assistance in risk assessment. A wide variety of chemical releases can be modeled. Releases can be either gaseous or liquid.

## 1342 Container Location

Chlorine containers should be located at least 50 feet from flammable materials and structures, such as wooden cooling towers. Radiated or direct sources of heat should also be avoided.

Additional design considerations should include ample area lighting, fire protection, drainage, and the possibility of mechanical damage. Locate the containers so that drainage from hydrocarbon sources will not expose them to a fire risk.

Containers located close to roads should be guarded and be far enough from the road to provide safety from vehicular traffic while still retaining good access for service and emergencies. Standard Drawing GA-S99975, Standard Details for Removable and Stationary Guard Posts, provides a typical design.

Location relative to hydrocarbons, hot lines, occupied buildings, ventilation intakes, roads, personnel gates, and safety equipment is extremely important and must be evaluated with facility safety personnel.

Refer to Section 1370 for other applicable requirements in locating containers at the chlorine user's site.

## 1343 Piping

Piping downstream of Chlorine Institute standard container valves should be ANSI Class 300, Schedule 40 for Monel pipe sized NPS 1½ and smaller, and installed per ANSI/ASME B31.3 as a minimum requirement. Socket-welded construction is preferred for NPS 1½ and smaller. Do not use unions in straight chlorine service.

Seal-weld all threaded joints, excluding the container isolating valves, pigtails and connections to instruments. Nonmetallic pipe and unions, such as PVC, are acceptable in chlorinated water service.

Piping should be located aboveground and be as simple as possible. Isolating valves should be readily accessible. Minimize the number of threaded joints and flanges that cannot be seal welded. Provide adequate support and protection from mechanical damage. Steel angle, channel or equal should be used to protect nonmetallic piping.

Avoid trapping liquid chlorine. Slope piping so that it is self-draining. Liquid chlorine has a high coefficient of thermal expansion and will produce high pressure and, possibly, piping failure when trapped and heated. Good practice will limit the system to a pair of isolation valves located at each container.

Do not use hydrocarbons, nonchlorinated solvents, alcohols, cutting oil, sealants, or grease in the fabrication or cleanup of chlorine piping systems. Chlorinated solvents such as trichloroethylene may be used to clean the piping before placing the system

into service, but these solvents must be used in accordance with their MSDS and the manufacturer's safety recommendations.

Chlorine piping systems should be pressure-tested when new and prior to being placed in service after repairs or alterations. Piping sections operating under vacuum (between the container valve and chlorinator) may be pressure-tested using N₂ gas and soap-tested for leaks. Safe testing practices using a compressed gas should be adhered to. A 150-pug test pressure is recommended for metallic piping systems, but may need to be lowered for nonmetallic piping materials.

Piping for pressurized chlorine feed systems should be hydrostatically tested in accordance with ANSI/ASME B31.3 requirements. Chlorine Institute Pamphlet 6, Piping Systems for Dry Chlorine, should be used for guidance in preparing a hydrostatic test procedure. It is important to thoroughly clean and dry the piping system after testing and prior to the introduction of chlorine. A dryness to a minus 40°F dewpoint is recommended.

## 1344 Vendor-Supplied Systems

All chlorination systems and their proposed site locations shall be reviewed, evaluated, and approved by facility management.

Vacuum-operated chlorinator systems with vacuum regulators attached at the containers offer the safest operation. Applications with chlorine requirements above 20 lb/hr may require a pressure-vacuum system. The pressurized portion of a system should be minimized to reduce the possibility of a leak. Manufacturers of chlorine gas feed equipment can supply their standard system design for evaluation by engineering and safety personnel. They also can provide system setup, maintenance, and periodic service.

Liquid chlorine usually damages the chlorinator components. The following methods may be used to prevent liquid chlorine from entering the chlorinator:

- Install a pressure reducing valve to flash residual liquid.
- Install an electric heater in the chlorinator (most can be factory equipped).
- Heat trace the piping between the containers and chlorinator.
- Install a liquid trap in the piping upstream of the chlorinator.

Chlorinators should be installed in weatherproof enclosures suitable for both the environment and electrical area classification. The enclosure should permit viewing the chlorinator safely through a suitable window.

## 1345 Sizing Criteria

The dependable continuous discharge rate of chlorine gas from a single one-ton container without frosting under normal conditions (70°F) is about 15 lb/hr against a 35 psig backpressure. The rate for a single 150-lb cylinder is about 1.75 lb/hr under similar conditions.

If the gas discharge rate from a single container will not meet usage requirements, alternate arrangements may be considered, such as manifolding more than one container to a common chlorinator or installing an evaporator. Operability and safety of these alternatives require review and approval by the facility management.

If two or more containers are manifolded together, they must be at the same temperature to prevent the transfer of chlorine from a warm container to a cool container. If a container becomes completely filled with liquid and the container valve is then closed, subsequent pressure buildup as the container warms may cause it to burst.

## 1350 Vapor Control and Fire Protection

### 1351 Vapor Control

Vapor control should be done only by personnel specifically trained in handling hazardous materials and experienced in using the appropriate protective equipment.

Alert designated emergency response personnel and downwind facilities regarding any leak. A liquid chlorine leak is appreciably more hazardous than a gaseous leak. Isolate the containers from a piping leak by using the isolating valves at the containers. If the container body is leaking, orient the cylinder or one-ton container so that gas, rather than liquid, is released. This will reduce the rate of release by a factor of fifteen.

To protect internal building environments and the occupants, immediately close doors, windows and air conditioning inlets and vents, and turn off the air ventilation system.

If a chlorine leak should occur, use water to knock down and safely direct the gas away from personnel. Do not apply water directly into the leak, and do not immerse the container in water or alkaline solution. Mixing water with chlorine produces hydrochloric acid, whose corrosive action can increase the size of the initial opening.

Contain drainage water if possible and route to a neutralizing reservoir. Liquid chlorine must be kept out of the sewer system. Soda ash may be used to contain the puddle.

### 1352 Fire Protection

Under fire conditions keep the chlorine containers cool to avoid releasing chlorine through their fusible metal plugs. If possible, remove containers from the fire zone and relocate to a remote area that will not endanger facility access or public safety.

If the container integrity is uncertain, contact CHEMTREC (800-424-9300) or your local CHLOREP (chlorine emergency plan) team for assistance before trying to move the container.

Providing additional spacing away from fire exposure; and using a radiant heat barrier, a water spray, fire monitors or hose stations may be considered.

## 1360  Facility Identification and Guarding

Equipment and lines handling chlorine should have paint stenciled identification, and signs should be posted in compliance with the Company's *Safety in Designs Manual*.

Guard posts, and "caution" and "no parking" signs should be installed at the perimeter of the chlorine storage area and each location where chlorine is used.

## 1370  Inspection

### 1371 Frequency

Due to the potential hazard that can result from equipment and/or piping failure, an effective semiannual facility inspection should be conducted. A typical inspection checklist is included in Section 1372. The inspection should be primarily visual. Nondestructive examination (NDE) may be used on piping as appropriate. Local facility inspection practices should be followed.

Some of the checklist items are suitable for more frequent inspection. Checking for leaks should be a daily routine. Emergency repair kits should be restocked after each use as well as inventoried at the semiannual inspections. It is suggested that inspection and testing be done weekly for safety equipment, including respiratory units, shower/eyewash and protective clothing.

### 1372 Typical Checklist

#### Housekeeping/Design

1. Number of containers and size? Vertical or horizontal? Are there more than the normal number of containers? If so, why?

2. Are guard posts installed?

3. Are positive hold-down clamps in position? Are safety chains in place?

4. Is the area free of combustible material?

5. Are "caution" and "no parking" signs in place?

6. Is the discharge line connected to the one-ton container gas valve in the 12 o'clock position? Is liquid valve in the 6 o'clock position capped?

7. Do empty and spare containers have protective domes installed?

8. Check DOT hydrotest date for each container. Are they current? DOT requires retesting every five years—return noncurrent containers to the supplier.

9. Are there any leaks?

10. Is a chlorine container valve wrench available?

11. Are emergency repair kits available and complete?

12. Are respiratory units properly located and functional?

13. Is the safety shower/eyewash operable?

14. Is the piping in good condition?

15. Does the system meet the design standards? Use this engineering guideline and Attachments I and II at the end of Section 1300 to prepare a detailed checklist.

#### Operation/Operator Awareness

1. Are operating standards and emergency response procedures adequate and readily available to those who use the facility?

2. Is MSDS 646 for chlorine readily available?

3. Are all personnel trained on the hazards, first aid and operation of the chlorine facility?

4. Are Operating Standards/Instructions followed?

5. Do operators use breathing air when changing cylinders?

6. Do operators/supervisors know what to do when a leak occurs?
