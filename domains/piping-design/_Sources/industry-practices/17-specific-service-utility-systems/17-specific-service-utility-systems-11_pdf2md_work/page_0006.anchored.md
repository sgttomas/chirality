It is a good safety practice to check frequently for leaks. A mist of aqueous ammonia vapor, when mixed with chlorine gas, will form a white cloud (ammonium chloride). A commercial 26-degree Baumé aqueous ammonia solution in an atomizer may be used to snoop for leaks. Avoid getting aqueous ammonia directly on brass or copper.

Emergency repair kits should be used to handle container leaks. Other techniques used for leak mitigation follow.

## Container Valve Leaks

A container valve leak can usually be stopped by tightening the bonnet packing nut with a special wrench that is supplied by the chlorine shipper for this specific purpose. Tighten the bonnet packing nut just enough to stop the leakage. Note that chlorine container valves are designed to achieve either a full open or full closed position with a single turn of the stem. They can be damaged if turned beyond this point. The valve should unseat when the end of the special wrench is struck with the heel of the hand.

## Container Body Leaks

If a container body develops a leak, turn the container so that the opening discharges gas rather than liquid. This will reduce the amount of chlorine discharged to the environment. Never immerse a container in water.

Chlorine cylinders deliver gas in the vertical or upright position. One-ton containers positioned horizontally have two valves on one end that are normally vertically aligned; the upper valve delivers gas and the lower valve delivers liquid. When a one-ton container is rolled over with a valve inadvertently left open, the flow will cycle between gas and liquid according to the valve orientation on the container. The most common cause of chlorine leakage has been the mishandling of containers.

## Associated Piping Leaks

If a leak develops in the piping, isolate the containers by closing the container valve. Always purge chlorine lines before attempting any repair. Nitrogen may be used as a purge gas. Safely discharge the purge gas and chlorine into an alkaline solution through a pipe or weighted rubber hose.

Chlorine can be absorbed in aqueous solutions of caustic soda (NaOH) or soda ash (Na₂CO₃). The recommended mixtures in Figure 1300-1 are given by courtesy of the Chlorine Institute.

Avoid the discharge of chlorinated water into drainage systems where it may react with hydrocarbons and corrode the drainage system.
