## 1337 Chlorine Storage

Chlorine containers should be stored outside in a secure, well ventilated area. Provide suitable platforms and supports to hold the containers, and keep them free of standing water. Platforms should be large enough for inspection and emergency repairs.

One-ton containers may require a monorail or crane with a special lifting beam. The containers can be set on roller trunnions to provide support, maintain fixed spacing, and facilitate container rotation in the event of a leak. All 150-lb cylinders should be secured with safety straps or chains and one-ton containers with positive hold-down clamps to prevent inadvertent movement during servicing or in an earthquake.

Minimize the number of chlorine containers that are distributed to the sites where chlorine is used. Spare containers with their associated valves tend to increase the possibility of a chlorine leak. Full and empty containers should be separated from each other as well as segregated from other compressed gas containers and hydrocarbon chemicals.

Usually a four-week supply at a consolidated storage area will be adequate; consult with purchasing regarding optimum delivery schedule to hold storage to a minimum. Chlorine containers should be used on a first-in, first-out basis.

# 1340 Design and Construction Requirements

## 1341 Dispersion Modeling and Risk Assessment

Dispersion modeling may be performed to determine potential chlorine concentrations in air in the event of an accidental release. Modeling results and risk assessment may be used to develop guidelines on design criteria needed to mitigate potential exposure to the community or plant personnel. Possible design criteria affected by the risk assessment are the quantity of chlorine stored and where the chlorine user should be located within the plant. Modeling results may also be used to develop emergency response plans for responding to accidental releases.

Concentrations resulting from a release are highly specific to release rates and meteorological conditions. Furthermore, releases of liquid chlorine forms fine liquid droplets (an aerosol) as well as vapor. The presence of the aerosol makes the chlorine cloud denser than air, which permits the cloud to travel a substantial distance downwind. Each situation should be modeled on a case-by-case basis. The Health, Environment and Safety Group at CRTC has a license to use a model developed by Energy Analysts Inc. called the Energy Analysts Hazards Analysis Package (EAHAP) which is specifically designed to predict concentrations from releases of hazardous chemicals. EAHAP can predict concentrations as a function of distance from the source.
