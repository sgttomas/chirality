Thus, under most conditions ammonia should be considered a flammable mixture. The extent of flammability, however, should be subject to evaluation, particularly in regard to electrical area classification.

Ammonia has a low heat of combustion. Thus, pools of pure liquid anhydrous ammonia will not sustain burning unless other heat sources are present. This should be a factor when considering firefighting techniques and the need for fireproofing around ammonia equipment.

## 1234 Autorefrigeration and Brittle Fracture

The boiling point of liquid ammonia at atmospheric pressure is minus 28°F (minus 33.35°C) and is considerably lower than the normal ambient temperature.

Equipment could be subject to low temperatures in the event of a rapid depressurization, a liquid ammonia spill, or a flange leak. The temperature of ammonia discharging under pressure will drop to minus 28°F (minus 33.35°C) when expanding to atmospheric pressure. Atmospheric pools of liquid ammonia can cool to about minus 58°F (minus 50°C) because NH₃ vapor above the pool becomes buoyant and sets up convection currents that cause rapid evaporation. Because steel can have poor toughness at low temperature, this severe chilling significantly increases the risk of a brittle fracture. Refer to the Company's *Pressure Vessel Manual* for additional information on brittle fracture. Brittle fracture can be avoided in new facilities by utilizing construction materials that will retain their toughness at the lowest temperatures that can reasonably be expected. It is achieved in existing facilities by avoiding temperatures lower than existing materials can safely withstand.

### Pressure Drop

Pressure drops taken across control valves, relief valves, sample valves, etc., will cause a drop in temperature when NH₃ is expanded. This may cause freezing or hydrates to form if water is present. The possible temperature reduction in valves and piping should also be considered in the material selection.

## 1235 Accidental Releases

Federal law (CERCLA) requires that the release to the environment of hazardous substances, such as anhydrous ammonia, be immediately reported to allow necessary emergency response to protect the public and environment. Any anhydrous ammonia release of 100 pounds (17 gallons, 2000 SCF) or more within any 24-hour period must be reported to both the National Response Center (NRC) in Washington, D.C. (telephone 800-424-8802), and if exposure occurs to persons outside of the facility, to state and local emergency response agencies (as designated to comply with Federal Law SARA—Title III). Additional state and local agencies may also require notification.
