## 1. INTRODUCTION

### 1.1 SCOPE

This DEP specifies requirements and gives recommendations for the design, construction, testing, operation and maintenance of gaseous oxygen systems with operating temperatures between -28 °C and +150 °C. Operating temperatures outside that range are addressed in Best Practices covering specific processes (such as gasification) or by the industrial gas vendors supplying the oxygen. For the purposes of this DEP, oxygen systems comprise vessels, heat exchangers, compressors, pipe, fittings, valves and instruments that come into contact with and are used for the transport and utilisation of dry gaseous oxygen (oxygen volume fraction 90 % or higher).

The Principal shall specify whether a system handling gas mixtures with an oxygen volume fraction of less than 90 % is to be considered an oxygen system.

For gas mixtures with an oxygen volume fraction greater than 21 %, and for air at pressures above 50 bar (ga), the cleanliness and inspection requirements of (6) shall apply.

This DEP is a revision of the DEP of the same number and title, dated March 1992. A summary of the changes is included in (1.5).

## 2. GENERAL SAFETY PRINCIPLES

Once ignited, many materials (including most metals) will burn violently in an oxygen atmosphere. Because of the risks associated with the ignition of oxygen handling systems, the goal of high system integrity shall not be compromised under any circumstances. In some cases, hazard analyses may be necessary.

Ignition may occur when energy in any form is supplied, but the likelihood of ignition will vary for each particular case. Valves, fittings, heat exchangers and compressors, for instance, have higher ignition risks than long straight lines and the shells of vessels.

It is important for heat energy to be able to dissipate before the ignition temperature is reached, and therefore the use of materials with a high thermal conductivity is preferred.

The risk of ignition of materials in oxygen can be minimised by adhering to a number of basic rules:

1. Use materials which have a sufficiently high ignition temperature and possibly also a low heat of combustion and high thermal conductivity and which have proved to be suitable for oxygen service, see (3);
2. Provide smooth surfaces and round edges in piping and equipment design and construction;
3. Use thick walls and internals with low surface to volume ratio. Thin or small items like wire meshes and small valve internals require the most ignition-resistant materials.
4. Remove all contaminants which may react easily, such as oil, grease and organic materials, see (6) and (7);
5. Avoid all sources of energy which could initiate a fire, e.g. high velocities, turbulence, uncontrolled elevation of temperature by adiabatic compression or gas vibration, see (4);
6. Avoid the presence of dust or particulate matter which, due to friction and/or impact, may react with the oxygen and/or eventually ignite the material of construction;
7. Safeguard personnel and equipment against leakages by fitting shields where necessary and/or remote operation of valves and machinery;
8. Use experienced personnel in the precommissioning, operation and maintenance of oxygen systems.

These aspects have been elaborated in the following Sections.

## 3. MATERIALS

### 3.1 METALLIC MATERIALS

Dry gaseous oxygen in the temperature range -20 °C to +150 °C is normally handled in carbon steel or austenitic stainless steel, subject to the velocity limitations given in (4.2). Higher velocities generally require the use of nickel-based (or copper-based) alloys. Specific metallic materials for oxygen system components shall be selected in accordance with the guidelines given in (4.2 to 4.7).

#### Carbon steels

Plain carbon steels are extensively used for dry gaseous oxygen service, in particular for transportation pipelines, but also for in plot piping with maximum operating temperatures below 80 °C. The ignition temperatures are relatively high whilst the specific heat and the heat conductivity are sufficiently high to prevent ignition, if the measures in this DEP are followed. Any condensation of water in the oxygen system must be excluded and contaminants in the oxygen must be non-corrosive. Strict cleaning procedures and measures to prevent corrosion during shut down are required. Adequate protection against external corrosion, in particular under external insulation or fire protection, is required as well. For minimum design temperatures below 0 °C, carbon steels shall have an impact toughness > 27J at -20 °C to avoid the risk of brittle fracture.

#### Stainless steels

Plain austenitic stainless steels, which nominally contain some 18%w of chromium and 8%w of nickel, generally have a higher ignition temperature than carbon steels and are more suitable for oxygen service. Oxygen cleanliness is more easily maintained. Suitable grades are S30403, S32100 and S31603. Grades with higher chromium concentrations should not be used. Likewise, duplex stainless steels that have higher chromium contents should not be used. Proper gas shielding during welding is required to prevent oxidation at the internal surfaces near the weld. Externally, protection is required against pitting corrosion and at temperatures above 50 °C against chloride stress corrosion cracking.

#### Nickel-based alloys

Nickel-based alloys with low chromium content are practically unignitable in oxygen. They have a high ignition temperature and the heat of combustion is low. Pure nickel (N02200) has a high thermal conductivity, but alloying elements decrease it. The most common alloys used for oxygen service are:

- 15 %Cr-8 %Fe nickel alloy N06600 (e.g. Inconel 600). This high temperature alloy usually has a carbon content of about 0.08 %w and a coarse grain size resulting in a sensitised microstructure and poor corrosion resistance in many environments. A particular concern is intergranular stress corrosion cracking in steam environments. In steam above 50° in external corrosive environment, the N06600 alloy shall be specified with a carbon content below 0.025 %w. This will reduce the allowable design stresses.

- 30 %Cu nickel alloy N04400, e.g., Monel 400. Good weldability with resistance to liquation cracking is to be assured through close specification of the silicon content.

The use of other nickel-based alloys that contain higher chromium, iron or molybdenum contents than those of the above alloys requires approval by the Principal, if the conditions are beyond the limits for stainless steel.

#### Copper and Silver

More noble metals like copper and silver have a lower heat of combustion than nickel, but their lower melting points result in lower ignition temperatures. Copper and silver with a low alloy content are suitable for low stress components in oxygen service. Alloys containing elements that are reactive with oxygen, like aluminium and zinc, should not be used.

#### Aluminium

Aluminium and its alloys shall not be used within the scope of this DEP, which covers the temperature range from -20 °C to +150 °C. Aluminium alloys are extensively used for cryogenic heat exchangers in oxygen-producing plants because of their high thermal conductivity and their suitability for the fabricating of very large heat transfer surface areas. However, the ignition temperature in oxygen is low and so is their melting temperature. The heat of combustion is very high and once the aluminium is ignited, the damage will be extensive and a blow-out will occur.

## 3.2 NON-METALLIC MATERIALS

Organic materials have lower ignition temperatures than metallic materials. Once ignited, they may promote ignition in adjacent metal components. The volume of organic materials should be minimised and they should be surrounded by metal to maximise heat dissipation.

The most common used non-metallic materials in the temperature range of -20 °C to +150 °C are:

- Fluorinated or chlorofluorinated resins and elastomers. They can be reinforced with oxides, glass ceramic, metals and alloys to improve their mechanical properties; however, such reinforcement shall not adversely affect their resistance to ignition.
- Graphite, which can also be used at temperatures well above 150 °C. Graphite is not recommended for the sealing of infrequently moving parts (stuffing boxes), when it is exposed externally to humid atmospheric or corrosive conditions for prolonged periods.

The selection of all non-metallic components shall be based on proof of oxygen compatibility in the required range of temperature and pressure. Such a proof can be derived from the list produced by the Bundesanstalt für Materialforschung und -prüfung (BAM), Berlin, Germany). Non-metallic materials that are not proven by BAM are subject to the approval by the Principal.

## 4.3 PIPING AND FITTINGS

### 4.3.1 Materials

Materials for straight piping, and for bends with a radius of at least 5D, shall be selected in accordance with the velocity limits described in (4.2) and Figure 1. Nickel-based alloys in accordance with (3.1) are required for:

- Fittings where direct impingement of entrained particles may occur, such as short radius elbows and tees, see Figure 2; and valve internals.
- Piping downstream of control valves for a distance of at least 10 times the nominal diameter, see Figure 6.

In areas in Figure 1 where carbon steel or stainless steel construction is permitted, fittings, valve internals and downstream piping of the same material may be used if the velocity limit is reduced by half.

Pipe sizes smaller than DN 50 shall not be used. The minimum wall thickness for carbon steel and stainless steel piping shall be 6 mm; this does not apply to instrument tubing.

### 4.3.2 Design

Oxygen piping systems shall be kept as simple as possible, with the smallest possible number of valves, fittings, branches and nozzles.

Oxygen piping shall be located as far as possible from other piping. Pipes containing flammable fluid or gas or steam should not be installed within 1 m distance of oxygen piping. The free space may be utilised for lines carrying water, nitrogen or other non-flammable fluids at ambient temperature. At points where the above requirements cannot be fulfilled, the Principal shall be asked whether any additional preventive or corrective measures are required.

Oxygen pipelines shall be located on the outermost position of pipe bridges.

Installation of oxygen pipelines through tunnels or (covered or uncovered) trenches should be avoided. If unavoidable, flanged connections or valves shall not be used (except for low point drain connections) and the tunnel or trench shall not contain lines carrying flammable fluids or gases. Oxygen piping shall not support other pipes.

Underground oxygen piping may be installed outside process and other hazardous areas and shall be clearly identified above ground and kept separate from electrical cables and other piping.

The underground piping should have external corrosion protection.

Expansion bellows shall not be used.

**Amended per Circular 35/03**

Slip-on flanges, lap flanges, or threaded connections for valves and fittings shall not be used. Socket welds are not permitted.

In carbon steel and stainless steel piping systems, branches shall be made with equal tees, reducing tees or swept outlets. In nickel-based alloy piping systems, branches should also be made with equal tees, reducing tees or swept outlets, but branch outlets (weldolets) and branch fittings (nipples) may be used.

Where nickel-based alloy is required in accordance with Figures 1A and 1B, branches should have minimum wall thickness, e.g. Schedule 80.

Changes in diameter shall be minimised. Where changes in diameter are unavoidable, they should be accomplished in steps of one nominal size at a time, and the distance between successive diameter changes should be at least 1000 mm, see Figure 2.

Dead ends in piping shall be avoided, see Figure 3.

The oxygen flow in piping shall be in one direction only. If flow in two directions cannot be avoided, the Principal's agreement shall be obtained.

To assist removal of the degreasing agent after cleaning, low points in equipment and piping, except underground piping, shall have drain connections without a valve but closed by a blind flange. Underground piping shall not be provided with drains for this purpose.

Above ground oxygen systems shall be earthed.

Nitrogen systems used for automatic purging shall be equipped with a filter and two pressure indicators. They shall be designed to prevent the possibility of oxygen flowing back into the nitrogen system.

Connections for purging and venting shall be situated so that purging and venting of the system can only be done in the normal direction of the oxygen flow.

Where an oxygen line enters the plot of a process unit or a hazardous area, a block valve shall be installed. This valve, however, may only be used in cases of emergency and shall be operated by a coded a fire panel, or remotely from one or more suitable locations.

Where oxygen piping is connected to other piping or equipment that may contain flammable fluid or gas (such as a reactor), provisions shall be made for ensuring separation of the two media during shutdown and startup. Two possible means of achieving this are (1) two double-block-and-bleed arrangements in series, or (2) a pressurised nitrogen wedge in a section of the oxygen pipe.

### 4.3.3 Insulating flanges

Insulating flanges of cathodically protected lines shall only be fitted in a vertical line section in order to avoid the collection of particulate matter that might bridge the insulation gap, Figure 4. The selected design and materials shall be subject to approval by the Principal.

Insulating flanges shall be protected against internal sparking caused by elevated electrical potential differences across the insulation adn stray currents, induction or otherwise. Polarisation cells of sufficient rating shall always be installed across insulating joints or flanges.

### 4.3.4 Vent lines

Vent lines shall be made of stainless steel. They shall be sized for a maximum velocity of 50 m/s. Consequently the pipe diameter may have to be increased immediately downstream of the vent valve. Vent lines should be vertical and straight; however, if bends cannot be avoided, to avoid eddy turbulence they should not be installed within a distance of 20 pipe diameters downstream of the vent valve. To prevent ingress of rain or other foreign matter, a construction as shown in Figure 5 or similar curved vent lines shall be applied. The vent valve shall be sized so that the maximum allowable velocity in the line to be depressurised shall not be exceeded (4.2). In some cases, a control valve may be required.

### 4.3.5 Gaskets

Gaskets shall be carefully sized to prevent any protrusion beyond the free inner surface of the pipe. Various graphite-based gasket constructions with inlay, e.g., Sigraflex-E-foil, have been approved by BAM for conditions up to 200 °C and 130 bar, and reinforced PTFE type gaskets have been approved for 150 °C and 30 bar. Special care shall be taken to prevent graphite flaking off into the line, particularly of the smaller size pipes where thermal stresses are higher. In such cases, the use of graphite-based spiral wound 304 SS gaskets with a 304 SS inner ring may be preferable.

Encapsulated asbestos-type materials, e.g., Durable Black and Garlock 900, as formerly endorsed by the Dutch Safety Gas Association (CAO) shall no longer be used, but replaced by appropriate substitute materials, e.g., Klingersil C4430. For applications around 150 °C and above, full graphite based gaskets could react with oxygen, limiting their lifetime. For these conditions, composite gaskets with an additional inner inert barrier should be

considered, e.g., Lens gaskets. The oxygen supplier should be consulted on the latest recommendations for gaskets.

#### 4.3.6 **Identification**

Oxygen piping shall be clearly identified by colour coding.

## 4.4 VALVES AND INSTRUMENTS

Only valves and instruments especially made for oxygen service and that have proved to be reliable in oxygen service shall be used. For selection of materials of construction and type, reference is made to (3).

If lubrication is required, a fluorocarbon lubricant suitable for oxygen service shall be used, the selection of which is subject to the approval by the Principal.

### 4.4.1 Control valves

All process control valves are considered to be in throttling service. Throttling valves may generate sonic gas velocities and are therefore considered critical. Only globe valves or needle valves (including angle and Y types) shall be used for throttling. The body and internals shall be constructed of nickel-based alloy.

A pipe section made of material suitable for high velocities (4.3.1) shall be installed downstream of throttling valves.

For combined control and Tight Shut-Off (TSO) service, a separate block valve shall be installed in series with the control valve, as combined service in a single valve is deemed impossible.

### 4.4.2 Block valves

Block valves shall not be used for regulating flow. They shall only be operated in the fully open or fully closed position. They shall not be operated if a pressure differential prevails. They shall only be opened if there are equal pressures on both sides of the valve or be closed if there is no oxygen flow. Block valves larger than DN 50, having a pressure differential of 18 bar or more across the valve in the closed position, shall be installed with an accompanying bypass as shown in Figure 6 to permit pressure equalisation. Only ball valves or gate valves shall be used as block valves.

Exceptions to the above include nitrogen purge valves which shall be opened only when there is a positive nitrogen pressure relative to the oxygen system, or where a block valve is to be operated (closed) in the case of extreme emergency. Examples are a block valve at the entrance of a process plot and a block valve upstream of a filter and/or measuring station (4.8). The block valve shall in either case be operable from behind a fire shield, or remotely from one or more suitable locations. Valves with non-rising stem (HOLD types) are preferred. Block valves with bodies of carbon steel shall only be used up to 10 bar (ga).

Block valves may have gearboxes which should be mounted horizontally with the stem to avoid leaking of grease into the stuffing box area.

Gate valves, ball valves or globe valves shall be of fire-safe design and have seals and seats of PTFE. The maximum temperature permitted is 120 °C, see (Appendix 2). The volume of PTFE shall be as small as possible. Ball valves with all-metal seats may be used above 120 °C, provided the stem seal and trim seats are suitable, see (Appendix 2). Any sealant connections shall be left undrilled or be permanently plugged.

Automatic (solenoid-operated) shut-off valves shall be single-seated globe valves with trim especially ground for tight shut-off. If agreed by the Principal, PTFE seat rings may be used when the temperature in the system is below 120 °C, see (Appendix 2).

### 4.4.4 Relief valves

Relief valves shall be designed to ensure maximum leak-tightness in the closed position. The internal elements shall be made from appropriate stainless steel or nickel-based alloy. The valve body shall be made from nickel-based alloy for system pressures exceeding 10 bar (ga) and from stainless steel for lower pressures.

#### 4.4.5 Check valves

Check valves should be of the plate-disc type (compressor valve type) without helical springs. Valve plates and spring plates shall be of stainless steel, or of Inconel X-750 for pressures above 70 bar (ga).

#### 4.4.6 Instruments

All instrument parts in contact with oxygen shall be stainless steel or nickel-based alloy, and have smooth surfaces. Filling fluids for capsules and diaphragm seals shall be such that they do not present a hazard if the diaphragm should fail. The liquid shall be a fluorocarbon and the selected type shall be subject to the approval by the Principal. The special liquid shall be indicated, e.g., by etching, on the outside of the item.

#### 4.4.7 Identification

All valves and instruments shall bear the warning:

***'OXYGEN! KEEP FREE FROM OIL AND GREASE!'***

## 4.5 FILTERS

Filters should be used to remove possible dust or particulate matter from gaseous-oxygen systems after new plant extensions have been commissioned, see (2, item 4). Filters should be placed upstream of equipment which have internal moving parts (such as a compressor), or equipment which can create high velocity and thus accelerate particular matter (such as a control station or throttling valve).

Filters shall be of the candle type. The elements shall be made of copper or nickel alloy.

Pore size and velocity requirements are as follows:

- for filters upstream of control and measuring stations: maximum pore/mesh size 50 μm, maximum entry velocity 0.2 m/s depending on filtering area;
- for filters in the oxygen feed to low-temperature reaction systems containing flammable matter (such as ethylene oxide plants): maximum pore size 10 μm, maximum entry velocity 0.05 m/s depending on filtering area;
- for filters in suction lines to compressors, see (4.7).

The filter elements shall be easily removable for inspection. The filters shall be designed so that the trapped particles cannot enter the downstream piping when the elements are removed.

An external indication shall be provided to indicate the direction of flow.

A filter should be equipped with a blanked-off drain connection, a purge (and pressurising) connection downstream of the block valve, and a vent connection downstream of the filter elements.

To avoid dead ends and the possible accumulation of dust or foreign matter, spare filters should not be installed.

Nitrogen connections, to be used for purging and pressurising sections of the oxygen system located downstream of an oxygen filter, shall be equipped with filters that meet the pore size requirements of the upstream oxygen filter.

## 4.6 SILENCERS

Downstream of silencers the flow is turbulent and with high velocity, leading to impingement and associated high risk.

The material of the pipe downstream of a silencer shall be made of stainless steel or other oxidizing resistant material, e.g., copper or nickel alloy, as required by Figure 1.

## 4.7 COMPRESSORS

Where oxygen has to be compressed, there are potential ignition hazards that shall be recognised and controlled. Operating personnel shall be safeguarded from injury, and the risk of compressor breakdown by fire or explosion shall be minimised by adhering to correct design and operating procedures.

For design aspects of compressors, DEPs 31.29.40.30-Gen (centrifugal) and 31.29.40.31-Gen (reciprocating) shall be consulted. Additional information may be found in (10).

## 4.8 OXYGEN MEASURING STATION

**Amended per Circular 30/00**

When oxygen is supplied, e.g., by a third party, it is often delivered by pipeline. At the point of entry to the plant, a custody transfer measuring station is usually installed. The design and safety philosophy is described below.

Apart from its normal function for accounting purposes, the oxygen measuring station has two additional functions:

- separation of responsibilities of supplier and customer; and
- safeguarding the supplier's system against damage caused by an emergency situation at the customer's end and vice versa.

An oxygen measuring station will normally contain the following main elements (starting from supplier's end): main block valve A filter, flow measuring element, oxygen purity analyser, excess flow valve B with by-pass valve C, and block valve D, see Figure 7.

The filter serves to avoid ingress of dust and other foreign matter from the oxygen supply line into the consumer's distribution network and mainly to minimise the risk of fire in the equipment downstream of that filter (especially the valves). For safety reasons it is recommended to use a single filter only and to avoid the installation of spare filters. Relief valves shall not be installed in the measuring station. Carbon steel shall not be used for the parts of the measuring station from the filter onwards. The drain(s) at the lowest point(s) serving to remove remnants of degreasing agents may be avoided depending on the de-scaling / cleaning / pickling arrangements. Ideally, the high velocity blowing of nitrogen will remove any entrapped liquids at low ends. The benefit of not having a drain is that no liquid / contaminants are trapped unnecessarily. In addition, such a drain is an additional leak point. Main block valve A and excess flow valve B should be controlled so that on failure of one of the power media, the positions (status) of the valves remain unchanged. The valves shall only change position when a command for change is received. The power media for control of these two valves shall be from an uninterruptible maintained supply.

Control valve C is controlled so that it will close when one of the power media fails.

Block valve D has an extended spindle and is operated from behind a fire shield.

Normal manual closing of valve A (via remote station) shall only be performed when the oxygen supply line and measuring station have been taken out of operation. Under certain emergency conditions, valve A shall be closed. These are:

- manual emergency closure from the panel (manned supervising panel) or locally (at measuring station);
- at high flow (say 120 % of contract), sustained for longer than t₁ seconds (where t₁ is closing time of valve B, say 10 sec);

  NOTE: At high flow, excess flow valve B will start to close immediately.

- at low pressure upstream of orifice, sustained for longer than t₁ sec;

  NOTE: At low pressure, e.g., contract minimum less 2 bar, excess flow valve B will start to close immediately.

- at high differential pressure across valve A.

  NOTE: This arrangement keeps valve A closed in the event of a high differential pressure, in order to avoid high velocities through valve and filter during re-opening. This is particularly appropriate in supply systems with a large difference between the pressure at normal flow and pressure at no-flow, e.g., in a long transport line. In order to be able to open valve A, the downstream connection to the excess flow valve B shall be pressurised with nitrogen to approximately the supply line pressure, e.g., in complicated systems, e.g., long supply lines, the approval of equalising the differential line by a standing instruction for the operating personnel (pressure equalisation) may be considered. However, the Principal's agreement must be obtained in writing for this application.

Excess flow valve B will close under the following conditions:

- high flow (say 120 % of contract maximum);

- low pressure upstream of orifice (say contract minimum pressure less 2 bar);

- manual emergency closure from the panel (manned supervising panel) or locally (at measuring station).

Valve B, when closed, cannot be (re)opened if either:

- the differential pressure across the valve (in the direction of flow) exceeds a certain value (say 2 bar);

- the control valve C in the by-pass is open.

By-pass control valve C shall be sized so that when fully open under critical differential pressure conditions, the actual velocity will not be higher than maximum allowable, see Figure 1, in the main line(s) downstream of valve B. Normally, it is adequate if the maximum trim size for valve C is one quarter of that of valve B.

Block valve D, operated via an extended spindle from behind a fire shield, shall not be closed unless the oxygen flow is zero. The valve serves to attain a positive shut-off after the measuring station has tripped or has been taken out of operation.

Rupture and/or fire in the oxygen line will initiate the following actions:

- If rupture is in section (a), downstream of valve B, the high flow closes valve B and the flow will be reduced to zero after which valve D is shut manually;

- If rupture is in section (b), downstream of flow measurement orifice but upstream of valve B, the high flow closes valve B and because the flow remains high, valve A will be closed after t₁ sec;

- If rupture is in section (c), downstream of valve A but upstream of orifice, the low pressure closes valve B and because the pressure remains low, valve A will be closed after t₁ sec;

- If rupture is in section (d), upstream of valve A, the same action as above for section (c) will occur.

Figure 8 is an example of a typical logic diagram for the system shown in Figure 7. This logic diagram may be simplified, but this requires approval by the Principal.

The following minimum distances shall be adhered to:

- 25 m to public roads/railways, warehouses for flammable products, public parking places, vessels containing flammable products, loading/ unloading stations, oil catchers;

- 15 m to plant buildings, unrestricted plant roads/railways, above ground pipelines containing inflammable products;

- 10 m to private parking places, buried vessels and piping containing flammable products.

A blow-out in an oxygen system has the same impact as a local explosion. The severity of the blow-out is related to the pressure level in the system. Care should be taken to avoid impingement of the 'oxygen flame' and the scattering of burning metal particles onto other equipment.

## 5. ORDERING, MARKING, PACKAGING

Orders for materials for oxygen service shall not be combined with those for other materials. The materials shall be delivered free from rust, scale, oil and grease, see (6). Small items shall be packed in plastic bags so that foreign material cannot enter during transport. Orifices should be sealed by metal, plastic or rubber plugs. Sealing with adhesive tape shall be avoided.

The openings of prefabricated pipework and components shall be sealed by means of blind flanges, metal or plastic covers.

The Manufacturer shall clearly label all materials and equipment in bold lettering:

***"SUITABLE FOR OXYGEN SERVICE"***

## 6. CLEANING

### 6.1 GENERAL REQUIREMENTS

The importance of meticulous degreasing and careful cleaning cannot be over-emphasized, as remnants of organic matter and minute solid particles in the presence of high pressure and/or high velocity oxygen may ignite, with the possibility of violent reaction. Careful attention shall, therefore, be paid to the cleanliness of oxygen systems.

Removal of contaminants such as greases, oil, thread lubricants, dirt, water, filings, weld splatter, paints, and other foreign matter is essential. An acceptable level of cleanliness can be accomplished by precleaning all parts and maintaining this condition during construction; by completely cleaning the system after construction; or, as is normally the case for a large plant facility, by a combination of the two. The cleaning procedure and inspection plan shall be established early in a project to ensure that the methods and sequence proposed are compatible with the plant design and construction programme. Any cleaning procedure shall be such that the final surface cleanliness, prior to system commissioning, will meet the inspection criteria given in (6.5).

See IGC 33/97 for additional information.

### 6.2 PRECLEANING

All materials including vessels, pipe(work), valves, (instrument) fittings, pumps, compressors, filters, etc. shall be precleaned prior to final assembly and erection. In the majority of cases, this cleaning requirement will be part of a purchase order or shop fabrication contract. Tender documents shall specify the internal surface cleanliness requirements given in (6.4) and require proposed cleaning procedures to be included in tender submissions.

For guidance, a list is given below of the typical methods used to preclean the internal surfaces of various items which go to make up an oxygen system:

Carbon steel vessels : [solvent or high pressure water (with or without detergent) or steam degreasing] + abrasive blast cleaning to surface finish ISO 8501-1, grade Sa 2 ½.

Stainless steel vessels : [solvent or high pressure water (with or without detergent) or steam degreasing] + [selective acid picking/passivation]

Pipe(work) : [alkaline degreasing] + [acid pickling or abrasive blastcleaning to surface finish ISO 8501-1, grade SA 2 ½]

Valves, compressors, pumps, filters : [solvent or high pressure water (with or without detergent) or steam degreasing] + [acid pickling or abrasive blast cleaning] for body/casing[(solvent vapour) or high pressure water (with or without detergent) or steam degreasing] for internals.

Instruments/Instrument fittings : solvent (vapour) or high pressure water (with or without detergent) or steam degreasing.

Following precleaning, the internal cleanliness of the system components shall be preserved at least until they are installed in the pipe on site, by means of:

1) hermetically sealed wrapping, containing desiccant bag, for small items;
2) firmly secured end-caps, plugs or gasketed blank flanges, as appropriate, to seal all openings on non-hermetically packaged items;

3) desiccant (bags), but not vapour phase inhibitors, attached to the inside of (temporary) end-caps and blank flanges;

4) phosphate conversion coating for acid cleaned, carbon steel items.

All precleaned parts or final assemblies shall be labelled/marked in accordance with the requirements of (5).

## 6.3 POST ERECTION (IN-SITU) CLEANING

Large diameter items, e.g., vessels, compressors, filters, and instrument fittings, should be isolated from pipework and cleaned separately, if required, using methods listed in (6.2).

Temporary spooling (in place of valves and the items mentioned above) or the separating of different sections shall be carried out to facilitate the in-situ cleaning of pipework. If the precleaned condition has been maintained during construction and erection, and there has been minimal site welding, in-situ cleaning can be limited to blowing the pipework out with high (near sonic) velocity, oil-free air, supplemented where necessary (and possible) with brush pigs.

If the precleaned condition has not been maintained during construction and erection, pipework should be cleaned by acid, detergent or solvent cleaning. Particular care shall be taken to ensure complete removal of any cleaning fluids. Low point drains should be provided to reduce the drying time; if a water-based cleaning fluid is used, the dew point of the residual air following rinsing and drying should be at least 10 °C below the minimum system service temperature.

For solvent cleaning, because of the health and explosion hazards, inhibited 1,1,1-trichloroethane or inhibited dichloromethane (or methylene chloride) are the preferred solvents (see NOTE below), dependent upon local regulations. A comparison of cleaning agents can be found in CGA/GAS 02-DIR.

NOTE: Certain processes may be adversely affected by solvent residue (e.g. poisoning of catalyst in ethylene oxide reactors). Under these circumstances, the Principal should be contacted for alternative solvents and/or cleaning procedures.

Following cleaning, an organic solvent may be considered to have been completely removed following cleaning when its concentration in the residual gas (air or nitrogen) is below the value specified by the Principal (for trichloroethane, this value is 20 mg/l).

Any in-situ cleaning required should be carried out after any hydrotesting and by specialist contractors.

A system that is complete and has received final cleaning, but is not to be put into immediate service, shall be preserved by pressurising with clean, dry, oil-free nitrogen.

The following precautions may be required:

- use demineralized water tested for Total Oxygen Content;
- use socks to ensure absence of particulates during final flushing and ambient nitrogen blowing;
- apply final blowing with hot nitrogen to ensure dew point of -20 °C.

## 6.4 TRANSPORT LINES

Long carbon steel transport lines may not be so easily cleaned as small parts. In particular, the filling of long lines with solvent may present the danger of its incomplete removal. The hydrostatic pressure test also presents a problem because it will cause subsequent rusting. The pipes should therefore be locally cleaned and inspected before they are joined together, under close control of working conditions.

In cases where hydrostatic testing can be replaced by a pneumatic test (with prior 100 % non-destructive inspection of welds), step (a) of the following procedure shall be carried out using oil-free filtered air or nitrogen instead of water.

a) Clean line with cleaning pig (scraping and wire brushing type) driven with clean oil-free potable water or boiler feed water. Repeat until water remains clean. (Perform hydrotest with the water contained in the line).

b) Empty line by means of pigs (ensure that "swabbing pigs" are non-linting), driven with oil-free filtered nitrogen.

c) Dry by flushing with oil-free filtered nitrogen.

d) Close off line and conserve by maintaining nitrogen pressure at 0.5 bar (ga).

An alternative method of cleaning transport lines is the use of "sand jetting", using grit and compressed nitrogen. This should be considered if available at the site in question.

## 6.5 INSPECTION FOR CLEANLINESS

### 6.5.1 General

It is necessary to verify that no rust, dust, dirt, or any other particulate matter is present in the system.

NOTE: No rust is impossible; the maximum amount should be specified, see IGC 33/97.

The maximum allowable concentration of oil on the metal surface is 1 g/m². Oil is then not visible, see (Appendix 2).

### 6.5.2 Direct inspection

All surfaces shall be subjected to direct visual inspection where possible, by both bright white light and ultra violet light methods as described in ASTM G93. The inspected surface shall be dry and free from:

- cleaning agents;
- adhered scale, weld spatter, loose particles or fibres;
- organic substances such as grease, oil, paint, sealants.

If direct inspection by ultra-violet light is not possible, surfaces shall be wiped with a clean, white, lint-free cloth that may be dipped in solvent (trichloroethane or dichloromethane), (6.3). The cloth shall then be inspected to ensure the absence of any oil, residue, discolouration, fibres, etc.

### 6.5.3 Indirect inspection

Parts which are inaccessible to visual inspection, either directly or with a boroscope or similar instrument, shall have solvent (trichloroethane or dichloromethane) circulated through them. A representative sample of the effluent solvent shall be taken and inspected as follows:

- the sample shall be inspected under bright white light and compared with a sample of new, unused solvent. No difference shall be detectable and there shall be no residue;
- the sample shall also be inspected under ultra-violet light (wavelength between 0.32 µm and 0.38 µm) and again compared with unused solvent. Contamination of 0.1 g oil/litre or more will cause fluorescence of the solvent;
- in doubtful cases, an analysis of the solvent used may be required. After evaporation at 100 °C, the sample shall not contain more than 20 mg/kg residue.

### 6.5.4 Target blowing

Position a white sheet covered with petroleum jelly, e.g., Vaseline, at the end of the line, and blow line with oil-free nitrogen. Dirt picked up by the nitrogen stream will be visible on the sheet.

## 7. CONSTRUCTION

### 7.1 CLEANLINESS PRECAUTIONS

Equipment used for the erection of an oxygen system should not be assigned to other work during the entire job. Transfer of personnel from other work should also be avoided to reduce the risk of oil contamination of the system.

Special care shall be taken to ensure that clothes and shoes of personnel working on the oxygen system bear no oil or grease and that all tools are completely free from oil, grease or other organic matter. Clean gloves shall be used.

All materials and equipment used for the oxygen system shall be stored and labelled in such a way that they are positively separated from other construction materials and equipment is achieved. Cleaning, packing and jointing materials shall be kept in closed/sealed containers until they are used.

Before installation commences, each part shall be carefully checked for cleanliness and, if required, be cleaned in accordance with (6).

During field jointing of pipe, seals and covers shall be kept in place until immediately before jointing in order to avoid ingress of foreign matter. Any oily matter that has contaminated the pipe shall be removed and the cleanliness shall be checked (wipe test) before each joint is made.

All valves should be carefully monitored for defects, rough edges, etc., prior to installation, and polished if necessary.

### 7.2 WELDING

All welding shall be performed by qualified welders using qualified welding procedures. Qualification tests for welders and welding procedures shall comply with ASME IX.

**Amended per Circular 35/03**

On pressure-containing welds, only full penetration welds shall be made; the root pass shall be done by the TIG welding process. Socket welds and fillet welds are not permitted. Gas welding is not permitted.

Selection of welding consumables and application of welding procedures shall be in accordance with DEP 30.10.60.18-Gen.

### 7.3 INSPECTION AND TESTS

All butt welds shall be 100 % radiographed. Interpretation of radiographs shall be in accordance with API 1601, ASME VIII, or ASME B31.5.

To ensure that the piping is smooth and obstructions, a gauge having a diameter of 2 mm less than the inside diameter of the pipe (taking into account the permitted tolerances) shall be passed through each section after welding.

Pressure testing shall be performed in accordance with the requirements of DEP 31.38.01.31-Gen. with oil-free potable water or boiler feed water. No detergents shall be added. Special care shall be taken that the system is dried after testing to prevent subsequent rusting of carbon steel.

It should be borne in mind that drying and cleaning carbon steel piping after pressure testing with water may be difficult. If allowed by the Principal, a pressure test with oil-free air or nitrogen may be substituted.

## 8. OPERATION AND MAINTENANCE

### 8.1 GENERAL

Operation and maintenance personnel shall be properly trained and instructed about the risks caused by an excess or deficiency of oxygen. Personnel shall wear suitable clean clothing, free from oil and easily combustible contaminants. Smoking shall be forbidden where there is any possible risk of oxygen enrichment. Personnel who have been exposed to enriched atmospheres shall not be allowed to approach fires, burning cigarettes, etc., until after thorough ventilation of their clothing.

### 8.2 OPERATION

The oxygen system shall be operated in such a way that the design and safety considerations are obeyed. For carbon steel and stainless steel systems, the velocities shall remain within the limits shown in Figure 1.

Block valves that are not equipped with a pressure-equalising bypass shall be in open position during pressurising and depressurising operations.

Pressurising, depressurising, venting and purging the system shall be done only in the normal direction of flow. This also precludes back blowing of installed filters. If purging is performed, the operator shall ensure that the nitrogen is free from contaminants and that the nitrogen pressure is and will remain higher than the oxygen pressure before hooking up the nitrogen system.

After cleaning, filters shall be pressurised, preferably with nitrogen, using the connection at the inlet.

For the operation of compressors, see DEPs 31.29.40.30-Gen. (centrifugal) and 31.29.40.31-Gen (reciprocating). Additional information may be found in (10).

### 8.3 MAINTENANCE

Before any inspection, maintenance or repair work on oxygen systems may commence, all oxygen shall be removed by venting and subsequent purging with oil-free nitrogen.

If internal inspection of equipment or piping parts is necessary, these shall be separated from the oxygen system by complete removal or by placing clean spades (having visible handles) at all connections. Before personnel enter, all nitrogen shall be replaced by air and adequate ventilation shall be maintained.

Repair work shall be carried out in accordance with (4) and (6), with particular reference to the safety precautions mentioned in (6.1). It is desirable to have a clean working area available.

## APPENDIX 2    EFFECTS OF OILY CONTAMINATION

Regarding the concentration of oily contamination of metal surfaces, the following information may contribute to a better understanding of the problem.

- At a concentration of 1 g/m² oil is not visible and smears cannot be detected when the surface is rubbed with a finger and the area inspected under a bright white light.
- At 2.7 g/m² the surface has a slightly oily appearance but no smearing is discernible after testing as above.
- At 5.4 g/m² the oil will flow and a slight smear can be detected.
- At 10.8 g/m² smearing is considerable.
- At 27 g/m² the oil can be pushed with a finger.

NOTE:    The most rigorous cleaning can reduce oily contamination to about 0.05 g/m².
