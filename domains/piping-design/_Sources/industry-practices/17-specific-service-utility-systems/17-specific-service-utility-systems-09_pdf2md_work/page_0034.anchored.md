#### 7.3.1.3 *(Addition)* **Low Flow Protection**

Listed below are three options for preventing low flow conditions, along with some associated operational problems. See the *Pump Manual* and the *Instrumentation and Control Manual* for more information.

1. Route pump seal and case vents back to the suction vessel. These vents can be left open if the pump is adequately sized.

   Operational problems:

   - These vents may be inadvertently blocked off when switching service, or left open to the wrong vessel with the potential for product cross-contamination.
   - This option may be inconvenient if venting is required frequently.

2. Route the minimum flow bypass back to the suction vessel. The operational problems of this alternative are similar to "1." above.

3. Install low-flow shutdown instrumentation. Use of low-flow shutdown switches is quite common. Generally speaking, the three major kinds of low flow switches are:

   - Mechanical (e.g., paddle type)
   - Differential pressure (uses an orifice plate as a flow measurement device)
   - Thermal (uses a thermistor to measure the rate of heat dissipation in order to detect low flow)

At present, differential-pressure type low-flow switches are commonly used on LPG storage vessels. Paddle-type switches have been found unreliable by some users. Thermal-type switches need electrical power to the switch (either 110V AC or 24V DC) but do not cause a head loss. Refer to the Flow section of the *Instrumentation and Control Manual* for more details.

Cautions when using shutdown flow switches:

- If used as an automatic shutdown device, failure of the flow switch will unnecessarily shut down the operation
- The impact and sequence of an abrupt shutdown must be considered
- Some shutdown logic (e.g., time delay) may be needed to avoid the effect of "water hammer."
