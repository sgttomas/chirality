# 1200 NH₃ Guidelines

**Abstract**

This section provides the Company's minimum standards for the design and construction of new anhydrous ammonia (NH₃) handling and storage facilities. Also included are anhydrous NH₃ recovery and handling facilities located within ammonia manufacturing plants and other processing units. Recommendations for upgrading existing anhydrous NH₃ facilities are provided as well.

Industry standard ANSI K61.1, Safety Requirements for the Storage and Handling of Anhydrous Ammonia, provides the basis for this section. Supplemental Company requirements are also covered in this section. This section does not restate all of the requirements of ANSI Standard K61.1 and has been prepared assuming the user will refer to ANSI Standard K61.1 when applying the requirements of this practice.

This section does not cover aqueous NH₃ facilities, refrigeration plants using anhydrous NH₃ as a refrigerant, anhydrous NH₃ transportation facilities, or anhydrous NH₃ manufacturing plants as exempted by ANSI Standard K61.1.

## 1210  Introduction

Anhydrous ammonia (NH₃) may be in either gaseous or liquid form. It should not be confused with aqueous ammonia, which is a solution of ammonia in water. For this section, ammonia and NH₃ shall be understood to mean anhydrous ammonia.

Anhydrous ammonia is a hazardous substance. When designing facilities for ammonia service, rigorous practices are warranted, because a failure may release gaseous ammonia or liquid ammonia that can quickly vaporize and produce large volumes of ammonia vapor.

Public complaints may occur at levels of 1 to 5 ppm, the odor threshold for sensitive people. Ammonia vapor presents a serious personnel hazard at concentrations as low as four hundred parts per million. Ammonia vapor is fatal almost immediately at a concentration of five thousand parts per million. Physical contact with liquid ammonia will produce severe skin burns.

Minimizing the potential for release of toxic concentrations of ammonia to the atmosphere requires high equipment reliability, avoiding the use of unnecessary equipment, minimizing the number of equipment connections, and protecting the facility against inadvertent damage or failure.

## 1230 Special Problems in Ammonia Systems

### 1231 Physiological and Health Effects

The Company's MSDS 500 and the NSC Data Sheet 1-251 should be consulted for the OSHA exposure standard, physiological and health effects, additional safety information, and first aid procedures.

Ammonia, under atmospheric temperature and pressure, is a colorless gas with a strong, unmistakable, pungent odor.

Gaseous ammonia is a potentially lethal substance. The degree of danger depends upon the concentration in the air breathed and on the time of exposure. The physiological effects of gaseous ammonia range from moderate eye and nasal irritation to serious edema and death.

Liquid ammonia is also hazardous to personnel. Liquid ammonia in contact with the skin will produce severe burns not only because of its corrosive action, but also through the freezing effect produced by rapid evaporation.

### 1232 First Aid and Personnel Protection

Ammonia plant personnel should be trained in safe operating practices and emergency procedures.

Safety shower/eyewash units shall be installed, according to the Company's *Safety in Designs Manual*, and maintained in good working order.

Self-contained breathing units and other safety equipment shall be provided in accordance with ANSI Standard K61.1.

### 1233 Ammonia Flammability

Ammonia is flammable in a concentration range of 16% to 25% in air at atmospheric conditions. It has an ignition temperature much higher than hydrocarbons, but can be ignited by any open flame or high energy spark from electrical equipment if the mixture is within the flammable range.

Because of the high lower flammable limit, spills of ammonia will not create flammable mixtures in open, well-ventilated areas except immediately near the release point. For this reason, the U.S. Coast Guard classifies ammonia as a nonflammable compressed gas as utilized in transportation. Certainly, however, pressure releases from compressors, pressure piping and storage, and pumps could create flammable

conditions within the release zone. There have been explosions and fires with ammonia under such conditions as well as within confined areas.

Thus, under most conditions ammonia should be considered a flammable mixture. The extent of flammability, however, should be subject to evaluation, particularly in regard to electrical area classification.

Ammonia has a low heat of combustion. Thus, pools of pure liquid anhydrous ammonia will not sustain burning unless other heat sources are present. This should be a factor when considering firefighting techniques and the need for fireproofing around ammonia equipment.

## 1234 Autorefrigeration and Brittle Fracture

The boiling point of liquid ammonia at atmospheric pressure is minus 28°F (minus 33.35°C) and is considerably lower than the normal ambient temperature.

Equipment could be subject to low temperatures in the event of a rapid depressurization, a liquid ammonia spill, or a flange leak. The temperature of ammonia discharging under pressure will drop to minus 28°F (minus 33.35°C) when expanding to atmospheric pressure. Atmospheric pools of liquid ammonia can cool to about minus 58°F (minus 50°C) because NH₃ vapor above the pool becomes buoyant and sets up convection currents that cause rapid evaporation. Because steel can have poor toughness at low temperature, this severe chilling significantly increases the risk of a brittle fracture. Refer to the Company's *Pressure Vessel Manual* for additional information on brittle fracture. Brittle fracture can be avoided in new facilities by utilizing construction materials that will retain their toughness at the lowest temperatures that can reasonably be expected. It is achieved in existing facilities by avoiding temperatures lower than existing materials can safely withstand.

### Pressure Drop

Pressure drops taken across control valves, relief valves, sample valves, etc., will cause a drop in temperature when NH₃ is expanded. This may cause freezing or hydrates to form if water is present. The possible temperature reduction in valves and piping should also be considered in the material selection.

## 1235 Accidental Releases

Federal law (CERCLA) requires that the release to the environment of hazardous substances, such as anhydrous ammonia, be immediately reported to allow necessary emergency response to protect the public and environment. Any anhydrous ammonia release of 100 pounds (17 gallons, 2000 SCF) or more within any 24-hour period must be reported to both the National Response Center (NRC) in Washington, D.C. (telephone 800-424-8802), and if exposure occurs to persons outside of the facility, to state and local emergency response agencies (as designated to comply with Federal Law SARA—Title III). Additional state and local agencies may also require notification.

## 1236 Stress Corrosion Cracking (SCC)

Carbon and low alloy steels are commonly used in anhydrous ammonia service, but they are susceptible to stress corrosion cracking (SCC) under some conditions. Consult with your materials engineer on a case-by-case basis to evaluate the potential for SCC and the best method to mitigate it. The initial cost of stress relief may be recovered through reduced inspection frequency. Refer to Sections 1242, 1243, and 1244 for stress relief requirements exclusive of SCC considerations.

#### Air Contamination

Stress corrosion cracking is caused by air contamination. Even a few parts per million of air in the ammonia can result in SCC.

#### Water Injection

The addition of 0.2% water to anhydrous ammonia will prevent SCC in the liquid phase. However, because ammonia is more volatile than water, SCC may still occur in the vapor phase, which will contain much less water than the liquid.

#### Stress Relief

Stress relief will prevent SCC if properly done. There have been reports of cracks in stress-relieved equipment, but these are generally attributed to stress relieving temperatures that were too low, nonstress-relieved repairs in stress-relieved equipment, or cracks that were missed during an inspection, repair, and stress-relieving operation.

#### Low Strength Steels

High strength steels crack more readily than lower strength steels. Though there are no strength levels that can be considered immune, the lower strength steels up to and including ASTM A516 Grade 70 present a much lower risk of cracking than the high strength quenched and tempered steels like ASTM A517. Though there is an extensive history of successful use of A517 for highway tank trailers, current practice is to use A517 only if stress-relieved.

#### Weld Hardness

Welding procedures that minimize weld metal and heat-affected zone hardness will reduce the risk of SCC. With steels up through Grade 70 (70 ksi minimum tensile) strength, it is not unreasonable to specify a maximum bulk weld metal hardness of 200 Brinnell.

## 1237 Corrosion

Copper, silver, zinc, tantalum, and their alloys are attacked by ammonia. These materials should not be used in ammonia service. Viton is an unacceptable elastomer for ammonia service. See the *Corrosion Prevention Manual* for further guidance. Materials selection and corrosion allowance should be reviewed with your materials engineer.

## 1240 Design and Construction Requirements

### 1241 Site Layout

#### Dispersion Modeling and Risk Assessment

Dispersion modeling may be performed to determine potential ammonia concentrations in air in the event of an accidental release. Modeling results and risk assessment may be used to develop guidelines on what design criteria are needed to mitigate potential exposure to the community or plant personnel. Possible design criteria affected by the risk assessment are the location of ammonia storage tanks, where the ammonia facility should be located within the plant. Modeling results may also be used to develop emergency response plans for responding to accidental releases.

The Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory measured concentrations resulting from actual releases of pressurized liquid ammonia at a test site in Nevada. Approximately 20% of the liquid that was released flashed to vapor because of the pressure drop to atmospheric conditions. Test results indicated that very little of the remaining liquid formed a pool on the ground. Most of the liquid was entrained into the atmosphere in the form of fine liquid droplets or aerosols. This phenomena results in substantially greater quantities of ammonia being entrained into the atmosphere than if a pool were formed.

The fact that very little liquid ammonia may pool on the ground should be considered in designing impoundments for nonrefrigerated storage. Remember, ammonia spills from refrigerated storage will pool. Also, ammonia gas, by itself, is lighter than air, but the liquid aerosol cloud has an overall density greater than air. This dense cloud can travel substantial distances downwind, evaporating as it travels.

Ammonia concentrations resulting from a release are highly specific to release rates and meteorological conditions. Each situation should be modeled on a case-by-case basis. CRTC's Health, Environment and Safety Group has licensed a model developed by Energy Analysts Inc. called the Energy Analysts Hazards Analysis Package (EAHAP) which is specifically designed to predict concentrations from releases of hazardous chemicals. EAHAP can predict concentrations as a function of distance from the source.

Contact the Process Risk Assessment and Risk Management Unit of CRTC's Health, Environment and Safety Group for assistance in dispersion modeling and risk assessment. A wide variety of chemical releases other than NH₃ can be modeled. Releases can be either vapor or liquid. For liquid releases, the aerosol effect is included.

To predict concentrations, the following information is needed: hole size, storage tank capacity, percent of vessel occupied by liquid, whether the release is in the gaseous liquid space (for a liquid release the liquid head above the release point is needed), description of the spill surface, and dimensions and construction material of any dike.

## Setbacks from Property Limits and Adjoining Plants

When considering NH₃ storage within NH₃ manufacturing or other process facilities, or as a separate facility, minimum setbacks from adjoining property, highways, main lines of railroads, places of public assembly, and institutional occupancy must be as defined in ANSI Standard K61.1. The intent of these requirements is to minimize overall risk outside the Company property in case of large releases of ammonia. It provides a reasonable basis for initiation of prearranged emergency procedures to mitigate and control such incidents. When ammonia lines cross under railroad tracks or a public road, review the designs for construction with the appropriate authority and secure necessary permits.

Within the confines of Company facilities, additional consideration must be given to the proximity of adjoining plants, road access for fire protection, proximity of plant equipment, maintenance clearances for equipment access, safety equipment locations, and unencumbered walkways for safe access by personnel.

In ammonia manufacturing or other process facilities, flammable materials will be present in much of the equipment utilized. Their potential for fire and personnel exposure will be important considerations.

## Equipment Layout

Layout of equipment is particularly important in ammonia service. The spacing of equipment should primarily be based on the same principles that govern layout within hydrocarbon processing areas. While the emphasis with ammonia is control of ammonia releases, and not control of fire control as with hydrocarbon handling equipment, the major factor in both cases is accessibility for control and shutoff of the release. All reasonable separation should be made between vessels containing large volumes of ammonia and equipment with a potential for releasing ammonia, such as pumps, loading racks, drains, or even flanges. Consider prevailing wind direction. Consider orientation; for example, if a horizontal vessel should release, the greatest danger area is in line with the vessel's axis. Space major equipment to provide accessibility for control of ammonia releases and fire and to divide the risk.

Refer to the *Fire Protection Manual* for recommendations on equipment spacing within the plant area. The spacing for ammonia storage and handling facilities should be similar to that for product storage tanks and low-pressure plants. However, ANSI Standard K61.1 should be followed when it requires greater spacing for storage tanks.

## Impoundment

For ammonia storage, the principal concern is the health hazard that could result from the ammonia vapor cloud developed during a release. Unlike storage of flammable hydrocarbons, fire would only be of secondary consideration in the ammonia facilities. Liquid ammonia spill fires are not self-sustaining unless additional heat is supplied from outside sources. It is important in setting any impoundment size that local codes and equations be reviewed to assure compliance. The following recommendations are intended to minimize the ammonia vapor cloud size and to contain any residual liquid.

**Refrigerated Storage.** For refrigerated storage, an impoundment capacity based on 100% of the volume of the largest vessel or tank in the diked area must be considered. This is in agreement with ANSI Standard K61.1 and provides a very conservative size.

**Nonrefrigerated Storage.** For nonrefrigerated storage, the quantity of flash vaporization and several other major quantities should be deducted to establish a need and size of any impoundment. Vaporization due to heat pickup by the liquid from the ground, evaporation from the accumulated liquid pool during the spill period, and the entrained liquid that is carried as an aerosol into the vapor cloud are such credits.

**Impounding Area Design.** The impounding area should be designed to minimize the surface area of contained liquid and thus minimize both evaporation and vaporization rates from heat pickup.

The most probable source of a leak leakage is a piping connection. Such a leak requires only a small amount of the designed impounding capacity.

The ground should slope away from the tank and piping manifold areas. A low, 2-foot to 3-foot dike situated 25 to 30 feet away from the tank—or a comparable trench—would restrict the flow of a partial spill from a pipe. A release that exceeds the capacity of this partial retention area could be run by ditches to low points within the impounding area.

It would be preferable to have sections within the impounding area that are much deeper than the average depth so that ammonia liquid could build up with minimum surface area during the initial accumulation period.

**Construction Materials.** The surface area of any drainage ditches and the impounding area should not be constructed of loose material such as gravel or rock. Rather, a damp compacted clay is preferable, so that a small amount of moisture could freeze upon contact with the liquid spill and act as a barrier to reduce further heat transfer. It is also important to avoid very wet soil or standing water that will increase the heat transfer before the surface becomes frozen. Good drainage as well as optimum soils or surfaces inside an impounding area will tend to keep vaporization to a minimum.

**Grading Design.** Design of grading under or near vessels and in drainage ditches should have a minimum slope of one percent.

**Segregation from Hydrocarbon Storage.** Ammonia storage vessels should not be located in the same impounding areas as hydrocarbon storage.

**Low Temperature and Hydrostatic Pressure.** Dikes or walls enclosing the impoundment should be designed for the appropriate low temperature and hydrostatic pressure.

## 1242 Columns, Vessels, and Exchangers

### Design and Construction

Design and construction of columns, vessels, and exchangers shall conform to Section VIII, Division 1 or Division 2, of the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code. The *Pressure Vessel Manual* also provides advice regarding good engineering practices for pressure vessel construction and use and cites applicable statutory regulations.

**Minimum Pressurizing Temperature.** Minimum pressurizing temperature (MPT) should be determined according to Section 400 of the *Pressure Vessel Manual*. The intent is to select materials, or consider materials, suitably resistant to brittle fracture at the temperature that would result from autorefrigeration if the vessel suddenly depressurized. Normal practice is to design for depressurizing to 40% of the maximum allowable working pressure. Precaution should be taken to ensure that the equipment does not autorefrigerate below the MPT when filling or being removed from service.

### Fabrication Specification

All columns and vessels should also conform to Company Specification PVM-MS-4749, Carbon and Low Alloy Steel Pressure Vessels for Critical Service, or be of equivalent quality, except in those instances where other more rigorous specifications apply because of thick shells or use of special alloys.

### Radiography

Full radiography of welds is required in accordance with the ASME code.

### Heat Treatment

Postweld heat treatment (stress relief) of carbon steel is required when specified by ASME Code or where other special service conditions demand it. The use of postweld heat treatment significantly improves resistance to brittle fracture and should be considered for that purpose, except where a metallurgical review indicates that the as-welded material has suitable toughness. Postweld heat treatment greatly reduces the probability of stress corrosion cracking, as discussed in Section 1236.

### Connections

Minimize the number of connections. The use of bridles for level instruments is one example.

### Connections Below Liquid Level

A valve should be installed on each nozzle below normal liquid level on columns and vessels. Flanged nozzles and valves shall be ANSI Class 300 minimum. Valves shall be used with confined bonnet gaskets. On horizontal storage vessels it is desirable to locate all valve connections for liquid piping outside the supporting piers. This makes the valves more accessible and avoids possible release of liquid ammonia under the main portion of the vessel.

#### Ammonia Leakage Into Exchangers

Consider the possibility and consequence of ammonia leakage into the other side of all exchangers, especially cooling water systems. Consider using leak detection and overpressure protection devices.

#### Repairs/Alterations

Repair or alteration must be done in compliance with API Standard 510, Pressure Vessel Inspection Code, or the code under which the vessel was initially constructed, as well as any state or local jurisdictional requirements.

## 1243 Tanks

### Design and Construction

Refrigerated ammonia tanks shall be designed and constructed in accordance with the ASME Code or API Standard 620 Appendix R, whichever is applicable.

### Temperature

The design temperature should be the minimum temperature to which the tank will be refrigerated, and should be so designated. Precautions should be taken to ensure the tank does not autorefrigerate below this temperature when being removed from service or refilled.

### Marking Refrigerated Tanks and Vessels

Each refrigerated tank or vessel should have a nameplate (in addition to the code-required nameplate) in an accessible place on the outer covering. This nameplate should be imprinted with the following information:

- Name and address of the builder.
- Date of fabrication.
- Maximum product volume or weight, whichever is most meaningful to the user.
- Design pressure.
- Minimum temperature for which the container was designed (°F).
- Maximum water level to which container may be filled for test.
- Density of the product for which the container was designed (lb/cu ft).
- Maximum level to which the container may be filled with liquid anhydrous ammonia.

### Tank Valves, Fill Pipes, and Discharge Pipes

Fire resistant shutoff valves must be provided for all connections except for thermowells. Where required by ANSI Standard K61.1, excess flow and back pressure check valves must also be installed. The positive shutoff valve should be located at the tank or as close to the tank as practical, consistent with the installation requirements of ANSI Standard K61.1.

When operating conditions make it advisable, a remotely operated shutoff valve should be installed on connections located below the maximum liquid level. See under Valve Selection in Section 1244.

## Repairs/Alterations

Tanks constructed per API Standard 620 shall be repaired or altered in accordance with API Standard 620 and any state or local requirements. See Section 1242 for ASME Code requirements.

# 1244 Piping

## Piping Code

As a minimum all piping should conform to the requirements of the latest edition of ANSI/ASME B31.3, Chemical Plant and Petroleum Refinery Piping, or to the design and construction project piping specifications where stricter standards were used in the design. Piping should be ANSI Class 300.

## Piping Manual

General piping design should follow the guidelines set out in the *Piping Manual*, with emphasis on flexibility to resolve thermally induced stresses.

## Fabrication Specification

All piping fabrication should conform to the general requirements of Company Specification PIM-EG-2505, Carbon Steel Piping Fabrication.

## Flanges and Valves

Minimize the number of flanges and flanged valves. Where flange leaks could cause direct ammonia impingement on columns or vessels, consider providing metal shields. In cold service piping (below minus 20°F), a low-temperature grade of carbon steel must be used. Refer to ANSI/ASME B31.3 Section 323.2.2 to select materials for cold service piping.

## Piping NPS 2 and Larger

Piping NPS 2 and larger shall be butt-welded.

## Piping NPS 1½ and Smaller

Minimum NPS is ¾-inch and minimum wall thickness Schedule 80. Small piping, NPS ¾ to 1½, should be socket-welded, or completely seal-welded if screwed piping is used. Socket-welded is preferred; seal-welded is acceptable but care is required to ensure that all exposed pipe threads are covered. Use small (NPS ¾ to 1½) piping only for auxiliary lines that can be separated from the main operating lines by a valve located in the root position.

Root valves shall be bridge-welded per Company Standard Drawing GD-L1057. Examples are drains, sample connections, and instrument root valves. Unions are not allowed.

## Stress Relief

Stress relief of piping is required where specified by code, where other service conditions demand it for prevention of stress corrosion cracking, or where required to meet weld hardness limits. All cold service piping NPS 2 and larger, operating below minus 20°F shall be stress-relieved.

NPS 1½ and smaller pipe and valves do not require stress relief in socket-welded or seal-welded screwed construction, but qualified weld procedures and welders *for cold service materials are required.*

## Gaskets

All gaskets shall be the confined type. Spiral-wound gaskets with 1/8-inch-thick centering rings are preferred. "Grooved" (serrated) design gaskets with centering rings should not be used; this type of gasket has a proven problem with leakage in moderate and higher pressure ammonia services. Viton is not to be used in ammonia service; Buna-N is acceptable.

Other types of confined flanges and gaskets are acceptable for exchanger shell flanges and for tank and vessel manways.

## Insulation

Prior to insulating, a protective coating must be applied to the exterior of cold lines and equipment that are cold enough to sweat. This will mitigate external corrosion.

Refer to the *Coatings Manual* and *Insulation and Refractory Manual* for guidance in selecting suitable materials for specific operating environments.

## Valve Selection

Conventional gate and globe valves shall be outside screw and yoke (OS&Y) design per API Standard 600, Steel Gate Valves, Flanged and Buttwelding Ends. All valves, as a minimum, shall be fire safe and meet ANSI/ASME/API standards. Body-bonnet closures may be bolted, welded, or pressure seal style with a secondary mechanical retention feature.

Screwed bonnets are acceptable on instrument valves when installed outboard of a piping class root valve. The screwed bonnet requires a mechanical retainer to prevent loosening when under operating pressure.

**Valve Trim.** Valve trim materials, specialty features, and functional application should be reviewed for compliance with project and facility process design requirements.

**Valve Materials Not To Use.** Do not use cast iron, brass, copper, or galvanized valve materials in ammonia service.

**Viton.** Do not use Viton for seats, packing, or O-rings in ammonia service.

**Small Valves.** For valves NPS 1½ and smaller a welded bonnet is recommended (equal to BTR/Vogt Fig. 2801 or Bonney Forge Fig. WL-10) to reduce the weight and to eliminate a potential leak point.

**Vibrating Service.** Refer to Appendix C for valve recommendations in vibrating service.

**Wafer Valves.** If flangeless wafer-type valves are considered, they should be the fully-lugged pattern. The full-lug style provides the necessary fire protection for the stud bolts.

**Ball Valves.** Orbit ball valves with a type "K" seat have been effective where tight shutoff is required. They offer an elastomer seal with a secondary metal-to-metal seating capability. Because they are more expensive than a standard gate valve, they are normally only used at primary shutoff points, e.g., block valves at fluid reservoirs, plot limit manifolds, and other points considered critical in an emergency.

## Branch Connections

Reinforced branch connections in ammonia service must be designed for cyclic service and use tees, weldolets and sockolets or thredolets. Table E in the Corporation Piping Specification (GB135169) meets this requirement. Miters greater than 15 degrees are not permitted.

**Vibration Design.** Design branch lines and connections to avoid resonant vibration. Root valves (NPS 1½ and smaller) should be bridge-welded in vibrating service in accordance with Standard Drawing GD-L1057. See the Company Piping Manual for vibration design criteria.

## Utility Connections

Minimize connections to utility systems and design them to positively prevent entry of ammonia into the utility system. See Figure 1200-1.

## Sample Connections

The number of ammonia sample connections should be minimized. Those that are provided should be designed to permit safe sampling. See Figure 1200-2.

Provide block valves on all piping and equipment connections used for sampling. The sample valve must be self-closing. Where operation requires that the self-closing valve be held in the open position for prolonged periods, a fusible-link type of self-closing valve and a remotely operated high-flow shutoff valve must be used.

A three-way valve permits the line to be flushed for a fresh sample and minimizes the possibility for accidental release to the atmosphere.

Locate the outlets of sampling and draw-off lines from ammonia vessels and storage tanks outside the shell drip line so that, if a leak develops, there is no possibility of ammonia impinging on the shell or supports.

## Protection From External Damage

Avoid areas with vehicular traffic when routing lines. Where it is necessary to route near roads and railroads, provide adequate protection for the pipe. Install pipelines aboveground where possible. Where aboveground lines must be routed underground for short distances, as under roads, provide highly reliable protection against frost heaving and external corrosion. Refer to API RP 1102. Spectacle blinds are not acceptable for ammonia service. The convenience of this device does not justify the increased hazards of lighter flanges, fewer bolts, the gasket materials used, and personnel exposure.

## 1245 Pumps and Compressors

### Centrifugal Pumps

If a centrifugal pump is required, select a quality pump which conforms to ANSI B73.1 or B73.2. The case should be a low-temperature grade carbon steel or 304/316 stainless steel. Always use a mechanical seal. Use a close clearance throttle bushing in the gland outboard of the mechanical seal and plug the drain/vent holes. The pump should have a minimum working pressure of 250 psig, in accordance with ANSI Standard K61.1.

### Reciprocating Pumps

Avoid the use of reciprocating pumps in ammonia service.

### Centrifugal Compressors

When a compressor is needed, consult Company mechanical equipment specialists.

### Instrumentation

The overriding instrumentation design objective is to minimize the opportunity for accidental release of ammonia. This is accomplished through use of reliable instrumentation, by minimizing the number of connections and the amount of instrument piping and tubing that contain ammonia, and by maximizing the structural integrity of piping and tubing. Specific requirements follow.

**Instrument Piping.** Except for orifice flange connections, pipe and valves connected to vessels and process piping shall be NPS ¾ minimum size. NPS 2 and larger connections will normally be flanged. Minimize flanges in NPS ¾ to 1½ sizes except where required for thermowells and control valves. Root valves are required to permit isolation of instrument systems from process systems. Instrument piping shall conform to the piping class used for the process system. Refer to Item 6 of Section 1244 for bridge welding requirements.

**Instrument Tubing.** Use of ½-inch OD, 0.065-inch wall seamless stainless steel tubing and stainless steel compression fittings (Parker or Swagelok) for instrument leads are recommended.

**Thermowells.** Thermowells must be flanged.

**Integrity.** Use reliable instruments to minimize redundant instrument systems. This, in turn, will reduce the number of process connections and thus reduce the chance of leaks.

**Installation Details.** Select instruments and installation details to minimize the amount of small pipe and tubing that carry process fluids. Mount transmitters as close as possible to the process connection. The use of sealing fluid (silicone oil) to displace the process fluid in instrument leads is recommended for differential pressure level instruments.

**Restriction Fittings.** Use restriction fittings in instrument leads to limit the amount of ammonia released in the event of a failure of the lead or instrument. Allowable

exceptions are float-operated level instruments where restriction fittings prevent proper instrument function, and in services where it can be clearly shown that plugging problems preclude their use. See the Company's Standard Drawing GB-41223 in the *Instrumentation and Control Manual* for details on restriction fittings.

**Level Indicators.** External level indicators are preferred for pressurized storage. Differential pressure (dp) cells should be considered for spheres. Both cells and external float chambers are suitable for horizontal vessels. Other types of level devices that can be isolated from the storage vessel also should be considered. Capacitance probes are an example.

When internal float-type gages are used—for example, for atmospheric storage—provide sealing valves (ball, plug, etc.) for maintenance use or emergency shutoff. Tape-view windows should be avoided, but as a minimum the viewing unit should have a self-closing shutter. Locate the float and guides so the filling stream does not impinge on the float. Consider the use of stilling wells to avoid damage to the tape.

Avoid the use of gage glasses. Where gage glasses are used, such as on the udders of spheres, normally-closed "deadman" valves should be installed on the leads. Frost-free glass extensions should be used on all gage glasses.

**Level Alarms.** Provide high level alarms for ammonia storage vessels. These alarms should be independent of the primary level gage or indicator to guard against a high level occurring as a result of the malfunction of the primary device.

**Signal and Control Leads.** The signal and control leads for those critical instruments and valves that are necessary for the control, shutdown, or isolation of ammonia equipment should be routed where they are unlikely to be exposed to or damaged by fire. Where this is not practical, these leads should be fireproofed within 50 feet of hazardous equipment such as pumps, compressors, or furnaces. See the *Insulation and Refractory Manual* for recommendations on fireproofing of critical valves, instrumentation, and shutdown systems.

**Vibration.** In vibrating service, consider the use of details similar to those shown in Appendix C.

## Atmosphere Monitoring for Ammonia

Consideration should be given to monitoring the atmosphere around ammonia facilities for low concentration ammonia leaks on the order of 20 ppm or less.

Sensors can be located near critical sources and the facility perimeter, and the instrument and alarm unit mounted in a remote control center.

# 1246 Foundations and Supports

## Differential Settlement

Give special consideration to the settlement of structures which could impose excessive stress on connections and piping. For adjacent equipment, foundations should be tied together where practical to avoid differential settlement. The use of piles should be considered where applicable. Also, consider providing permanent bench

marks to enable checking for differential settlement between piping and major equipment items.

## Materials Consideration

Within storage impounding and drainage areas, supports for vessels and piping should be designed to withstand the thermal shock encountered in an ammonia spill. Any fireproofing provided on these supports also should be suitable for the temperature generated by the spill.

## Refrigerated Storage Tanks

The design of foundations for refrigerated storage tanks should include protection against ground heaving due to freezing.

# 1247 Relief Systems

### General

Properly designed and installed pressure relief systems are essential to the integrity of ammonia handling and storage facilities. They limit the pressure which could develop under certain operating or emergency contingencies to levels which are within the allowable design of vessels and associated equipment. The overpressure protection system must also provide for safe disposal of relief materials.

### Industry Standards

Reference should be made to the following API publications to determine relieving loads and for design (including sizing), installation, and testing of pressure relieving facilities.

- API RP 520, Recommended Practice for the Design and Installation of Pressure Relieving Systems in Refineries; Part I—Design, and Part II—Installation.
- API RP 521, Guide for Pressure Relieving and Depressuring Systems.
- API Standard 2000, Venting Atmospheric and Low Pressure Storage Tanks.

### Design Considerations

Overfilling and fire are the two most important contingencies in sizing pressure relief protection for ammonia storage vessels and associated equipment.

Operationally, overfilling represents the greatest risk. The relief valves required to prevent overpressure when filling the storage container are usually sized to handle the maximum fill rate. Either the discharge from such relief valves must be tied to a closed disposal system, or positive design (including instrumentation) and operational steps taken to assure against overfilling.

Sizing of pressure relief valves for fire exposure is covered by API RP 520 Part I. The low heat of combustion and inability of ammonia to sustain a fire without outside heat input should be taken into account.

Overpressure for loading systems might occur because of shut-in on the discharge side of the loading pumps. Either the piping system can be designed to be suitable for the overpressure created or liquid relief valves can be routed back to the suction system.

## Installation Requirements

For ammonia storage tanks and spheres, pressure relief valves may discharge directly to the atmosphere if permitted by governing regulations. When such vapor releases go directly to the atmosphere, the following areas require detailed consideration.

**Discharge Stacks.** The discharge stacks should point directly upwards and be at a minimum of 7 feet above the vessel top or any equipment within 50 horizontal feet. Vapor releases from this arrangement will be significantly diluted with air while still within the jet momentum release plume (see API RP 521) and will minimize the chance for large cloud formation.

**Drain Holes.** Drain holes should be provided in the bottom of the discharge elbow to avoid buildup of water which could be frozen by ambient temperature or autorefrigeration or could form hydrates.

**Discharge Stack Support.** The vertical discharge stack from the pressure relief valve must be supported independently of the valve. Otherwise the flow reaction forces can impose stresses on the valve flanges resulting in leakage. All bolts should be installed in the discharge flange of the valve.

**Discharge Stack Covers.** Caps or hinged covers over the discharge stacks to prevent entry of rain or snow into the stack should be avoided. Hinged connections can rust and prevent full opening during safety release, creating high backpressure and severely reducing valve capacity. Caps can also freeze in place. If a cover is installed, drain holes should be provided in the bottom elbow.

## Closed Relief Systems

If a closed system for pressure relief discharge is utilized for regulatory or other reasons, the following design features must be taken into account.

**Sloping of Pipe.** The piping must be sloped without traps from the relief valve outlet flange to a blowdown or collecting drum where liquids can be removed. The piping must be designed for the thermal contraction stresses resulting from the autorefrigeration conditions.

**Brittle Fracture.** The material of the discharge piping and liquid drums should be selected to prevent brittle fracture due to shock chilling associated with flashing ammonia.

**Pressure Drop.** Pressure drop in the relief system must be adequately analyzed to avoid excessive back pressure on any pressure relief valve. All valves involved in the same contingency must be considered. API RP 520, Part I, and API RP 521 provide background on the limitations of various types of valves.

**Segregation From H₂S, CO₂.** If ammonia releases are handled through closed systems with other releases, care must be taken to assure that the ammonia is segregated from H₂S or carbon dioxide and to avoid plugging by ammonium sulfide or ammonium carbonate.

#### Pressure Relief Valve Selection

Pressure relief valves should be steel with flanged process connections and bolted bonnets. Small relief valves which are provided to protect against thermal expansion are acceptable with screwed and seal-welded or socket-welded process connections.

#### Block Valve Selection

Locked-open block valves under redundant full-capacity pressure relief valves on storage vessels permit testing and maintenance while the vessel is in service. They are allowed under Appendix M of Section VIII, Division 1, ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code, and should be considered except where prohibited by state or local regulations.

#### Thermal Relief

Provide steel thermal relief valves for all sections of piping that can be blocked in while pressurized and for the body of valves which seat against both upstream and downstream pressures. Refer to the *Instrumentation and Control Manual* for calculation procedures and valve recommendations for thermal expansion of liquids.

## 1248 Electrical Area Classification

If ammonia were to be released in ammonia handling and storage areas and not be readily dispersed, the area must be classified as Class I, Division 2, Group D. An example of such an area is an enclosed compressor building. See Section 1233.

## 1250 Vapor Control and Fire Protection

### 1251 General Considerations

Review the need for additional equipment and systems to minimize and control the effect of ammonia leakage or to extinguish fires. These facilities should be provided on the basis of probability, extent, and consequences of such an occurrence.

Prime factors to consider are identification of the release, isolation and shutoff, including accessibility to equipment, and dilution and control to minimize vapor spread. While firefighting is still a consideration around equipment handling only ammonia, it is secondary to the control of vapor releases. Fire water systems with monitors, hose reels and (possibly) deluge systems are the basic tools for controlling and diluting vapor releases. When exposure to the public is a concern, a specially designed water curtain may be useful. Automatic leakage and fire detecting shutdown systems; isolating and depressuring systems; atmospheric NH₃

monitors, fireproofing, and drainage, may provide further protection in an ammonia release or fire.

Each method has advantages and disadvantages, and may involve considerable maintenance to ensure that it will function when needed. Careful analysis is necessary to avoid neglect of some detail which could make a costly protective system inoperative in an ammonia release or fire.

## 1252 Specific Protection Methods

### Vapor Control Using Water Fog Systems

The primary hazard with ammonia is of personnel coming into contact with vapor or liquid ammonia. Therefore, vapor cloud control during an emergency is a top priority.

Liquid ammonia has an expansion ratio of 850:1. The absorption ratio of ammonia vapor in water is 1300:1 and is dependent on water temperature (cool water absorbs more than hot water).

Vapor cloud control with water, especially with a water fog, is very effective. In addition to the high absorption characteristics of ammonia in water, water fogs provide an air pump effect which tends to dilute the vapor with air, reducing the ammonia content in the spreading cloud.

Monitors with spray nozzles should be mounted on the periphery of storage facilities, and hydrants should be available to accommodate portable monitors on the downwind edge of tanks in case of a spill.

Depending upon wind conditions and the size of the release, access to manually operated portable monitors downwind of a spill can have definite limitations. Where there are special risks downwind, remotely operated water fog systems should be considered. Whether consisting of a series of fire monitors with specially designed nozzles or of fixed fog nozzles, the water fog system must be located so as to minimize the water spray on any liquid ammonia. Water spray onto a liquid ammonia pool will result in very rapid "boiling" of the pool and almost immediate vaporization.

Monitors also should be located to cover any pumps or compressors and loading racks so as to provide for dilution of a release at the source point.

Avoid direct impingement of water on equipment nozzles and other components undergoing autorefrigeration to mitigate the risk of brittle fracture.

### Minimizing Heat Input

Considerable fire exposure can be tolerated by equipment which is full of liquid, equipment that is insulated to a nominal degree, and equipment whose vapor spaces can be kept wet with water. Facilities, particularly fixed monitor nozzles, should be installed to keep vessels, especially pressurized vessels, wetted down during exposure to fire. However, as stated in Section 1252 under Vapor Control Using Water

Fog Systems, water should not be sprayed on a liquid ammonia pool in an impounding area.

Insulation and weathercoating should be selected to give maximum fire resistance; for instance, stainless steel weathercoating should be used instead of aluminum.

## Fire Water System Design

A fire water system will generally be required around ammonia handling and storage facilities to provide water for diluting releases, to enable access for closing valves, and to handle fires. The size of the fire water system will depend on the amount of and size of the equipment handling NH₃. The system should be looped so that the area is normally supplied through two directions. The pumping system as well as fire monitor locations, hose reel locations, and hydrants should be based upon principles covered in the *Fire Protection Manual*. In storage areas where dilution of NH₃ vapor with water is anticipated, the size of the system may be determined by the number of nozzles that could be put into operation at one time.

## Fireproofing

In areas exclusively handling ammonia, fireproofing may practically be reduced to one to two hours and fireproofing of pipe supports may be eliminated. The prime areas of concern are within 25 feet of pumps, compressors or other equipment handling ammonia liquids or vapors under pressure where releases could be anticipated. Fireproofing of bolted connections is particularly important. Where hydrocarbon handling equipment is also present in the immediate vicinity, the requirements for this equipment must be considered.

In any case, fireproofing is not required for saddles with a minimum web height of 9 inches above a horizontal vessel support structure. This includes exchangers larger than 16 inches in diameter. Normally, fireproofing of vessels and structures above 30 feet in height is not justified.

Anchor bolts for equipment such as columns and vessels should not be fireproofed. Anchor bolts may experience severe corrosion problems if they are fireproofed.

Refer to the *Insulation and Refractory Manual* for recommendations on how much fireproofing to use for process plant supporting structures and where to use it. Acceptable types of fireproofing and installation considerations are also covered there.

## Vessel Connections

Bolts in manholes, flanges, and other bolted connections are the most vulnerable parts of vessels containing ammonia during a fire, since they are not cooled by the evaporating liquid. Bolted connections should be made only as they are required for maintenance or inspection and should be located where they would be least affected by a fire.

## Isolating Valves

Install block valves in protected locations; these valves should be operable in case of accidental release or fire to prevent discharge of stock into the atmosphere from

long lines. Provide block valves where ammonia lines enter or leave process or storage areas.

Because a vapor cloud can rapidly develop after an ammonia release, consideration should be given to providing remotely operated valves on certain types of equipment. These include pumps, compressors, and bottom connections on storage vessels in ammonia service, particularly those which might be normally open for loading or unloading. Remote shutdown valves should also be considered on lines to loading racks, particularly if flexible hose is used for loading connections. Remotely stopping ammonia pumps and compressors should also be considered.

## 1260 Identification of Ammonia Lines and Equipment

Ammonia lines should be paint-stenciled for identification at important control points such as valve manifolds and plot boundaries, and throughout plants which have a large number of ammonia lines or that handle sizable volumes of ammonia. See the *Safety in Designs Manual* for guidance.

Hazard signs and "yellow lining" of equipment boundaries should comply with the requirements of the *Safety in Designs Manual*.

## 1270 Existing Facilities

Most Company facilities already meet the standards presented in this section. Where they do not, they must be evaluated and a priority system developed to upgrade the facility on the basis of relative importance and potential danger.

Where massive replacement or renovation of a piping system is judged necessary and no immediate personnel risk is involved, the changes can be achieved during routine maintenance, scheduled shutdowns, or when planned modifications are made. Where a detected personnel hazard exists, the need to eliminate that specific hazard should justify a timely expenditure.

### 1271 Upgrading Checklist

When upgrading existing facilities, the following items are considered of greatest importance and should be checked for deficiencies. They are listed in a possible order of priority. Correction of any deficiencies should receive early attention.

1. Install appropriate warning signs and color coding. See the *Safety in Designs Manual* for guidance.
2. Install breathing air systems or self-contained breathing apparatus.
3. Install safety shower and eyewash units.
4. Install facilities for leak detection and containment appropriate for the location of the ammonia facility. Consider fixed area leak monitors and fixed water systems.

5. Seal-weld all screwed piping.

6. Replace any unconfined gaskets.

7. Eliminate piping smaller than NPS ¾. Use ½-inch OD, 0.035-inch wall minimum (0.065-inch wall preferred) seamless stainless steel tubing at instruments.

8. Review branch connection reinforcement and upgrade as good engineering judgment dictates.

9. Install restriction fittings in instrument leads. (See the Company Standard Drawing GB-J1223 in the *Instrumentation and Control Manual*.)

10. Remove unnecessary cross-connections and jumpovers.

11. Simplify piping by removing any dead legs and all out-of-service or unused piping systems.

12. Eliminate redundant backup instrumentation and make the primary instruments reliable.

## 1272 Inspection

Due to the potential hazard that can result from equipment and/or piping failure, an effective scheduled inspection program is essential. Inspection methods and scheduled frequency must address the probable nature and rate of corrosion. The objective is to provide effective insurance against unexpected failures or leaks. Refer to specific equipment safety instruction sheets (SIS) or local facility inspection records for additional details on plant inspection requirements.

### SCC Growth Rate

Stress corrosion cracks usually grow slowly. Therefore, the potential hazard due to SCC can be greatly reduced by an effective inspection program.

The only truly effective inspection method for detecting ammonia SCC is wet fluorescent magnetic particle inspection. Dry magnetic particle and dye penetrant inspection often find cracks but can be expected to miss many of them.

### Typical Locations for SCC

Ammonia stress corrosion cracks typically appear at welds, on the inside of the vessel. The cracks are most often transverse to the weld seam rather than parallel to it (nozzle welds excepted). This orientation is fortunate because a through-crack in this direction is more likely to simply leak than cause a catastrophic rupture.

## 1273 Facility Audit

Each facility should be audited annually. A typical audit checklist follows.

#### Housekeeping and Design

- Do facilities meet the design standard?
  - Are the valves under PSVs locked open?
  - Do remotely operated valves and excess flow valves operate properly? When were they last tested?
- Is inspection of this facility up-to-date?
- Are facilities in good condition?
- Are the facilities properly identified with vehicle barriers and hazard signs in place?
- Are safety shower/eye wash facilities adequate?
- Are fire fighting/vapor cloud control facilities (such as fire monitors) available near ammonia handling facilities?
- Are self-contained breathing units available near ammonia handling facilities?
- Are emergency suits available and in good condition? (Consult with your safety group about the need for emergency suits. They are typically provided for NH₃ handling facilities.)

#### Operations and Operator Awareness

- Are the operating standards adequate?
- Are written emergency procedures adequate?
- Do the operating standards address brittle fracture concerns when they are within the possible realm of operation?
- What percentage of operators are trained to wear an emergency suit?
- What percentage of shift supervisors are trained to wear an emergency suit?
- Are operators trained to respond to an ammonia release?
- Date of last ammonia release hypothetical?
- Are personnel trained on ammonia hazards and first aid?
- Is a copy of MSDS 500, Anhydrous Ammonia, available in the control room? Is the location of the Chemical Directory readily accessible?
