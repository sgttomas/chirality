# 1000 H₂S Guidelines

**Abstract**

This section recommends minimum standards for the design and construction of equipment containing moderate to high concentrations of hydrogen sulfide. These recommendations will satisfy the requirements of a major installation such as a gas plant or refinery. Additional supplemental specifications, standard drawings and recommended practices have been listed for these as well as other types of installations. The management of each facility must judge the extent to which they will be required to supplement the minimum standards recommended here.

## 1010  Introduction

This engineering guideline is applicable to environments containing H₂S, H₂S-hydrocarbon mixtures, and aqueous or amine solutions containing H₂S. Equipment is generally assumed to be carbon steel. However, the principles presented herein are equally applicable to alloy material.

In designing equipment for H₂S service, recognize that H₂S is fatally poisonous, even at concentrations as low as a few hundred parts per million in air. For this reason, the fundamental objective is to minimize the release of toxic H₂S to the atmosphere. This is accomplished by requiring high equipment reliability, minimizing the number of connections, avoiding unnecessary equipment, and protecting against damage and failure.

This engineering guideline applies primarily to the design of new facilities, although upgrading of existing facilities is discussed in Section 1080.

## 1020  H₂S Concentration Limits

The H₂S concentration in process fluids is used in this guideline to define the extent to which special design and construction standards must be applied. "High concentration" is defined as a gas containing over 24% H₂S by volume, or a liquid in which H₂S vapor pressure exceeds 4.0 psia at operating temperature. "Moderate concentrations" are gas streams containing 1% to 24% H₂S by volume, or liquid streams with an H₂S vapor pressure of 0.1 to 4.0 psia at operating temperature. For gas streams containing less than 1% H₂S (10,000 ppm) or for liquids having an H₂S vapor pressure below 0.1 psia ("low concentration"), equipment should be constructed in accordance with conventional standards for hydrocarbon service, while recognizing the material limitations discussed in Section 1045. For mixed phase streams, it is generally appropriate to consider only the H₂S concentration in the vapor phase.

These high, moderate and low concentration ranges were selected because they correspond to distinctive changes in H₂S concentration in many process plants containing H₂S. Special applications may require modification of these recommendations to take into account considerations such as very high or very low pressure or proximity to populated areas. See Section 1057 for some examples.

### 1031  Special Standards and Regulations

In some geographic locations, special standards and/or regulations may exist that will influence the design and construction of equipment or facilities handling H₂S.

Such standards and/or regulations must also be followed where they are legally required. Some examples are:

1. The U.S. Code of Federal Regulations, Title 30, Part 250 (30 CFR 250). This applies to drilling operations and required preventive measures for detection and control of H₂S in known and unknown reservoirs on the Outer Continental Shelf.

2. The Railroad Commission of Texas, Oil and Gas Division, Rule 36. This applies to all drilling, production, and transportation facilities within the state of Texas, if H₂S concentrations exceed 100 ppm by volume. Rule 36 does not apply to petroleum refineries. It imposes additional design and construction requirements beyond those outlined in this section.

3. NACE Standard MR-01-75, Material Requirements—Sulfide Stress Corrosion Resistant Metallic Material for Oil Field Equipment. This limits the use and hardness of many materials in H₂S service. Although it is a voluntary standard in most jurisdictions, it is a mandatory standard under the Railroad Commission of Texas, Oil and Gas Division, Rule 36 for most oil field and pipeline installations handling H₂S. While MR-01-75 is primarily applicable to producing installations, it is also used to identify acceptable valve trim and equipment materials for sour service.

4. Canadian requirements. Canadian provincial authorities may require that pressure vessels in H₂S service be 100% radiographed and postweld heat treated. Check regional requirements.

5. Other federal agencies or local jurisdictions. These may impose additional legal restrictions on the operation of facilities or equipment in H₂S service. To obtain information on current or pending legislation, contact Chevron Corporation's Health, Environment, and Loss Prevention staff in San Francisco, California.

# 1040 Special Problems in H₂S Systems

## 1041 H₂S Hazards

H₂S is a fatally poisonous gas. The degree of danger depends upon the concentration in the air breathed and on time of exposure. It is easily detected by smell at low concentrations, but at higher concentrations H₂S instantaneously deadens the sense of smell (olfactory nerves) so that there may be no warning of danger. H₂S is colorless. It is heavier than air, and can therefore collect in low spots if there is little air movement. H₂S gas is flammable, has a low ignition temperature of about 558°F, and forms explosive mixtures in air within the range of 4.3 to 45 volume percent.

## 1042 Pyrophoric Iron Sulfide

In an oxygen-deficient atmosphere, iron and steel will react with H₂S to form iron sulfide deposits on the surface of the metal. However, some iron sulfides are unstable in the presence of air. When equipment containing iron sulfide corrosion products or deposits is opened to the air, the iron sulfide will tend to revert to iron oxide and SO₂. Sometimes this chemical reaction occurs so rapidly that the iron sulfide actually glows.

Such a material is called **pyrophoric iron sulfide**. It constitutes a possible ignition source which must be considered during equipment shutdowns. Also, the evolved SO₂ can quickly render equipment unsafe for entry. Where large quantities of iron sulfide can accumulate, as in exchangers or packed columns, enough heat may be generated on air exposure to seriously damage the equipment.

It is not generally possible to predict in advance when an iron sulfide deposit will be pyrophoric and when it will not. However, iron sulfide which is wet with water or oil is less likely to experience spontaneous combustion than deposits which are dry. Keeping iron sulfide deposits water-wet is one way to prevent their rapid combustion.

## 1043 Solids Formation

Solids may form in H₂S-containing systems for a variety of reasons. Plants and facilities should be designed to prevent solids formation where this can reasonably be accomplished, and to permit easy removal if they form. The following types of solids can be encountered.

### H₂S Hydrates

At low temperatures and high pressures, H₂S reacts with water to form a hydrate. Once formed, the solid, hydrated compound can be removed only by increasing temperature or lowering pressure to decompose it. Hydrates do not dissolve in water, and addition of cold water will only cause more hydrate to form. Figure 1000-3 shows conditions under which hydrates will form.

### Sulfur

H₂S reacts with oxygen to form elemental sulfur. Deposits of sulfur are difficult to remove. To prevent sulfur formation, all air, including traces, must be excluded from systems containing H₂S. Any water added to H₂S-containing systems, such as makeup or onstream water wash, must be deaerated. The entry of oxygen into an H₂S-containing system not only causes sulfur formation, but also stimulates corrosion and scaling.

### Iron Sulfide

Iron sulfide is a black solid, insoluble in water. It is most often formed by H₂S corrosion of carbon steel equipment. However, dissolved iron in process streams will also precipitate as iron sulfide when the stream contacts H₂S. Catalytic units are especially sensitive to iron sulfide, since nominal quantities may cause plugging or

![Fig. 1000-3 Conditions of H₂S Hydrate Formation](figures/17-specific-service-utility-systems-08_p0008_fig01_fig-1000-3-h2s-hydrate-formation.png)

high pressure drop in catalyst beds. Sulfide scale in exchangers or furnace tubes will also cause loss of heat transfer.

## Ammonium Bisulfide

Ammonia and H₂S react to form ammonium bisulfide (NH₄HS). Formation is favored by large quantities of ammonia and H₂S, low temperature, and the absence of liquid water. Ammonium bisulfide deposits can be removed by water washing or by heating. They sublime at temperatures above about 300°F and are also very corrosive in aqueous service.

## Ammonium Bicarbonate or Carbonate-Carbamate

Depending on the source of the gas, H₂S-containing gases may contain ammonia or CO₂. When two process streams are mixed, one containing ammonia and the other containing CO₂, deposits of either ammonium bicarbonate or a mixture of ammonium carbonate and carbamate may form. These solids dissolve readily in water, and can be removed by water washing. They are also decomposed by heat, and can be removed by raising the temperature. In systems where unfavorable concentrations of ammonia and CO₂ are present, piping and instruments should be steam traced.

## 1044 Air Pollution

Air pollution requirements demand that the release of H₂S to the atmosphere be reduced to a minimum. Any source of H₂S leakage constitutes a source of air pollution as well as a personnel hazard.

H₂S has an obvious and unpleasant odor which is detectable even at 1 ppm concentration. It also causes blackening of some paints and tarnishing of some metals at even lower concentrations. Local regulations may restrict concentrations to less than 0.1 ppm.

## 1045 Material Considerations

The selection of construction materials for H₂S service requires special attention regardless of H₂S concentration. Other components in the process stream, such as NH₃, CN⁻, and brine, can significantly affect the stream's aggressiveness and should be considered. The following problems are common. Most of them are discussed in detail in the *Corrosion Prevention Manual*.

### General Corrosion

H₂S generally accelerates corrosion. However, under wet conditions, the rate of corrosion is greatly affected by the presence of other compounds such as ammonia, dissolved salts, or acidic substances. Copper alloys corrode rapidly in many H₂S services and are not recommended.

### Velocity-Induced Corrosion

High fluid velocities or turbulence often increase local corrosion. This is common in sour MEA or DEA, and in wet fluids containing both ammonia and H₂S.

### Sulfide Cracking

Ferritic steels, such as carbon steel, low alloys, Cr-Mo steels and precipitation hardenable stainless steels, can crack from exposure to aqueous solutions of H₂S if either the base metal or welds are too hard. To avoid the possibility of sulfide cracking of welds in carbon steel vessels, columns, exchangers, and piping, weld metal hardness should be limited to 200 BHN maximum. On compressors and other mechanical equipment, avoid use of alloys stronger than 90 ksi yield strength, or harder than 235 BHN (equivalent to about Rockwell C22). Welds in Cr-Mo steels should always be stress relieved. 18-8 stainless steels are immune to sulfide cracking and can be used for valve trim in those services where cracking of 12Cr trim is a recognized problem. In some sour services, low alloy steels and API 5LX pipe up to grade X65 have been used with success with a weld hardness of 235 BHN; however, this requires careful consideration of the aggressiveness of the sour environment. In general, carefully consider the possibility of sulfide cracking before using any material harder than 235 BHN.

### Amine Stress Corrosion Cracking

Steel piping and equipment in amine (e.g.; MEA, MDEA, DEA or DIPA) service may require stress relief to prevent stress corrosion cracking. Cracking can also

occur during steamout if equipment is not first water washed. Consult Materials and Equipment Engineering at CRTC for specific recommendations.

**Polythionic Acid Stress Corrosion Cracking**

This may cause failure of austenitic stainless steels that have been sensitized by welding, heat treatment, or long-time service at temperatures over about 750°F. Damage usually occurs during shutdowns when iron sulfide scale is exposed to moisture and oxygen; a soda ash wash prior to opening the equipment to air will prevent polythionic cracking.

**Hydrogen Blistering**

In H₂S-containing services, atomic hydrogen formed in the corrosion reaction may diffuse into the steel, causing blisters to form at internal voids or discontinuities.

## Pipe Connections

Minimize the number of flanged connections. Avoid the use of pipe unions.

**Gaskets.** Confined gaskets should be used for all flanges and manways where leakage would result in release of H₂S to the atmosphere. Raised face or small tongue-and-groove flanges with spiral-wound gaskets, or ring-joint flanges are acceptable.

**Flanges and Nozzles.** For high H₂S concentrations, flanges should be ANSI Class 300 minimum and nozzles NPS 2 minimum. For moderate H₂S concentrations, ANSI Class 150 flanges and NPS ¾ minimum size nozzles are acceptable.

#### Instrument Mounting and Pipe Bridles

On vessels handling high H₂S concentrations, avoid mounting instruments directly on the vessel. Instead, mount the instruments on a flanged pipe bridle so that level instruments can be isolated with block valves from the vessel for testing and repair. While most instrument mounting connections are smaller than NPS 2, others may require a larger bridle to handle the increased number and/or size of connections required with interface system design. Provide elevation-growth-style supports to accommodate the additional loading of pipe bridle and instruments.

## 1052 Tanks

Tanks should conform to Company Specification TAM-MS-967 or be of equivalent quality.

## 1053 Piping

### High H₂S Concentrations

For piping in high H₂S concentrations only, use ANSI Class 300 flanges as a minimum and provide 100% radiography of welds. Piping Class Q is preferred (see Corporation Specification GR135169). The N piping classes are acceptable if 100% radiographed.

### Moderate H₂S Concentrations

For piping in moderate H₂S concentrations, the H and N piping classes should be used within their temperature and pressure limitations. Random radiography is acceptable as a minimum; however, note the exception given under Vibrating Services, following.

### General Piping Requirements

The following requirements apply to all piping within the scope of this engineering guideline.

**Large Piping Connections.** Piping NPS 2 and larger shall be butt welded.

**Small Piping Connections.** Small piping connections, NPS ¾ to 1½, shall be socket-welded or screwed and completely seal-welded. Socket-welded connections are preferred; screwed connections with seal welds are acceptable, but be sure all exposed pipe threads are covered. Screwed connections must be free of pipe thread dope or tape prior to seal welding.

The minimum pipe size recommended is NPS ¾, and the minimum wall thickness for small piping shall be Schedule 80. Use small (NPS ¾ to 1½) piping only when an operating necessity. Bridge-weld short nipples per Standard Drawing

GD-L1057. Note that orifice flanges with a rating of ANSI Class 600 or less are furnished with NPS ½ root valve connections because of the available flange thickness. Bridge-weld these root valves to the flanges. Note that Section 1051 (under Flanges and Nozzles) limits nozzle size on vessels containing high concentrations of H₂S to NPS 2 minimum.

**Company Specifications.** All piping should conform to the general requirements of Company Specifications PIM-MS-2505, 4770, or 4772, or equivalent quality. Compliance with the weld hardness limit is crucial.

**Gaskets.** All gaskets shall be the confined type (spiral-wound or oval ring-joint [ORJ]). Spiral-wound gaskets must have the 1/8-inch-thick centering rings.

**Stress Relief.** Stress relief of piping is required only where specified by Code, where other service conditions demand it for prevention of stress-corrosion cracking, or where required to meet weld hardness limits. (See Company Specifications PIM-MS-2505, 4770, and 4772 for weld hardness limits on carbon steel, stainless steel, and low alloy piping).

**Pipe Connections.** Minimize the number of flanged connections. Avoid the use of pipe unions in H₂S service. Unions are screwed fittings that cannot be seal-welded at the union ring, and represent a potential leak source, especially under vibrating conditions.

**Valves.** All valve body-bonnet closure designs shall be of ANSI/ASME/API bolted, welded, or pressure seal style with secondary mechanical retention features. Screwed bonnet or union bonnet valves should not be used. Welded bonnets are preferred on NPS ¾ through 1½ valves. Conventional gate and globe valves should have an outside stem and yoke. Use steel valve materials; do not use cast iron, brass, or bronze.

In some situations special valves may be warranted, for instance, when odors from stem leakage are intolerable or when tight shutoff is required.

To minimize stem leakage, valves with grease seal bonnet and deep stuffing box features are desirable. All grease seal styles require a scheduled lubrication program to be effective, or, in the case of grease-seal plug valves, to prevent possible seizure of the plug.

Among the quality valves available are the Rockwell-Nordstrom "Dynamic Balance" (grease-seal with bolted cover) plug valves, Crane's Pacific Valve (deep stuffing box with grease injector) gate and globe valves, Orbit ball valves (type "H" seats as a minimum), Cooper Industries' "WKFLOW-R-SEAL" (bolted bonnet, full-conduit) gate valves and Rockwell-Edwards "Y-pattern" globe valves. Valve trim materials, specialty features and valve functional application should be reviewed for compliance with project or facility process design requirements.

Lubricated plug valves perform well and are economical in cost. Because of elastomer seals and lubricant limitations, design temperatures should not exceed 350°F. Crane's Pacific Valve gate and globe valves with grease seal bonnets utilize a deep stuffing box with a double ball check injection fitting that allows grease to be safely

injected into the stuffing box. However, these valves are more expensive than plug valves and, to date, are available only from a single supplier.

Orbit ball valves can be effectively used where tight shutoff is required. However, the standard Orbit sour trims are not resistant to sulfide cracking and should not be used in high H₂S service. Instead, Orbit's "T7 Special Chevron (SC) modified trim" should be specified.

Bridge-weld small valves with short nipples. An alternative is to use extended body gate valves (Smith or Vogt) for vents and drains. These valves have an integral nipple on one end (socketweld) and a female threaded connection on the other. The valves are reduced-port and therefore not recommended for any service where rod-out may be required.

**Vibrating Services.** In vibrating service, 100% radiography is recommended; reinforce branch connections in accordance with Table E of the Corporation Piping Specification (GR135169). Review piping drawings and eliminate any unsafe piping designs. Refer to Appendix C, Piping Vibration Guidelines.

**Sampling Connections.** Minimize sampling connections. Where used, they must be designed for H₂S service conditions to permit safe sampling. Refer to Standard Form PIM-EF-403.

**Protection of Piping from External Damage.** Route lines for greatest protection from external damage. Where it is necessary to route near roads, railroads, or other hazardous areas, provide adequate protection for the lines. Install piping above ground where possible. Where piping must be buried, as in oil field gathering systems and pipelines, reliable external corrosion protection is essential. Where aboveground lines go underground for short distances (under roads) enclose the lines in oil-filled sleeves or provide similar highly reliable protection. Railroad right-of-way and public roads require permit and design approvals by the appropriate regulatory authority. Refer to the *Pipeline Manual* for recommendations on casings.

**Piping Run Design.** Design piping runs to be self-draining, without low spots or liquid traps where water can collect. Eliminate dead legs which lead to corrosion and prevent a clean system flush. Design also for easy cleanout where subject to solids formation.

This is especially true when used in conjunction with a centrifugal pump. To minimize the hazard, a closed atmospheric tank with an elevated vent and low pressure alarm on each pump discharge are required. It is recommended that only a low-hardness water be used, i.e., demineralized water or steam condensate. Low-hardness water will minimize calcium precipitation in piping that can cause scale buildup and plugging.

**Plot Limit Isolation.** Provide reliable plot limit isolation. Double block and bleed, dropout spools or specialty valves are some of the alternatives.

## 1054 Pumps and Compressors

In initial design, avoid pumps or compressors wherever possible in high H₂S concentrations because of the potential leak source they represent. Centrifugal pumps and compressors are preferred over reciprocating pumps and compressors because of their higher reliability.

### Centrifugal Pumps

When a centrifugal pump is required, select a very high quality pump that has a low shaft deflection and will meet the requirements of API 610 and Company Specification PMP-MS-983. The case should be steel or higher alloy, not cast iron or bronze. Wherever possible, use a mechanical seal and inject a clean non-H₂S-bearing fluid as a seal flush. The source of the flush should be reliable and available at all times when the pump is in operation. Use a close clearance bushing outboard of the mechanical seal and plug the openings in the seal-to-bushing chamber. Site specific evaluation of regional air quality standards is recommended to decide if pumps should be ordered with stuffing boxes large enough to accommodate a future retrofit to the double-chambered style mechanical seals for increased leak protection.

### Reciprocating Pumps

When a reciprocating pump is used, use steel material on the liquid end, including steel rods. Provide a non-H₂S-bearing flush to the lantern ring. Select reciprocating pumps that meet the requirements of API 674.

### Reciprocating Compressors

When a reciprocating compressor must be used, two compartment-enclosed distance pieces are required. The crank-end compartment should be purged with inert or sweet gas at a pressure higher than the cylinder-end (sour gas) compartment. The cylinder-end compartment should be vented to a safe location. Special consideration should be given to the safe design of distance pieces vented to closed systems such as a relief header or return to suction. Refer to Figure G-3 of API 618 for more information. Additional site specific requirements may influence the design. Consult a CRTC Materials and Equipment Engineering mechanical systems engineer for further information. Select reciprocating compressors that meet the requirements of API 618.

### Centrifugal Compressors

When a centrifugal compressor must be used, separate lube oil and seal oil systems are required with the seal pots/degassing tank vented to a safe location. Shaft sealing may require special mechanical or labyrinth seals. Casing blowdown drains should also be vented to a safe location. Additional site specific requirements may influence the design. Consult a CRTC Materials and Equipment Engineering mechanical systems engineer for further information. Select centrifugal compressors that meet the requirements of API 617 and Company Specification CMP-MS-1876.

#### Copper Alloys

Copper alloys may have poor resistance to H₂S. Use them with care where there is the likelihood of wetted contact with an H₂S-containing process stream. Acceptable copper-containing items include sleeve bearings, thrust bearings and mechanical-seal throttle bushings.

#### Other Alloys

On compressors, pumps and other mechanical equipment, avoid use of alloys stronger than 90 ksi yield strength, or harder than 235 BHN (equivalent to about Rockwell C22). Exceptions to this hardness limit include components such as reciprocating compressor valves and piston rods where greater hardness is required for reasonable service life.

## 1055 Instruments

The design objective is to minimize the opportunity for the release of H₂S. This can be accomplished through use of reliable instrumentation, limiting the number of connections, minimizing the amount of instrument pipe and/or tubing that contain process fluids, and improving the structural integrity of the piping system. Specific requirements are as follows.

#### Root Valves

Root valves, which permit isolation of instruments from the process, shall conform to the material, temperature and pressure rating requirements of the appropriate piping classification. Install root valves in accordance with Standard Drawing GD-I1057 (contained in this manual). Minimum root valve size is NPS ¾, except for orifice flanges, which, up to ANSI Class 600 flange rating, require NPS ½ root valves. All instrument root valves should be bridge-welded. Do not use offset gage valves for level gages, because of their union-type connection features and potential plugging of the integral check valve. Refer to Standard Drawings GB-J1058, 1059, 1060 (see the *Instrumentation and Control Manual*) for standard connections to level gages.

#### Instrumentation Tubing

Instrumentation piping beyond the root valve should be designed for safety and reliability, and must conform to the process design requirements of the piping classification. Type 316 stainless steel tubing for instrument leads. Type 304 stainless steel with a minimum wall thickness of 0.065 inch (16 gage) is recommended. Do not weld the tubing or seal-weld the tubing connectors. Refer to Section 300 of the *Piping Manual* for further guidance on tubing selection.

#### Restriction Fittings

Restriction fittings should be used in instrument leads to limit the amount of H₂S released in the event of failure. Allowable exceptions are level controllers, etc., where restriction fittings prevent proper instrument function, and in services where it can be clearly shown that plugging problems preclude their use. See Standard

Drawing GB-J1223 (in the *Instrumentation and Control Manual*) for details on restriction fittings.

#### Level Instruments

In all concentrations of H₂S, gage glass level gages with flanged connections can be used where pressure and temperature conditions of the process permit the use of ANSI Class 300 flanges. The use of gage glasses with pipe union connections is not recommended in H₂S service. Gage glasses shall be the heavy, encased type with accessible shutoff valves between glass and vessel. For safety considerations, the gage glass rating for saturated steam service, Steam Working Pressure (SWP), which is about 25% of the actual glass rating, should equal or exceed the process equipment design pressure. In H₂S service, where pressure and temperature conditions require an ANSI Class flange rating greater than 300, consider using redundant displacer-type or differential pressure-type level transmitters with receiving indicators. Design the piping system to allow safe, isolated testing and calibration of the transmitters. Do not use magnetic-follower level gages for level indication in H₂S service; they tend to malfunction owing to solids formation between the float and the tube.

#### Isolation of Process Fluids

Select instrument types which minimize the amount of small pipe and tubing that will contain process fluids. Mount transmitters as close as possible to primary elements. Consider diaphragm seals, where possible, for further isolation.

Select control valve packing and lubricant to minimize H₂S release around the stem. An alternative is to use bellows seals where odor control is a necessity. Without scheduled maintenance by qualified instrument personnel, bellows seals tend to fatigue and fail. Specify the same packing and lubricant above the bellows seal as you would for H₂S service.

#### Continuous Purges

To prevent instrument leads from plugging and for the safety of maintenance personnel, continuous purges compatible with the process (N₂, H₂O, etc.) have been used successfully and should be considered where solids formation conditions exist. Design the purge piping to prevent the process from backing up into the purge source.

## 1056 Relief Systems, Vents, and Drains

In major facilities such as gas plants, refineries and offshore platforms, relief valve discharges and frequently used vents and drains must be routed to a closed relief system. The relief system should recover or flare excess gas in a way that will conform to applicable air pollution regulations. Vents and drains used infrequently for startup or system cleanup must be connected with temporary piping when they are utilized.

In minor facilities where recovery or flaring of H₂S is not practical, occasional or incidental release of H₂S may be routed to a vent stack which will then disperse to

the atmosphere. This can only be done where permitted by state and local regulations.

Relief valves should be steel with bolted bonnet connections. Both bellows-type and conventional-type relief valves have successfully been used connecting to a closed relief system. In a bellows-type relief valve the bellows is vented to the atmosphere. A failure could allow the contents of the relief header to leak to the atmosphere. Proper periodic maintenance of bellows-type relief valves by qualified instrument personnel will minimize the risk of failure. The conventional-type relief valves have been susceptible to spring failure due to sulfide stress corrosion cracking of the alloy steel spring. The current practice, for H₂S service, is to specify conventional-type relief valves with Inconel springs or springs/bellows selected to meet, as a minimum, the sulfide stress cracking material selections listed in NACE MR-01-75. Relief valves should be mounted as closely as possible to the equipment being protected to minimize plugging problems in the high pressure side. The low pressure side, however, must still be designed to be self-draining to the relief header.

## 1057 Special Situations

### Large Flanges in Low Pressure, High H₂S Service

One example of a special situation is the use of large equipment flanges in portions of a sulfur plant where very low pressure high H₂S is processed. Leak free experience has been accumulated using unconfined composition gaskets on large exchanger and furnace flanges. The use of confined gaskets would cause complications such as gasket availability and joint assembly. The flanges were designed in accordance with the ASME Code, although the system pressure is below the scope of the Code. Much of the success can be attributed to the fact that this is a very low pressure service and leaks are less likely than if unconfined gaskets were used at higher pressures.

### Producing and Pipeline Facilities

Producing and pipeline facilities frequently have H₂S environments which are particularly aggressive. As mentioned in Item 3 of Section 1031, a useful materials standard for aggressive sulfide cracking environments is NACE MR-01-75. It was written specifically for oil field equipment and, in some instances, exceeds the recommendations of this guideline.

### Systems With Subatmospheric Pressure

Systems with subatmospheric pressure are inherently safe from leaking H₂S to the atmosphere and normally can be designed as for hydrocarbon equipment. Atmospheric leakage into the subatmospheric system should be minimized to avoid mixing H₂S and oxygen. Such mixing can stimulate corrosion and scaling, or form a potentially explosive mixture.

#### Systems in Which H₂S Concentration May Vary

When a system can carry different streams or streams of varying H₂S concentration, a decision must be made as to which H₂S concentration to design for. Examples are:

- Flare systems where H₂S content may be high in the event of a relief valve discharge.
- Batch processing systems which evolve H₂S during some stage of the operation.
- Plants where H₂S concentration may be abnormally high during an H₂S plant shutdown.

Design standards used for such systems should be based on engineering judgment, and must take into account H₂S concentrations as well as duration and frequency of the higher H₂S condition. The minimum design standard is that dictated by H₂S concentration during the normal operating condition.

## 1060  H₂S Protection and Monitoring

### 1061  General Requirements

Two methods are available for protection against accidental release of H₂S to the atmosphere:

- Equipment handling high concentrations of H₂S may be enclosed in a building with controlled ventilation so leaks can be vented to a safe place. The building atmosphere should be continuously monitored for H₂S with permanently installed and regularly tested H₂S monitors.
- H₂S monitoring stations with alarms may be used to rapidly detect H₂S leakage. Automatic shutdown may be mandatory in some operations.

The concept of enclosing equipment in a controlled ventilation building has been used extensively to protect personnel outside the building against accidental H₂S release. However, it creates a potentially hazardous condition within the building when entry is required or when outside vapor releases can be cycled through the ventilation system.

Where H₂S monitors are used, atmospheric sample locations should be chosen to provide rapid detection and good area coverage. Prevailing wind direction and probable leak sources are important considerations in establishing sample points. Improved containment and monitoring technology now make it generally preferable to utilize H₂S detection and alarm equipment to monitor the plant atmosphere.

H₂S monitoring equipment is required in major installations (refineries, gas processing plants, compounding plants, refinery tank farms, offshore platforms, and oil field facilities in populated surroundings) that handle fluids containing high H₂S concentrations. H₂S monitoring equipment should also be installed in similar facili-

ties handling moderate H₂S concentrations, when factors such as high pressure, H₂S concentration, nature of the equipment, potential volume of fluid release, and dispersal characteristics of the released fluid combine to create a major potential hazard.

H₂S monitoring equipment should also be included in other installations when engineering judgment indicates a need, for the protection of Company personnel or the general public, or when required by federal, state or local regulations.

In all installations handling high and moderate H₂S concentrations, supplied breathing air or self-contained breathing apparatus such as Scott Air-Paks shall be available at control houses and strategic field locations.

## 1062 H₂S Monitoring Instruments

The H₂S analyzers used for atmospheric monitoring should utilize the best technology available at the time of installation. Individual catalytic monitors with single channel dedicated electronics should be selected for maximum reliability.

For process plants, the monitoring and alarm system should consist of:

- Detectors strategically located near potential leak sources.
- Facilities in the control room to show location and magnitude of high H₂S readings.
- A distinctive H₂S alarm system, clearly audible in all areas of the plant. Since noisy equipment affects audible sound, a flashing light may also be necessary to alert the operators. Standardize on the selection of audio and visual alarms to ensure that sound and sight warnings are uniform throughout a facility.
- Emergency aid call switches at strategic field locations. Consider installing alarm switches at same location as the field-mounted Scott Air-Paks and emergency safety showers.
- An area evacuation warning system which can be activated from the control house and from several other plant locations.
- Monitoring and alarm equipment on control house ventilation inlets, if control house location makes it vulnerable to H₂S leaks.
- Wind socks to aid personnel in determining which direction an H₂S leak may be carried.

## 1070 Identification of Hazards

All equipment should be identified by color coding and warning signs, when H₂S content of the contained fluid falls within the scope of this engineering guideline. Other applicable regulations may require color coding or warning signs at lesser H₂S concentrations. The identification system requirements shall conform to all applicable federal, state, or local regulations. In the absence of any overriding

agency regulations, use yellow color coding as defined by the *Safety in Designs Manual*.

Locked fences with warning signs can be used to keep unauthorized people out of isolated locations.

Where equipment contains hazardous quantities of H₂S, warning signs and signs limiting access to authorized persons should be located at:

- The plot limit of facilities.
- Points where the equipment can be opened (vessel manways, drains, sample connections, etc.).

The Railroad Commission of Texas Rule 36 and 30 CFR 250 contain specific requirements regarding warning markers and security provisions for their respective jurisdictions.

## 1080 Upgrading Existing Facilities

### 1081 Application of This Guideline

Most existing Company facilities meet the standards presented in this guideline. Where they do not, they should be evaluated and a priority system developed to upgrade the facility on the basis of relative importance and potential danger. Where massive replacement or renovation of a piping system is judged necessary, and no immediate personnel risk is involved, the changes can be achieved during routine maintenance, scheduled shutdowns or when planned modifications are made. Where a detected personnel hazard exists, the need to eliminate that specific hazard should provide justification for a timely expenditure.

The standards presented here are for new construction and should be used any time extensive modifications or additions are made to existing facilities.

In upgrading existing facilities, the following items are considered of greatest importance and should be checked for deficiencies. They are listed in a possible order of priority. Their correction should receive early attention:

1. Install appropriate warning signs and color coding.
2. Install breathing air systems or self-contained breathing apparatus.
3. Install atmospheric H₂S monitoring equipment.
4. Seal-weld screwed piping.
5. Replace any unconfined gaskets.
6. Eliminate piping smaller than NPS ¾. (Use ½ inch stainless steel tubing at instruments as specified in Section 1055 under Instrument Tubing.)

7. Review branch connection reinforcing and upgrade as good engineering judgment dictates.

8. Install restriction fittings in instrument leads, in accordance with Section 1055 under Restriction Fittings.

9. Remove unnecessary cross-connections and jumpovers.

10. Simplify piping by removing any dead legs and all out-of-service or unused piping systems.

11. Eliminate redundant backup instrumentation and make the primary instruments reliable.

## 1082 Inspection

Due to the potential hazard that can result from equipment failure, an effective scheduled inspection program is essential. Inspection methods and scheduled frequency must address the probable nature and rate of corrosion. The objective is to provide effective insurance against unexpected failures or leaks. Refer to local facility safety instructions for additional details.
