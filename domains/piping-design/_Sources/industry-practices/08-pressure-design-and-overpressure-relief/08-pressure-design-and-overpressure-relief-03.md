## Summary

This document covers the Relief and Blowdown requirements for the Nanushuk Project. This covers relief valves, system depressurization and deinventory and the flare system.

The flare and relief system for the will be designed in accordance with API STD 520 Parts I and II, and API STD 521.

# 1   Overview

Atypically in the form of an automated control valve that is designed to maintain a set pressure in the system, but also pressure relief valve(s) installed on the system that will relieve pressure if the pressure reaches the Maximum Allowable Working Pressure of the system. The possible relief paths are identified below.

## 1.1   Nanushuk Processing Facility (NPF) Process Systems

All process systems within the NPF shall relieve to the flare system. All equipment with a design pressure of 100 psig and above shall be designed to relieve and blowdown to the High Pressure (HP) Flare. All other process systems shall relieve to the Low Pressure (LP) flare. To reduce the risk of over pressuring the process separation train equipment due to blocked discharge, all of the separation train equipment will be designed to a minimum 100 psig design pressure; this includes, but is not limited to:

- Inlet Separator - already above 100 psig design pressure due to operating pressure at 180 psig
- IP Separator – operates at 55 psig
- LP Separator - operates at 10 psig
- Crude Dehydrator - operates at 10 psig
- Desander Packages – operates close to separator pressure associated with it
- Produced Water Degassing Drum - operates at 10 psig
- LP Separator PW Pump and Crude Dehydrator Pumps (suction and discharge)
- IP Preheater and Crude Heater - operates close to separator pressure associated with it

Since the Flash Gas compressor is interconnected with the separation train, this will require the flash gas train to be sized for a minimum of 100 psig design pressure as well including, but not limited to:

- 1st and 2nd Stage Flash Gas Suction Cooler
- 1st and 2nd Stage Flash Gas Suction Scrubber
- 1st and 2nd Stage Flash Gas Suction Scrubber Pump
- 1st and 2nd Stage Flash Gas Suction Compressor
- 1st and 2nd Stage Flash Gas Suction Compressor Piping

With this design, nearly all the major process equipment will relieve and blowdown to the HP flare system. This leaves the LP flare available for smaller low pressure equipment such as the vents and seal gas purge from the compressors.

## 1.2 Drillsite and Flowline Process Systems

Although the Drillsites and Flowlines are outside the scope of the NPF, there will be provisions made at the NPF to allow for relief and manual blowdown from the flowlines and pipelines. The manual blowdown that will be provided at the NPF for all of the flowline systems will relieve to the HP flare. There will also be relief valves provided on the flowline for each drillsite that will relieve to the HP flare, this allows for overpressure protection of the pipeline itself, but also allows for the drillsite process equipment to relieve directly into the pipelines instead of relieving to the atmosphere. The relief system will be sized for a certain number of drillsite wells not shutting-in on demand, the number of wells use for relief sizing will be determined as a result of the Fault Tree Analysis, and is typically around 10% of wells, but will be confirmed later. It will also be sized for fire relief of heaters (or vessels); the relief load to be provided by others. Drillsite equipment will have to be designed to be able to relieve into the pipeline and have a design pressure at or above the setpoint of the flowline relief valves.

## 1.3 NPF Utility Systems

Hydrocarbon utility systems such as fuel gas shall also relieve to a flare system. High pressure fuel gas shall relieve to HP flare, and LP fuel gas shall relieve to LP flare. Liquid utility fluids shall generally relieve back to their respective storage tank, but certain combustible liquid utility fluids may relieve to the flare system if it is not practical to relieve back their respective the storage tanks; an example of this is a methanol line in the process module if the tank is located outside the module.

Non-hydrocarbon utility systems shall relieve to a safe location depending on the utility fluid. If the fluid is stored in an atmospheric tank, the fluid should relieve to the tank. An example of this is a relief valve on the fine water mist system should relieve back to the fine water mist water storage tank. Another example of this is a skid lube oil system; any lube oil relief valves should relieve back to the skid lube oil tank, crankcase, or off skid lube oil storage tank, depending on the application.

Compressed Air can relieve directly to the atmosphere, but the vent pipe shall be vented to a location where the force of relieving compressed air is not a hazard. Compressed Nitrogen can also relieve directly to the atmosphere, but it must be vented to the outside, and in a safe location where the venting cannot cause an asphyxiation hazard.

## 1.4 Atmospheric Tanks (All systems)

All atmospheric tanks shall have a relief path, regardless of the system the tank belongs to. Non-hazardous fluids such as fire water, may be stored in a tank that has a vent that is continuously open to atmosphere. These vents must be sized to handle both the max fluid inflow and outflow from the tank, and be sized for liquid overflow for the max fluid inflow case to prevent the tank from exploding or imploding.

Hazardous fluids contained in tanks are typically designed to be closed systems. These tanks must use LP fuel gas (or nitrogen in some cases) as a blanketing gas, with the feed rate of the blanket gas shall be designed to handle the max tank outflow. At the NPF, these tanks must also be connected to the vapor recovery system that is sized to handle the maximum inflow volume into the tank. In addition to these provisions, these tanks must be equipped with a pressure relief and vacuum breaker device that is sized for maximum inflow on the relieving device, and maximum outflow on the vacuum breaker device. Some tanks will also require emergency venting and will be evaluated on a case by case basis.

# 2 Relief Valves

Relief valve design and regulation shall be designed to API 520 and 521 and comply with applicable Alaska State regulations. For all process systems, relief valves shall be used as pressure relieving devices. Relief valves are preferred for all systems, but rupture discs or *other reliving devices* may be used by exception on certain auxiliary or utility systems.

Tank relief system shall be designed according to API 2000.

## 2.1 Design Pressure

Pressure containing equipment shall be designed for maximum operating pressure or expected upset, plus a safety factor, refer to NPF-PRCRT-000001 *Process Design Criteria*, "Design Pressure" section for the safety factor as well as other considerations.

The design pressure will take into consideration the required margin to prevent lifting relief valves before a high-pressure switch is triggered. Some equipment will have a design pressure much higher than the anticipated operating pressure to allow for safe integration into the flare system. See section 2.

## 2.2 Relieving Cases

All pressure containing systems shall be reviewed for the need for pressure relieving devices. Each relief valve location shall be reviewed for applicability of all of these cases.

Conservative relief load estimates will be made for each device for each of the common mode failures using short cut methods (such as by analogy with other plants or by making simplifying assumptions regarding key data such as latent heats, peak flowrates, etc.). From this the probable governing contingency for the flare/relief system is identified. More rigorous calculations will be provided for large relief loads that are identified as potential controlling case.

## 2.3 Design to Minimize Need for Relief Valves

Generally, all pressure containing systems shall have overpressure protection, typically in the form of relief valves. The requirement for overpressure protection may be minimized for a particular system if it

---

has been determined that overpressure cannot occur on the system. It should be attempted to design systems to reduce the need for relief valves when it is practical to do so.

Systems where relief valves may not be required due to overpressure not possible due to equipment capability:

- Centrifugal Pump Discharge Piping (when pump curve for the largest impeller for a given pump casing is reviewed and confirmed the line cannot overpressure, and the suction line is the same pipe spec as the discharge line.

- Interconnected Piping Systems with same design pressure – in certain cases, multiple pressure containing devices (e.g. vessels) within the same continuous system with the same design pressure, may only require a single relief valve to protect the entire system, provided there is no potential to block the relief path from all equipment in the system and the relief valve, such as an automated control valve or ESD valve. In this scenario, this single relief valve must be sized for relief of all pressure containing equipment within the system.

Systems can also be designed to remove the need for relief valves, examples of this are:

- Designing systems with the same design pressure as the upstream system design pressure, to eliminate "failed control valve" (blowby) overpressure scenario.

- Designing compressor suction and discharge piping and equipment to the same design pressure may eliminate the need for an additional relief valve on the compressor suction side (this does not eliminate the need for a relief valve on the discharge side)

- For shell and tube heat exchangers, use fluid systems with the same design pressure if possible

- Remove unnecessary obstructions to the relief path in interconnected equipment within the same pressure system. An example of this would be for a compressor train containing a scrubber vessel, a cooler, and a pulsation dampener, this equipment should be connected together without any obstruction. An obstruction could be an automated control valve, automated ESD valve, a pressure control valve (PCV), or other inline device that can stop or limit flow. In line filters should be reviewed if they are considered an obstruction. If this system was designed without obstruction, only one PSV is required for this system, but still must be sized for relief from all sources in this system.

## 2.4 Burst Discs

As an alternative to PSVs, burst discs may be used in place of a PSV if there is a design issue with locating a PSV at the particular location and/or where a PSV will not react fast enough to mitigate the pressure surge such a during a tube rupture. Burst discs are smaller and cheaper but have the significant disadvantage that once they burst, the entire system is blown down, and the burst disc must be replaced.

Burst discs are not to be used in locations where overpressure surge may occur frequently. Burst discs are only to be installed on primary process lines by approved exception by Company and/or Operations.

If a burst disc is selected for a particular location, the requirement of flowrate capacity and upstream and downstream piping requirements are identical to PSVs. The burst discs shall be sized according to API 520.

## 2.5 High Integrity Pressure Protection System (HIPPS)

There may be certain systems, where the use of a PSV or burst disc is not practicable, or the potential relief load exceeds the relief system capacity, or when the relief system is nonexistent such as on export lines tie-in location. In these systems, HIPPS methodology may be used as positive isolation to mitigate the relief scenario by making it non-existent. The use of HIPPS is to be approved by company on a case by case basis. An example of a system to use HIPPS is the export pipeline, which feeds into the KPE Oil Pipeline system. Since there are potential hazards in the KPE system that we cannot control, the export pipeline will use HIPPS systems to mitigate the hazards originating from the KPE pipeline.

# 3 Emergency Depressuring

Shutdown and Depressuring (or Blowdown) systems at the NPF will be utilized to quickly and safely isolate and de-inventory equipment in the case of leak or fire. The blowdown system will be sized to depressure high-pressure equipment and relieve to the HP Flare as determined by a "vessel survivability assessment" to mitigate jet fire scenario. A conservative approach will be taken with equipment with operating pressures of 100 psig or less, especially rotating equipment. Further investigation is necessary at a later time to determine if there will be restrictions to depressuring rates, especially with compressors. Pressure settle out studies will be done in FEED.

Blowdown of process systems will be by manual initiation from the Central Control Room (CCR) or initiated automatically per Cause and Effect (C&E) logic, for example in the case of confirmed fire. The NPF will be broken down into process segments for staged blowdown, based on fire zones as well as to accommodate vapor velocity limitations (as in packed beds). These segments will be isolated with Shutdown Valves (SDV). In concert with the isolation of process sections, Blowdown Valves (BDV) will open to the flare to initiate depressuring. SDVs will fail closed on loss of actuation and BDVs will fail open on loss of actuation (i.e. loss of electrical power, instrument air, or hydraulic fluid).

Blowdown and deinventory shall occur only after complete isolation of the affected system controlled by the Emergency Shutdown (ESD) System, for more information on the ESD system refer to NPF-PRPHL-000004 *Emergency Isolation and Shutdown Philosophy.*

## 3.1 Blowdown Valves

In the event the transient and emergency condition requires system depressurization, the system will open the system blowdown valves which will release hydrocarbon streams to the flare. Blowdown valves will be designed with the same considerations as emergency isolation valves described in the Emergency Isolation and Shutdown Philosophy, with additional considerations for:

1. Designed to withstand pressure drop across the valve. Considerations include:

   a. Protection against erosion and valve damage from the pressure drop

   b. Low temperature design of the valve and downstream piping

   c. Review of hydrate formation conditions

   d. Noise

2. Consider pipe stress, pressure surge, and undesired forces

Depending on the application, a restriction orifice or diffuser may be installed to control these factors.

## 3.2 System Blowdown

Considerations for the blowdown system include:

1. A blowdown valve shall be provided for all equipment or systems that are bounded with emergency isolation valves. There must be a path for every isolated system in the facility to be depressurized to flare. In some low-pressure systems where automated deinventory of the system is not necessary, such as in a low pressure system with a small contained volume, a manual depressurization valve may be used in lieu of an automated blowdown valve.

2. Opportunities for trapped pressure, such as downstream of check valves, must be reviewed in design to ensure all sections of piping can be depressurized during an emergency blowdown.

3. The blowdown system shall be designed to start blowdown after isolation is completed, such as with time delay, in order to minimize flaring.

4. The blowdown valve and piping shall be sized to provide the appropriate depressurization rate; this includes:

   a. Ensuring the isolated system is depressurized in the time per the project requirement; see section 4.3, depressurization time.

   b. Ensure depressurization rate does not exceed the acceptable depressurization rate of the equipment within the system, such as with compressors.

## 3.3 Depressurization Time

The blowdown system must be designed at a minimum to meet the required depressurization time for all individual systems. The requirement is to reduce the system pressure down to 100psig or half of design pressure, whichever is lower, in 15 minutes. The flare system shall be designed to handle this blowdown rate for the entire facility. Note that this is only a minimum requirement; a particular system may have a blowdown capacity larger to accommodate other blowdown requirements.

## 3.4 Staged Blowdown

In order to allow safe deinventory of low-pressure systems, an analysis of the system will be done to ensure the blowdown of the facility does not create problems from backflow due to backpressure generated by the blowdown. This may also be a consideration to not exceed flare system capacity.

## 3.5 Considerations for Compressors or Sensitive Equipment

Certain equipment, particularly compressors, may have a maximum depressurization rate defined by the manufacturer. If the sizing criteria for blowdown on the system requires a faster depressurization rate than that equipment manufacturer allows, the following steps should be taken:

1. Confirm with the manufacturer if any mitigations can be made to increase the allowable depressurization rate to the required blowdown rate for the system to meet the blowdown time requirement.

2. If no mitigations are possible, or practical, the blowdown system shall be sized for the maximum depressurization rate of the equipment, and system isolation valves should be moved closer to the sensitive equipment or added to split the system to minimize the contained volume within the slow depressurization system.

3. If isolation valves cannot be moved or added to minimize system size, the system shall be reviewed for all safety considerations given the system will be required to depressurize slower than the minimum blowdown requirement per the Relief and Blowdown Philosophy.

## 3.6 Liquid Deinventory

Liquid contained within process systems during an emergency isolation and blowdown will remain contained within the system, and no specific automated provisions will be made to deinventory the contained liquids. However, the blowdown system shall be designed to handle any evolved vapor from the liquids during depressurization, and for the fire case.

# 4 Flare System

## 4.1 High Pressure Flare

The HP Flare System handles the hydrocarbon reliefs from the high-pressure systems. The hydrocarbon reliefs are collected in the HP flare collection header and routed to the HP Flare KO Drum. The flare gas is then routed to the HP Flare from the KO drum. Liquid separated in the KO drum will be pumped into the slop oil tank. The HP flare stack will be equipped with a sonic flare tip with a 100% smokeless operation.

Fuel gas will be used as purge gas to keep air out of the flare stack and prevent a flammable mixture from forming. Purge gas will continuously flow into the header to maintain positive pressure on the HP flare header.

The HP Flare KO Drum shall be designed for the highest anticipated flowrate relieving event.

## 4.2 Low Pressure Flare

The LP Flare System handles the hydrocarbon reliefs from low pressure systems. The hydrocarbon reliefs are collected in the LP flare collection header and routed to the LP Flare KO Drum. The flare gas is sent to the LP Flare from the KO drum. The LP flare stack will include the associated LP Flare Assist Air Blower(s) to achieve 100% smokeless burning. Liquid separated in the KO drum will be pumped to the Slop Oil Tank. Purge gas will continuously flow into the header to maintain positive pressure on the LP flare header.

The LP Flare KO Drum shall be designed for the highest anticipated flowrate relieving event for the LP flare system.

## 4.3 Flameout

In case of flare flameout, the flare pilots will automatically re-ignite. The flare shall be designed such that, in the event that re-ignition fails, the concentration of hazardous components near personnel and equipment with spark potential will be minimized, assuming the flare is functioning as a vent only. This will be reviewed as part of the flare dispersion analyses. Considerations will be given to not only ground-level locations, but also elevated locations where personnel or ignition sources may be present.
