## 153 Directing the Contractor Design Activities

Administrating the design activities of a contractor (or supplier) may involve only a single Company representative. On the other hand, large, complex projects may involve Company personnel providing direction in such areas as design, operations, process, construction, purchasing, materials control, materials inspection, and finance. The functions of Company representatives vary from job to job; however, some elements of all of these activities come up on each project.

It is not possible for Company representatives to check all of a contractor's work. Therefore, it is important to determine the contractor's strengths and weaknesses early in a project. Not only are no two contractors the same, usually each team from the same contractor is different, depending on the personnel assigned. Sometimes it is necessary, on a spot basis, to bring in Company personnel with expertise in piping, instrumentation, electrical, or another field to reinforce contractor capabilities in weak areas.

The Company representative should listen to the contractor's point of view. Often he has good ideas. In dealing with the contractor the approach is to be steady and firm, but fair. The Company representative should work constantly to maintain a good relationship with the contractor.

The Company representative must understand the scope of the job and the applicable Company specifications and standards. He also needs to understand his role and that of the contractor. He needs to be able to direct the contractor but not do his work for him. It is imperative that the Company representative keep good records of communication with vendors, contractors, and other offices.

Continuously monitoring a contractor's activities is most effective. Such questioning keeps the Company informed on progress and tends to force the contractor to police his own activities. This is particularly important with piping and instrument diagrams, plant layout (plot plans), and piping layout. Of equal importance is the direction given the contractor by Company representatives. Instructions (written) should come early and should represent the consensus of the Company team. Changing directions leads to delays and inefficiency. Only one Company representative should give direction to a contractor. Other directions should be channeled through the prime representative to avoid conflicting instructions.

Mechanical contractors often base their process and designs on guarantees provided by vendors. Such guarantees may be unrealistic and are usually difficult to enforce. Company representatives should provide direction that results in a safely operating and low-maintenance facility. He should select proven equipment particularly with rotating equipment. Unproven equipment should be avoided. Systems should be kept as simple as possible. For instance, complex shutdown systems may be unreliable and may reduce overall safety and operating factors.

The critical path for each job must be developed early. Historically it has been during design; however, the recent arrival of complex computer-driven electronic control systems makes this area one to be closely watched. Each job is different. Careful attention must be given to the critical path, which sometimes develops in unsuspected areas.
