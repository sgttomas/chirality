# 100   General Information

**Abstract**

Piping is the bond that holds together any industrial facility, large or small. However, because of its undramatic presence piping is often taken for granted.

In fact, piping operability, ease of maintenance and, moreover, cost and safety are primary concerns. Piping is typically 20 to 25% of total plant cost, and 10% of all fires within the Corporation are related to piping. Good piping design always balances operation, maintenance, and cost. Proper design is as necessary for piping as it is for the equipment it connects.

The *Piping Manual* is a guide for the basic design and construction of piping systems. It focuses on design fundamentals, guidelines for practical installations, and specification and purchase of materials and services. It is applicable to large and small facilities whether used for upstream production, downstream refining, chemical manufacture, offshore production or marketing. Its guidelines encompass the experience of the Corporation's Operating Companies. The manual's broad applicability makes it useful to both engineers and operating personnel.

The *Piping Manual* is concerned only with piping systems in stations and plants. It does not provide design information for pipelines. The manual includes certain topics related to operations and maintenance, but not a comprehensive description of these functions.

## 110 Codes and Industry Practices

The various piping codes (such as ASME/ANSI B31.1 and B31.3 in the United States) contain practices necessary for safe piping systems, but are not intended to be complete specifications for all phases of design. The Company recognizes this fact, and the guidelines and specifications in this manual provide the supplemental requirements normally needed to obtain economical systems for basic fluid services. A few requirements—where experience has shown them to be better choices—are more stringent than code minimums. Engineers responsible for design and construction of piping systems are expected to be familiar with and to comply with the appropriate codes even though some of their provisions may not be specifically included in this manual.

## 111 Piping Code History

The American Standards Association Code for Pressure Piping, ASA B31.1, 1942 through 1955, was for many years a guide for design, fabrication, erection, and testing of industrial piping. Early on, the Company recognized this code as an acceptable basic standard. The Operating Companies and the Engineering Department subsequently developed numerous standards, specifications, and design practices to supplement and extend the Code provisions.

In 1955, the ASA recognized that a single volume of code provisions would no longer meet the growing and divergent needs of industry. It was decided to issue separately bound sections of the ASA Piping Code—each section covering the piping requirements for a major industry. In 1966, the ASA was reorganized as the United States of America Standards Institute (USASI), and in 1969 the name was changed to the American National Standards Institute (ANSI).

Plans for a separate Chemical Industry Process Piping Code (B31.6) were discontinued when by mutual industry agreement both requirements were incorporated into ANSI B31.3. In December 1978, American National Standards Committee B31 was reorganized as the ASME Code for Pressure Piping, B31, Committee under procedures developed by The American Society of Mechanical Engineers and accredited by ANSI. The code designation was also changed to ANSI/ASME B31.

The applicable sections of ASME/ANSI B31 are:

- B31.1 Power Piping
- B31.2 Fuel Gas Piping
- B31.3 Chemical Plant and Petroleum Refinery Piping
- B31.4 Liquid Petroleum Transportation Piping Systems
- B31.5 Refrigeration Piping
- B31.8 Gas Transmission and Distribution Piping Systems
- B31.9 Building Services Piping
- B31.11 Slurry Pipelines
- B31G Manual for Determining the Remaining Strength of Corroded Pipelines: A Supplement to B31, Codes for Pressure Piping

## 112 Code Application

The Corporation Piping Specification (GB135169) and the on-plot specifications discussed in Sections 700 and 800 use ASME/ANSI B31.3 as their principal basis. Producing Department off-plot piping is covered in the *Pipeline Manual*.

The Corporation Piping Specification also includes specifications for two pipe classes for steam boiler external piping to the first block valve that comply with the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code, Section I. This code is discussed in Section 120.

## 120 Government Regulations

Engineers responsible for design and construction of piping in the United States are expected to determine which codes are legally required and if there are any other federal, state, or local regulations governing such construction. The Company requires compliance with the most stringent practice.

In general, piping that conforms with ASME/ANSI B31.3 and the Corporation Piping Specification will meet the legal requirements in North America for refineries, loading terminals, and gas, natural gasoline, bulk, and chemical plants. Similarly, conformance with ASME/ANSI B31.3 and piping specifications cited in Sections 700 and 800 generally assures legal conformance for producing facilities in North America.

All sections of ASME/ANSI B31 have been adopted by most provinces of Canada. Some sections, including B31.3, have also been adopted by some states and localities in the United States; however, it is not a legal requirement in most states.

## 121 OSHA Compliance

In the United States, compliance with the Occupational Safety and Health Act (OSHA) is mandatory. Other countries have similar legislation. For general petroleum industry piping, *the major effect of OSHA is on construction safety and to forbid the use of regular cast iron for flammable or combustible liquids having a flash point below 200°F, or a temperature within 30°F of their flash point. Steel, ductile cast iron or malleable cast iron are required.* The details of OSHA regulations are still changing, and the most recent revision should be reviewed.

OSHA regulations strongly encourage use of the B31 codes by stating that compliance with B31 codes and specific OSHA rules is prima facie evidence of compliance with the basic requirements of OSHA.

## 122 Boiler Piping

Many state and local regulations require piping immediately connected to a boiler or steam generator to conform to ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code Section I, Power Boilers. The engineer responsible for the design and construction of steam generating facilities should refer to ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code Section I,

to ASME/ANSI B31.1 Figure 100.1.2, and to local regulations to determine their jurisdictional limits. ASME/ANSI B31.3 specifically covers steam piping beyond the mandatory limits of B31.1 and, unless local regulations require otherwise, is used for such steam piping.

## 123 Pipeline Applications

U.S. Government Code of Federal Regulations, Title 49, Part 192 (49CFR192) and Part 195 (49CFR195), cover interstate oil piping and essentially all gas transportation piping. The minimum design requirements for oil and gas pipelines are defined in 49CFR192 and 49CFR195. However, ASME/ANSI B31.4 and B31.8 provide supplemental design information, and their use is recommended. In case of conflict, the applicable part of 49CFR governs. These requirements are detailed in the *Pipeline Manual*.

## 124 Foreign Applications

For installations outside the United States and Canada, the engineer responsible for piping design and construction should determine if there are national or local regulations pertaining to piping design. For consistency in engineering and fabrication ASME/ANSI B31 is preferred over local rules, unless local rules are more stringent. The ASME/ANSI codes remain good guidelines where no other regulations exist.

## 131 Corporation Piping Specification

Piping components are designed and fabricated according to current standards and codes established by national bodies such as the American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME) and the American National Standards Institute (ANSI). The Corporation Piping Specification, developed and used by design groups and operations units within the Company, is based on these standards and codes.

Piping codes prescribe minimum practices necessary for safety. They are not complete specifications. The Corporation Piping Specification adds requirements normally needed to obtain safe and economical piping systems for basic fluid services.

The Corporation Piping Specification (GB135169) was developed for process facilities. The specification covers services encountered in most of the Company's operating facilities.

The Corporation Piping Specification is based on experience, judgment, and economics; use it as a guide for the design of safe and economical piping systems. It is not mandatory. The Specification meets or exceeds ASME/ANSI B31 requirements, and the engineer responsible for design and construction may modify the

recommendations, within the constraints of local legal requirements, when a particular situation warrants it.

The pipe classes included in the Specification cover most low pressure hydrocarbon and utility services. However, they do not include pipe classes for all possible requirements.

Examples of services **not** covered are:

- Chemicals (except sodium hydroxide, concentrated sulfuric acid, monoethanolamine (MEA) and CO₂ mixtures)
- Hydrogen above 425°F (alloy may be required for metallurgical stability) or over 50% by volume
- Temperatures above 750°F, which require an alloy to prevent graphitization of carbon steel
- Corrosive hydrocarbons that require an alloy for corrosion protection (for example, piping systems containing acids or high temperature H₂S)
- Low temperature services—below about 30°F (special material may be required for thick components even above 30°F)
- Piping above NPS 24 in Class 150 and Class 300 flange ratings and over NPS 16 in Class 600
- Boiler code piping above Class 600 flange rating

For these situations or other unique services, the piping engineer can develop his own pipe classes and consult with CRTC's Materials and Equipment Engineering - Technical Services.

## 132 Creating Piping Specifications for Projects

A project piping specification is created to provide a concise record of the basic piping components used in the numerous utility and hydrocarbon process services required for new facilities. Individual class sheets segregate the different services with their respective design conditions and list the selection of piping components.

The creation of a piping specification for a specific project begins with a process flow diagram (PFD), which outlines the essential operating conditions and primary equipment required to produce the process. It provides the information needed to choose of major equipment and piping, and it identifies the principal chemical characteristics of the process stream. CRTC's Materials and Equipment Engineering group uses the latter information to make material recommendations for individual piping systems, including corrosion allowance and valve trim. The recommendation is provided in a Material Selection Report.

The following are developed from the process flow diagram: (1) Piping and Instrument Diagrams (P&IDs), (2) a Plot Plan, and (3) a Project Piping Specification. The P&IDs show the instrumentation, control features, ancillary piping, and the pipe

class and line identification system. The Plot Plan establishes equipment location and pipeway routing so that distance and elevation can be determined.

The Project Piping Specification can be developed from a computer data base used for the Corporation Piping Specification. This data base is occasionally up-dated and should always be reviewed for new project applications.

Additional piping class sheets can be developed either from scratch or from a previous project that had a similar process. ASME/ANSI B31, Section 200 of this manual, and the *Fluid Flow Manual* will help you calculate the required pipe wall thickness and pipe diameter needed to satisfy the design conditions. Pressure calculations can be done manually using Standard Form Industry Source 02 Doc EF-539, Wall Thickness Calculations.

When developing a piping class you must stay within the ASME/ANSI standards for flange rating and pipe wall thickness. Otherwise, you are creating a special design with engineered items. Pipe and fittings that are not commercial mill production or valves that are not standard manufacturer's models entail additional expense and extended delivery schedules. They are not generally economical except in very large quantities. Nonstandard items also create a need for field segregation, identification, and for additional project spare parts. Future maintenance on nonstandard items can often be complicated because of long delivery schedules for parts.

For new classes, be sure to review the branch reinforcing and change of direction tables for design adequacy and component selection. Contact CRTC's Materials and Equipment Engineering - Technical Services for guidance.

## 133 Piping Item Description Database

The *Piping Item Description Database* contains material ordering descriptions for piping components referenced on the individual class sheets of the Corporation Piping Specification. Piping items that appear in other published Corporation standards are also included. The database is no longer reviewed or updated. Updates now normally occur only when current projects request that specific items be reviewed.

Corporation piping class sheets list an alphanumeric identification code (commonly referred to as an L-number) opposite each item description. These abbreviated references are intended to provide concise information for draftsmen and designers. They are not complete enough for procurement.

Piping components (pipe, valves, fittings) require lengthy descriptions for purchase orders. To save space and time on drawings, the ordering information for each component has been assigned a Industry Source 02 alphanumeric code (L-number). The L-numbers and the corresponding ordering descriptions standardize the content and notation in descriptions and provide a reliable, independently reproducible file on components. Projects can maintain a cumulative record of individual items and produce a project material list. The *Database* covers standard items that are produced routinely by manufacturers. It does not cover "engineered" items that are considered project specific.

The ordering description for each piping component includes material, process of manufacture, pressure rating, physical attributes, manufacturer, special trim or construction, etc. The full ordering description (long) is used to place an order.

The Industry Source 02 L-numbers are generated from a comprehensive classification index that segregates piping components in a systematic order. The item coding requires 5 to 9 alphanumeric characters, depending on the type of component. Each code character describes a particular feature of the component and the complete item code chronicles the significant identifying features. The heart of the coding system is the classification index found in the first section of the *Piping Item Description Database* under the tab "Index." Refer to the "Foreword" for an example of how the description is categorized.

Piping components cannot be created simply by the construction of an L-number. The items must already exist as a manufactured item conforming to one of the national or industrial standards (ASME, ANSI, API, MSS) that will satisfy the requirements of the ASME/ANSI B31 section used as the design basis. The manufacturer should be technically and commercially acceptable.

The *Piping Item Description Database* valve descriptions contain figure and/or model numbers obtained from published information provided by the manufacturers. Most valve manufacturers print a disclaimer indicating that they reserve the right to change their catalog without notice. Because of this, the full text of the item description on your purchase order is especially important.

Minimum inspection requirements are normally inherent in the purchase order if one of the national standards (e.g., ASME, ANSI, API, MSS) has been included in the ordering description. Additional inspection and nondestructive examination may be required because of process conditions. These requirements can normally be handled by one of the notes located in the "Material Ordering Description Notes" pages of the Database.

Item selections should be based on three considerations relating to process conditions (1) type of material, (2) design pressure, and (3) design temperature. Technical consulting services dealing with material selection, piping components, ASME/ANSI piping codes, acceptable manufacturers, and quality assurance are available through CRTC's Materials and Equipment Engineering group. Related commercial information is available through Industry Source 02 Purchasing Department.

During the course of a project, distributors or alternate manufacturers will offer "equal to" items. Prudent bid evaluation is highly recommended with the burden of proof for documentation placed on the bidder. A good way to limit the number of "equal to" evaluations is to request quotations from only those who represent the manufacturers already reviewed by CRTC's Materials and Equipment Engineering - Quality Assurance.

## 140 Engineering Judgment

The availability of specifications for a given service does not eliminate the need for sound engineering judgment in piping design. The piping engineer is not released from the responsibility to think.

Every piping application should be reviewed in light of conditions the system might be exposed to. What system upset pressures, temperatures, and fluid compositions are possible? What are possible changes in service? Will the system be required to alternate between two or more services?

In addition, it may be necessary or desirable to modify existing pipe classes to deal with problems arising in specific applications, for example:

- Extraordinary conditions such as earthquake, high wind, other unusual dynamic loadings, or unusual superimposed dead loads

- Cold climates which may require more use of steel valves, and special materials to avoid brittle fractures. Autorefrigeration in LPG service is discussed in Section 1100

- Mercury, ammonia, or ammonium salts in cooling water or product, which may dictate elimination of copper alloys (brass, bronze, etc.).

- Proximity to the ocean or excessive chlorides in cooling water or product may rule out the 300 Series stainless steels.

- High H₂S concentrations may limit the choice of copper alloys and place restrictions on valve trim and weld hardness

- Emergency water and utility lines, which may require steel valves where subject to fire damage or mechanical hazards

- Variation in corrosion allowance or selection of material used for handling of corrosive material

- Use of lighter wall pipe for low pressure systems

- Use of standard schedule materials when maintenance considerations are high priority

It is necessary that the user of this manual realize that its use **does not release him from his responsibility to use sound judgment** in the selection of materials, fittings, valves, and other piping items to meet safety and economic considerations. No attempt has been made to provide for all of the in-between or gray areas.

## 150 Guidelines for Administering the Design Process

This manual deals mostly with the pressure and layout design of individual lines in piping systems. However, the successful design of a compressor suction line, for example, does not make the whole piping system in a compressor plant succeed.

Successful piping systems depend on consideration for plot plan layout, selected equipment, process parameters, civil and structural constraints, etc. The piping engineer, therefore, needs skills that deal with the design management and construction management phases of any facility design.

Appendix E provides some discussion and tools to help the engineer define his project and place it in perspective. These tools are not comprehensive project management guides, but they will give a basis for gaining a broader picture.

## 151 Meeting Operator/Owner Needs

Layout and mechanical design are basic to the development of a project, but no less important is the designer's need to understand the owner's operating organization and how the new facility will fit into this organization. As early in the job as possible, the designer needs to determine:

- How the operating organization will operate the facility (during start-up, shutdown, normal operation, and emergencies)

- Number of operators to be assigned

- Whether a central control building or a local control room is required

- If a complex electronic centralized control system or only local control is required

- What employee amenities are required (change rooms, showers, lockers, food facilities, etc.)

- Where the new facilities will be integrated into any existing facilities. This has major impact on layout studies

- What unable infrastructure exists (utilities, off-plot systems, maintenance facilities, administrative facilities, laboratory facilities, transport systems, etc.)

An acceptable operating factor (percent of the time the facility is in operation) needs to be agreed upon with the operator. Many facilities are designed to run for several years between major, scheduled shutdowns; at other times the unit will be shut down only in extreme emergencies. Parallel systems that allow load-sharing (thus decreasing the system duty), require piping for easy, efficient, and safe switching. Other facilities can be brought down as needed, quickly repaired and brought back on stream in a few hours. The operating factor and maintenance provisions of a system affect the extent of sparing and redundancy of equipment, piping, and instrumentation. It also influences the quality level of piping and other equipment. The designer may want to specify higher quality for piping and equipment that must operate unspared for several years at a time, such as critical valves, sampling systems and piping having seal welding.

Infrastructure (off-plot and utilities) requirements need to be established early in the project and a close review conducted with the operators to determine what may already be available at the selected site. For instance, start-up and shutdown systems often are dependent on existing infrastructure; new facilities may require the addition of parallel or larger piping in the existing plant.

Early attention should be given to environmental permits; frequently they control the project schedule. Environmental conditions should be considered that could bear on operating and maintenance personnel safety and health. Examples include noise levels and exposure to hazardous materials.

Finally, variations in operating styles from one location to another must be recognized, such as the extent of plant supervision and the experience and capabilities at all levels of the operating staff. Availability of support facilities also differs. Some organizations have internal shop, lab, and administrative services readily available; others rely heavily on outside and contract services that may or may not be able to respond quickly.

## 152 Meeting the Needs of Construction and Maintenance

The primary goal in laying out and designing a new facility is operability, but other activities such as construction and maintenance can ultimately have significant cost impact on the project. These activities need to be addressed throughout the design period.

How the facility will be built should be considered when making all major decisions. Until recently construction followed design and material procurement, but we now recognize that construction representatives should be involved from the beginning, both when the design agency is the builder and when the design agency and

the builder are two different organizations. This allows daily decisions concerning construction methods. For instance, the extent to which piping will be shop fabricated determines the type and quality of the drawings needed. Also, improved construction schedule planning should result, improving job efficiency and possibly allowing a shorter overall project schedule.

Working together during the design phase, construction and design representatives can develop the design detailing to efficiently build the facility, rather than, as in the past, too much detail in some areas and too little in others. For example, repetitive small piping details (for steam traps, manifolds, auxiliary pump piping, auxiliary compressor piping, furnace burner piping, etc.) often can be handled with a single, three dimensional drawing with suitable instructions for similar installations. Overall schedules can be developed that consider the whole design, procurement, and construction activity for the total job. Outside influences not completely under the project teams control such as material deliveries, labor availability, shop space, owner time constraints, etc. can be dealt with as a team effort.

The designer must understand how maintenance will be performed and provide the means to perform it efficiently. Maintenance representation during the design period allows anticipation of maintenance requirements in the layout.

Maintenance forces can be anything from all internal to all contract. Sometimes construction and maintenance forces are one and the same.

Maintenance issues that need consideration during plot plan studies include laydown areas for equipment that will be repaired in place, such as large compressors, reactors, and columns. Piping around equipment requiring routine servicing, particularly rotating equipment, should be arranged for easy (30-inch minimum) access. Instruments should be located, and process and instrument leads routed, for easy access. Where equipment can be removed and repaired while the plant continues to run, piping should be arranged so this task can be done efficiently and safely. Maintenance access should be considered during plot plan studies for any equipment that needs to be removed for servicing. Access by cranes and cherry pickers is particularly important. Be sure overhead clearance is sufficient and also clearly signed. Consider putting guard beams on pipeways that cross over roads.

Modularization is an increasingly acceptable construction method. It maximizes the amount of construction done in a shop environment. Equipment, piping, instrumentation, electrical power supplies and supports are fabricated in a contractor's construction shop or yard, often in a climate and under labor conditions that are more favorable than at the job site. Completed units are shipped to the field, where final assembly takes place, usually in a much shorter time than would otherwise be possible. Modular construction is used out of necessity for most offshore and Alaska Industry Source 03 Facility facilities, and it is now gaining popularity in other locations. This method should be considered early in the design period to determine if it will lead to economies and improved construction quality control.

## 153 Directing the Contractor Design Activities

Administrating the design activities of a contractor (or supplier) may involve only a single Company representative. On the other hand, large, complex projects may involve Company personnel providing direction in such areas as design, operations, process, construction, purchasing, materials control, materials inspection, and finance. The functions of Company representatives vary from job to job; however, some elements of all of these activities come up on each project.

It is not possible for Company representatives to check all of a contractor's work. Therefore, it is important to determine the contractor's strengths and weaknesses early in a project. Not only are no two contractors the same, usually each team from the same contractor is different, depending on the personnel assigned. Sometimes it is necessary, on a spot basis, to bring in Company personnel with expertise in piping, instrumentation, electrical, or another field to reinforce contractor capabilities in weak areas.

The Company representative should listen to the contractor's point of view. Often he has good ideas. In dealing with the contractor the approach is to be steady and firm, but fair. The Company representative should work constantly to maintain a good relationship with the contractor.

The Company representative must understand the scope of the job and the applicable Company specifications and standards. He also needs to understand his role and that of the contractor. He needs to be able to direct the contractor but not do his work for him. It is imperative that the Company representative keep good records of communication with vendors, contractors, and other offices.

Continuously monitoring a contractor's activities is most effective. Such questioning keeps the Company informed on progress and tends to force the contractor to police his own activities. This is particularly important with piping and instrument diagrams, plant layout (plot plans), and piping layout. Of equal importance is the direction given the contractor by Company representatives. Instructions (written) should come early and should represent the consensus of the Company team. Changing directions leads to delays and inefficiency. Only one Company representative should give direction to a contractor. Other directions should be channeled through the prime representative to avoid conflicting instructions.

Mechanical contractors often base their process and designs on guarantees provided by vendors. Such guarantees may be unrealistic and are usually difficult to enforce. Company representatives should provide direction that results in a safely operating and low-maintenance facility. He should select proven equipment particularly with rotating equipment. Unproven equipment should be avoided. Systems should be kept as simple as possible. For instance, complex shutdown systems may be unreliable and may reduce overall safety and operating factors.

The critical path for each job must be developed early. Historically it has been during design; however, the recent arrival of complex computer-driven electronic control systems makes this area one to be closely watched. Each job is different. Careful attention must be given to the critical path, which sometimes develops in unsuspected areas.

Another very important function of the Company representative is coordination with other organizations. These include vendors, construction agencies, the Company client organization, other Company departments, and Company specialists (such as fire protection). He may not be the middleman in all contacts but he must know that they are taking place and what they are about.

Not covered in this section, are the Company representative's contract administration responsibilities. Also, personnel administration becomes a factor if there is a change in contractor or Company personnel assigned to a job. Project scheduling and cost control activities are also important and require close monitoring.

## 154 Personnel Safety and Protection of Equipment

Throughout the plant design process, the designer must keep safety in mind. The facility must be safe for all technical, operating, and maintenance personnel as well as the public. Unfailing attention must also be paid to the protection of the facility itself to avoid the prospect of long, costly shutdowns.

Safety needs to be addressed in preparing the plot plans (plant layout) and the piping and instrument diagrams (P&IDs).

Plant layout is one of the first areas where safety becomes a factor. Careful thought is required on location of fired equipment, which is a continuous source of ignition when in operation. There are Company rules regarding spacing, location, and setbacks (see the *Safety in Designs Manual*). However, each case is unique and requires the judgement of the designer. The location of high pressure equipment is important. Here, the attachments (piping and instrumentation) are the most likely source of leaks. Easy access to operating valves and personnel protection from hot, uninsulated surfaces are other safety considerations.

Instrumentation shown on piping and instrumentation diagrams should be simple, proven and properly applied. Complex systems are prone to human error and mechanical malfunction, and can be less reliable from a safety standpoint. Highly interlocked shutdown systems can be so complicated that it becomes very difficult to start up the process. This can lead to poor operating practices such as bypassing shutdowns and alarms.

Generally, making facilities safe also protects equipment from damage that may force extended shutdowns. Alarms, shutdowns (manual and automatic), and relief valves provide such protection.

When developing P&IDs the possibility of cross-contamination of streams due to leaking equipment and pressure differentials must be carefully considered. Valves cannot be considered leak-tight. Only line blanks and dropout spools can ensure no leakage. Breaktanks are often used in drinking water service to ensure no contamination. Also, various operating scenarios need to be thought about that could lead to crossing of streams while in operation. See Section 100 of the *Utilities Manual* for more information on isolating systems.

The designer should be cautioned that the use of new, untested, or overly complicated equipment, particularly for critical areas, may lead to unexpected problems and increased start-up difficulties. This is especially true in areas where the established practices and equipment work, are simple, and ought to remain so.
