# 1 General

## 1.1 Scope

This Specification is intended to assist in the design, materials, fabrication, and testing of in-plant piping and other systems for the Industry Source 03 projects on the Industry Source 03 Facility of Alaska. This specification shall also apply to all piping furnished with packaged equipment.

This specification is not intended to replace progressive architecture or engineering, or impede the constant search for new designs, new materials, or new methods that permit better and more economical piping designs in the future.

No attempt has been made to provide standards to cover every possible combination of service conditions. Hence, where no detailed specifications are available to completely cover the requirements, the designer should be guided by the principle set forth in these general requirements along with his best engineering judgments.

In-plant process and utility piping systems shall be constructed in accordance with the latest edition of In-Plant Piping Design Criteria (Industry Source 03 Doc PICRT-000001). For repair, replacement, or expansion of existing facilities, the use of original construction specifications may be considered.

## 1.2 Sour Service

Any process stream with detectable H2S (or expected to have detectable levels of H2S in the future) should be evaluated for susceptibility to sulfide stress corrosion cracking. This includes all systems containing production fluids. NACE MR0175/ISO 15156 may be useful in determining appropriate materials and procedures for pressure containing components, and other wetted parts based on their potential and consequences of failure that meet the process conditions.

Any system susceptible to sulfide stress corrosion cracking shall be designated as corrosive H2S service if it contains free water such as produced water, liquid hydrocarbons, wet gas, or rich glycols. Corrosion due to H2S can be significant and should be designed for either by materials selection or corrosion control measures, including corrosion allowances. Systems that normally do not contain free water such as dehydrated crude oil, lean glycols, NGL, and dry gas (lift gas, fuel gas, and injection gas) should be evaluated for start-up or upset conditions which can occur and that make these systems susceptible to sulfide stress corrosion cracking.

## 2 Piping Design Criteria

### 2.1 General Design Considerations

It is the responsibility of the Contractor or other 3rd parties to perform all work in a manner consistent with all applicable codes on health and safety regulations.

P&ID's should be the basis for piping design, and should show piping schematics, line numbers, process requirements, equipment, valves, piping specialties, controls, and instruments. Changes to these diagrams after they are issued for design should have prior approval of the Company.

#### 2.1.1 Severe Cyclic Service

The piping analysis shall consider the effects of pressure cycling, thermal cycling, and other cyclic loading (e.g., displacement, wind sway, vibration etc.). The stress analyst shall determine the number of cycles a piping system shall be designed for.

Severe cyclic conditions occur when the Displacement Stress (or Operating Stress under B31.3 Appendix P) exceeds 80% of the Allowable Stress and the number of cycles (N) exceeds 7000 (equivalent number of full displacement cycles during the expected service life of the piping system). It shall be noted by the stress analyst that per B31.3, if the piping is under severe cyclic conditions, then there are additional Code requirements concerning the piping materials, types of components allowed, construction, examination, and safeguarding.

Any piping within 100 linear feet downstream of reciprocating compressor or reciprocating pump discharges, or to the first piping vibration isolation hanger or support, whichever is less; or any piping,

that will be exposed to process ambient in excess of "Normal Service Conditions", as determined by localized process fatigue factors caused by temperature, pressure, vibration, or other operational transients, shall meet the ASME B31.3 requirements for "Severe Cyclic Service".

## 2.1.2 Temperature Conditions

Usual operating conditions do not include more severe temporary conditions, such as conditions incidental to startup, shutdown, or abnormal operation. Temporary conditions govern as design conditions only when there is clear evidence they definitely exceed time and severity limits in the ANSI B31 codes.

## 2.1.3 Design Temperature

The temperature range used in displacement stress calculations shall generally be from the lower of the minimum ambient temperature and design minimum temperature to the design temperature shown in the Line List. However, consideration shall be given to all possible operating/upset scenarios, especially those producing moment reversal in the piping.

Temperature considerations shall include the Joule-Thomson effect and auto-refrigeration effect upon the rapid depressurization as appropriate.

The maximum design temperature shall be the actual steel temperature expected in service coincident with the load case under consideration to determine the appropriate design stress for the selected material.

The minimum design temperature is used to determine the suitability of the material to resist brittle fracture shall be the lowest steel temperature expected in service.

In cases where the calculated membrane stress can vary with the minimum design temperature (e.g., during depressurization), the various combination of stress and temperature shall be evaluated and agreed with the Company to determine the most severe condition for the purpose of a material selection. The minimum design temperature will normally be -50 °F for low temperature systems and -20 °F for all others.

Both the maximum and minimum design temperatures should be reflected on design documents, such as line list and piping isometrics, to indicate general design requirements. In the case of a deviation from the selected line class limits, this deviation should be noted on the P&ID's.

## 2.1.4 Design Pressure

Minimum positive design pressure will normally be 25 psig. In most cases the line class selected set the design pressure. The design pressure should be reflected on the design documents and drawings such as line list and piping isometrics.

The internal and external design pressure and temperature of piping shall be determined considering the following condition (e.g., normal operation, start-up operation, shut-down operation, pump-out etc.). The maximum differences in pressure between the inside and outside of any piping component shall be considered, including the loss of external or internal pressure.

By requiring the maximum available design pressure to be specified, there will be instances of large differentials between design pressure and operating pressure. In such instances, consideration should be given by using lower than maximum safety and shut-down device set points in order to provide adequate annunciation of operational upsets.

For lower pressure line classes, the design pressure is set equal to the flange limit. For higher pressure line classes, the design pressure may be set as low as the safe operating limit and govern by pipe wall thickness at a pressure less than the flange limit.

For ASME B31.4 and B31.8 piping the full ASME B16.5 100 °F flange limit may be used up to 250 °F. Therefore, when based on the flange limit, the maximum design pressure occurs at design temperature up to 250 °F for B31.4 & B31.8 but it is limited to 100 °F for B31.3. At temperatures above these limits, derating is required due to flange limits in ASME B16.5 and the corresponding design pressure will therefore decrease. When based on metallurgy and pipe wall thickness, derating may also be required due to reduced stress allowable and tensile/yield strengths used in respective design code and wall thickness calculations at elevated temperatures.

In the case where derating is required, both the maximum temperature and derated design pressure should be indicated. In case of a deviation from a selected line class limits, this deviation should be noted on the P&ID's.

Piping downstream of equipment (i.e., exchangers, control valves) shall be designed for the same pressure requirements as that of the inlet piping. This includes piping up to and including the last block valve downstream of the equipment unless an overpressure protecting device is present.

Consider hydrotesting piping at full flange rating for low pressure system. Hydrotest pressure shall always be based on the maximum design pressure.

## 2.1.5 External Pressure Systems

Design pressure for piping systems operating under external pressure shall be 15 psi. Exception may be taken where suitable protection against external pressure failure is provided.

## 2.1.6 Load Affecting Piping Stresses

Allowable stress values apply to total loads imposed on piping materials. All piping design, including ASME B31.4 and B31.8, should provide for all loading significantly affecting pipe material stresses, according to ASME B31.3, Sections 301.4 through 301.10.

### 2.1.7 FRP and HDPE Pipe

The use of HDPE, FRP, or glass reinforced epoxy or polyester material pipe should normally be limited to design pressure conditions of less than 150 psig.

FRP piping systems should have a rated burst pressure of no less than 3 times the system design pressure, and in all cases should be designed to accommodate full vacuum conditions.

HDPE and FRP pipe shall be designed for the specific application in accordance with Manufacturer's specifications.

Normally, FRP and HDPE pipe material should not be used for any service containing hydrocarbon liquids or gases, but the presence of small quantities of hydrocarbons will not necessarily exclude its use, e.g., atmospheric drains, and module sump drains.

FRP hydrotesting should include five cycles at 1.5 times the design pressure.

### 2.1.8 Corrosion Allowance

The design wall thickness should include the corrosion allowance specified in the appropriate line class. In general, a 1/8-inch corrosion allowance should be added to the design wall thickness of carbon and low alloy steel piping in corrosive service and 1/16-inch corrosion allowance in noncorrosive service. No corrosion allowance (0.0 inches) is incorporated where FRP or corrosion resistant alloys, such as stainless steel or duplex stainless steel, is specified.

Generally, crude oil, all waters (i.e., sea, produced, fire, and potable), produced water, produced gas, and utilities such as glycol (process and heating/cooling), and methanol, are considered corrosive. Fuel gas, instrument air, nitrogen, and halon are generally noncorrosive.

### 2.1.9 Sour Service

Sour service is defined as having 100 ppm H₂S, or greater than, 0.05 psia partial pressure of H₂S.

For piping in sour service, threaded joints shall not be used. Root valves shall be socket-welded or flanged on the process side and threaded instrument/sample side. Seal welded threads may not be substituted where socket welded connection are specified.

For piping in sour service, the use of flanges shall be minimized. Whenever feasible, lines with flanges should be arranged so that the flanges are grouped together, thus concentrating these potentials sources of leaks in one location for more effective monitoring and maintenance.

## 2.2 Piping Layout and Clearances

### 2.2.1 General Plant Layout

- Pipes shall be designed with the shortest possible line runs and liquid pockets and the minimum number of fittings, respecting flexibility requirements.

- Piping layout shall ensure that valves are readily accessible, easily operated, and provide proper access to allow maintenance of valves and associated instruments. The design shall enable valves to be removed where hot work is not normally permitted.

- Piping and platforms around columns, where necessary shall leave clear space for lowering equipment to the ground. Space shall be left around equipment and instruments to facilitate maintenance and removal (e.g., tube bundle withdrawal, cleaning, level instrument bridles, etc.).

- Buried piping shall not be used, except when required to provide access (e.g., road crossing), and overhead placement is impractical. Underground lines shall be provided with means for emptying. Where lines need to be drained completely, the piping shall be sloped and be provided with drainage points.

- The pipes shall be designed so as not to block operator access to the equipment, providing the necessary space for maintenance, especially when the equipment or elements have removable parts, such as: columns or pressure vessels with internals, compressors, turbines and pumps, heat exchangers with removable bundles, fired heaters and boilers, basket or cartridge filters, line cleaning accessories.

- Special attention shall be given to locate vent, drain, sample taps and unit limit valve manifolds, so that free circulation roads or open fire equipment will not be categorized as "hazardous areas". Hazardous material shall be released at a safe distance from the drain valve and operator.

## 2.2.2 Floor Clearances

Pipe routing along the floor shall be avoided unless there is no other choice. Piping that is run along a module floor should be arranged so as to prevent a tripping, striking, or safety hazard. Clearance between floor or platform and piping (including the bottoms of flanges) should be 12 inches, but not less than 6 inches. Ramps should be provided over these lines when they run in main aisles or walkways.

Lines crossing each other shall have a minimum clearance of an inch (1") pipe-to-pipe, after allowing for insulation and deflection. Attention shall be given to pipe movement caused by thermal expansion.

## 2.2.3 Penetrating Lines

Lines through floors of elevated structures should be run through sleeves. The sleeves should be sized to permit flanges to pass through, if field welds are impractical. The sleeves should be sized to clear pipe or insulation by a minimum of an inch (1"). Consideration should be given to thermal expansion and/or cold spring when sizing sleeves. Sleeves should project not less than 4 inches above the floor or platform.

Lines penetrating buildings (i.e., module walls) should be provided with flashing to prevent exposure to outside elements. Lines penetrating firewalls should also be provided with flashing for containment.

## 2.2.4 Lines Carrying Category "M" Fluids or Hazardous Chemicals

Lines carrying ASME B31.3 Category "M" fluids or hazardous chemicals should be located with special consideration to avoid spraying personnel in case of leaks. All flanged joints and valves should be provided with plastic indicating type safety shields to avoid injury to personnel.

## 2.2.5 Pipes, Walkways and Obstructions Clearance

Minimum width of passageways (walkways) between pumps, equipment, and any obstruction including piping should be 3 feet. Minimum headroom should be 7 feet, except for hand wheel locations should be 6 feet 6 inches.

Minimum width of platforms should be 3 feet. Minimum clearance around any obstruction on the platform should be 2 feet 6 inches. Minimum headroom on the platform is 7 feet. Maximum vertical distance between platforms should be 30 feet.

## 2.2.6 Electrical and Instrument Clearance

Adequate space on pipeways should be provided for installation of instrument tubing runs and electrical conduit.

Classified areas shall be considered when locating electrical and process equipment.

Electrical equipment should be protected from possible leakage, which may develop from equipment or piping (i.e., screwed or flanged joints) located above them. Piping shall not be routed through electrical switchgear room.

### 2.2.7 Operation and Maintenance Clearance

Piping systems should be laid out so that all valves, strainers, and instruments can be operated and serviced safely. When possible, valves should be accessible at a platform or grade level.

Control valves and relief valve assemblies shall be accessible from grade or platform, and orifice runs shall be routed at the lowest possible elevation for maintenance. Orifices should be accessible by a portable ladder or fixed access way or platform as per Company request. Senior orifices and relief valve assemblies should be accessible by fixed access way or platform and should be located so that they are accessible by davit or monorail if weight requires.

Instrumentation and equipment that must be observed, adjusted, or regularly serviced during operation should be accessible from a platform, ladder, module floor, or grade. Level instruments over 10 feet from the module floor should be accessible from a permanent platform.

Sample connections should be readily accessible from grade, module floor, or platforms, except that a ladder may be used to gain access to sample connections where samples will be taken infrequently, or not on a normally scheduled basis. Process sampling systems should be designed to minimize personnel exposure to H₂S.

Areas designated for tube pulling, operation, access, maintenance, or future requirements should be kept clear of all piping. Piping should be laid out so that equipment such as control valves, relief valves, start-up strainers, turbine rotors, pump casing and rods, heat exchanger bundles, compressor pistons, etc., can be removed with a minimum amount of dismantling and without providing additional supports. Access to manways and other access openings should not be obstructed. Piping and equipment should be laid out such that dismantling of adjacent piping or equipment is not required to access or to remove a piece of equipment.

### 2.2.8 Vessel Manholes

Vessel manways located more than 14 feet above the module floor should be provided with permanent access platforms.

### 2.2.9 Drainage

Drain outlets, other than those piped directly to sumps, should normally be 12 inches, and in no case less than 6 inches, above the finished floor.

Piping should be suitably free draining to allow the drainage of equipment and the piping itself, whenever possible. All drains periodically blown down, including instrument, piping drains, and drains from major equipment, shall be independently routed to the sump drain valve location.

Drain headers on dirty vessels should be provided with valves for flushing the header after vessel cleaning. Drain systems should be repairable without a production system shutdown. This is

accomplished by installing corrosion resistant valves between the active production system drain points and the drain system piping.

### 2.2.10 Dead Legs

The design of piping systems should minimize dead legs or trapped sections. Where such a section is unavoidable, suitable vent and drain connections should be provided to ensure that residual fluids trapped in valve manifolds or isolated sections of piping can be safely removed. Install piping/tubing to provide corrosion inhibitor circulation to these "dead" areas. The following guidelines should be followed to minimize potential dead leg problems.

- Future Connections - Piping branches for future tie-ins should be made up fitting to fitting without nipples or other extensions. When possible, such connections should be at the top of a horizontal line rather than at the bottom. Top connections in gassy or oily waters will fill with gas or oil, reducing the dead leg effect.

- End of Header - When possible, headers should end with an elbow rather than a tee. When a header is designed for future expansion (i.e., is ended in a blind flange) the flange connection should be located at the last branch and not at the end of an extended pipe.

Pipe Support - When a piping system changes direction and the elbow is short of a pipe support, the pipe should not be extended to the pipe support. Rather, there should be an elbow with a welded dummy to provide support for the pipe.

### 2.2.11 Bypass or Re-Circulation Lines

Piping should be made up fitting to fitting with a valve installed as close to the main line as possible. Bypass connections should be oriented on top or side to minimize solids and water accumulation.

### 2.2.12 Strainers

Flat or coned strainers with 316 SS mesh sized for the service should be provided for pumps and compressors for use during startup. Pressure taps shall be provided for measuring pressure drop at all permanent and temporary strainer. Provisions should be made to remove the strainers after startup. The weight of strainer shall be considered in the design for ease of maintenance.

Permanent strainers in compressor suction line shall be provided with isolation to permit easy removal. Maximum design pressure drops and flow direction shall be indicated by a nameplate permanently attached to the strainer.

Permanent strainers should normally have a steel body incorporating a basket that can be removed without dismantling piping and preferably with a cover closure not requiring the use of a spanner. The mesh size should suit the type of equipment installed. They shall be in accessible position close to the equipment they protect and valve to facilitate cleaning. Duplex strainers should be of the type which

cannot have a cover removed while that side is in service. For permanent strainers or filters, selection of the screening materials shall be based on the specific application and manufacture's recommendations.

Temporary strainers should be fitted in other cases between the suction valve and the equipment and should ensure that the debris is completely removed from the system when the strainer is cleaned, and be cleaned easily without disturbing the main piping. Piping shall be designed to incorporate the strainer, and no pipe springing shall be allowed for retroactive installation. A suitable spacer shall be provided, when specified by Company, for use on removal of the strainer.

## 2.2.13 Tie-In Points

The tie-in points for piping on packaged equipment should be brought to the edge of the skid. Where extending to skid edge is not reasonable or practical, vendor shall consult with the Company for approval of exceptions. All skid piping for connecting to platform or plant piping off the skid should terminate in flanges or unions.

## 2.2.14 PSV Discharge Lines

PSV discharge lines should be self-draining. Pocketing of relief valve discharge lines, blow-down lines, or flare lines shall be avoided. Where this is impractical, a valve drain shall be provided at the low point. Connections to headers should be to the top of the header to prevent accumulation of scale, grit, etc. back onto the PSV.

## 2.2.15 Corrosion Monitoring Coupons

Access for corrosion monitoring coupons should be provided to points in piping systems where erosion and/or corrosion is anticipated (i.e., well flow lines, bends, and production manifolds). Flanged spools should be considered for piping in high erosion and corrosion areas.

## 2.2.16 Quill Type Fittings

Quill type fittings should be used for all chemical injection points.

## 2.2.17 Line Classifications

The piping line classes and associated materials for piping systems will normally be selected based on service descriptions associated with the line classes listed in the Module Piping Material Specification (Industry Source 03 Doc PISPE-000006). In general, this specification provides a division of services into line classes according to pressure, temperature, design code, service conditions, corrosion allowance, and materials of construction. The use of standard line classes facilitates the design process and promotes standardization of materials.

The particular material class used for any given application should be as indicated on the P&IDs. Piping items not covered by Company piping material specifications should be identified with a tag number and called out on the P&IDs and the piping isometrics.

The use of spec breaks can have a considerable economic impact. A slightly more expensive, superior pressure or temperature line class could eliminate the costs of additional safety devices needed to protect a lower classed line.

Changes in line pressure classes should be made at isolation (block) valves. The isolation valves indicated as per P&ID, are normally on the low pressure side of a control, relief, or check valve. The valve and all piping on the high pressure side should be rated for the high pressure line class. If a bypass is furnished, the bypass valve and piping on the high pressure side should be rated for the high pressure.

Piping wall thickness should be determined in accordance with the latest revision of the applicable ASME code. Corrosion allowance should also comply with the appropriate material class. Note that additional wall thickness may be required for structural strength in certain pipe spans.

## 2.4 Piping Sizing Requirements

Lines should be sized for design condition and governed by service conditions and economics. Satisfaction of technical requirements to meet the service conditions establishes the basic line size. Application of allowable stress (span), structural and economic considerations to this analysis establishes the line size selected for the final design. Line sizing selection and wall thickness shall be based on the following considerations:

- Process condition

- Available pressure drop and horsepower

- Velocity limitations (noise, erosion, vibration, slugging, pump/compressor suction etc.)

- Support spacing, allowable stresses and line deflection

- Material availability and economics

- Consolidation of sizes and wall thicknesses based on material ordering (mil run)

No changes in pipe diameter or wall thickness in pipe diameter or wall thickness may be made without consideration of all the above items. Deviation from initial parameters based on process conditions (i.e., increasing in line size for span) may be carefully coordinated with the process engineer prior to selecting a final pipe diameter and wall thickness.

Piping smaller than ¾-inch nominal is not to be used except in instrument or other auxiliary utility services, such as chemical injection. This size limitation does not apply to miscellaneous piping located on equipment and vessels. A minimum line size of 2 NPS should be considered for lines located in the piperacks.

Pipe sizes of ¾, 1¼, 2½, 3½, 4½, 5, 7, and 9 inches should not be used except where equipment connections contain these sizes. In such cases, transitions to other commercial pipe sizes are to be made as close as possible to the equipment connections.

With due consideration to economy, pipe sizing should match piping materials that are in regularly produced commercial sizes. Because of the high material cost, special care should be given to the sizing of internally coated carbon steel, stainless steel, and alloy lines.

## 2.5 Piping Flexibility

### 2.5.1 Flexibility Investigations

The piping system should be designed to have adequate flexibility to prevent pipe movement from causing overstress of pipe or support material, leakage at joints, or distortion of connected equipment due to excessive forces or moments. Piping should be supported to minimize vibration and piping stresses by using anchors, guides, supports, and structural bracing.

The effect of vibration, natural frequency and uncertainty prediction in temperature from equipment on connecting piping shall be assessed and considered in flexibility analyses.

### 2.5.2 Method of Analysis

Piping systems, when required, can be analyzed by simplified, approximate, or comprehensive methods of analysis.

Approximate or simplified methods can only be used in the range of configurations for which their adequacy has been demonstrated. Approximate methods will still be used for rough checks, since the majority of pipes, in going from A to B, will be inherently flexible.

Computer solution (such as Auto-Pipe or Caesar II or other equivalent stress analysis computer program) should generally be used when a comprehensive analysis is required due to the complexity of the systems involved.

Pipe stress analysis should normally be performed for critical lines in accordance with the calculation of piping criticality. Flexibility analysis should be categorized giving consideration to strength, reaction hazard (e.g., nozzle loads) and service hazards. ## 2.6 Piping Support

### 2.6.1 Support Guidelines

Supports should normally be located at line direction changes. Whenever practical, anchors should be at the midpoint between expansion loops.

Adjustable supports should be provided for both inlet and outlet piping connected to pumps, compressors, heat exchangers, large meters, and similar equipment on which the manufacturer has imposed limits on nozzle loads, and/or elsewhere when growth or movement from thermal expansion is required. The piping supports should be designed so that forces and moments transmitted to the equipment by weight, misalignment, vibration, or thermal expansion are within the equipment manufacturer's limits from ambient to operating temperature. Use of expansion joints requires Company approval.

Relief valve discharge piping and supports should be designed so that stresses induced by connecting lines should not overstress the relief valve body. The reactive forces during discharge should also be considered. Supports and connections should be arranged to facilitate removal and replacement of the relief valve.

The use of cold spring for reducing forces is discouraged and should only be used with approval of the stress engineer.

Piping should be supported on both sides of control valves such that the control valve may be removed for maintenance without providing additional supports for the piping.

Piping and pipe support structures should be designed for the most severe conditions of coincident loads. The following are the types of loads which must be considered:

- Dead Load: The dead load should consist of the weight of the pipe, pipe fittings, attachments and components, and insulation.

- Process Loads: Gravity load from the weight of the operating contents of the pipe. Longitudinal stresses in the pipe due to the operating pressure. Piping analysis shall include sufficient load cases to account operating scenarios as defined through discussion as needed by the Process department.

- Thermal Loads: The stress of growth effects due to the change in temperature from ambient condition (or installation temperature) to the operating temperature.

- Momentum Loads: The momentum load should include the reaction forces due to the change in momentum in surge and slug flow, and the reaction due to the opening of relief valves or snap-acting control valves.

- Friction Loads: The friction load due to movement of piping on pipe supports should be calculated based on a friction coefficient of 0.4 for steel on steel. The friction coefficient for Teflon or the slide

supports should be 0.1, or should be based on the manufacturer's data, with proper engineering judgment.

- Dynamic and Static Effects: Piping systems should be designed to allow for expansion and contraction. The dynamic effects (impact, wind, snow weight, earthquake, and vibration) and weight effects (live loads, dead loads, and test loads) should be considered in the design so that stresses created by these combined loads do not exceed the allowable stresses prescribed by the ASME/ANSI Code for Pressure Piping. (For large air and vapor lines that are to be hydro-statically tested, temporary supports may be required).

- Seismic Loads: The part of the support system that has been considered in the design to provide the required resistance to the seismic forces described in the IBC for seismic loading.

- Test Load: Gravity load from the weight of the hydrostatic testing medium in the pipe. Longitudinal stresses in the pipe due to the testing pressure.

Hangers for pipe 3 NPS and larger should be designed to permit adjustment after erection while supporting all loads. Class 2 fit turnbuckles and adjusting nuts should have full thread engagement. Means should be provided for determining that full thread length is engaged. All adjustable supports should be provided with suitable lock nuts or locking devices.

Dead legs for supporting pipe should be avoided on all piping. Dummy legs may be provided when pipe runs fall short of supports. Dummy legs should have capped ends and should be seal welded. The use of re-pads at the dummy leg to pipe-elbow weld is preferred.

Piping should be supported on racks or by swivel hangers spaced as necessary to prevent excessive sagging, mechanical stresses, or vibrations, with consideration given to concentrated loads such as valves, flanges, and relief valve reaction forces.

Preferably, piping on racks should be laid on top of supports and held in position using U-bolt clamps to anchor the pipe or to permit longitudinal motion. U-bolts should be as follows:

- Support should rest on platform beams or solid foundation, not on deck plate.

## 2.7 Pipe Structural Supports – Package Equipment

Piping supports should be furnished on packaged equipment skids so that all pipes can be run parallel or perpendicular to the skid. Piping supports and bracing should be of sufficient strength to support stresses developed when the skid is lifted and transported. Maximum support spacing on skids should be 8 feet.

Consideration should be given to piping flexibility on packaged equipment due to weight and stiffness caused by short runs and close connections of flanges, valves, and other piping components.

Pipe support structures on a skid may be combined with support structures for other services such as instrumentation lines and electrical conduit to promote an orderly layout on the skid.

## 2.8 Pipe Support – Corrosive Environments

Pipe supports to be erected in highly corrosive environments should be designed to maintain the integrity of structural coatings and minimize drilling of structural members. Consideration should be given to locating secondary supports which provide adjustable locations for pipe hangers over the area where field erected supports will be needed.

Engineering of the metallurgy and methodology of welded supports to alloy piping shall have Company approval.

All engineered pipe support components should conform to MSS SP-58.

## 2.9 Vibration

Precaution should be observed in the design of piping connected to equipment producing harmonic effects or pulsations, excessive vibrations, improper operation, or damage to equipment and instruments.

- Evaluate system for flow-induced vibration at branches.

- Evaluate dynamic excitation from low frequency rotating equipment.

- Eliminate coincidences of piping system acoustical and mechanical resonances.

- Eliminate small diameter branch connections and overhang mass on all compressors, pumps suction and discharge lines in close proximity to source of vibration.

Where such conditions could exist, consideration should be given to the use of pulsation dampening designs and devices.

## 2.10 Flanges, Blinds, Blanks, and Special Flanges

The location of blinds and blanks should be shown on piping plans, model, and isometric drawings as indicated on the P&IDs.

## 2.11.2 Minimum Wall Thickness for Hot Topping

As a general rule of thumb and for structural purposes, (i.e., regardless of hot tap welding requirements for wall thickness minimums), the run (header) piping should be a minimum of ¼-inch wall thickness for at least three (3) run pipe diameters upstream and downstream of branch connections. This does not apply to inline butt-weld branches which match existing walls. Any structural requirements in addition to or instead of this should be identified by pipe stress analysis.

## 2.11.3 Group 1: Integrally Reinforced Branch Connections of O-lets

### 2.11.3.1 Conventional O-lets

Conventional o-lets are groove and fillet weld-on type of fittings as opposed to the insert or butt-weld type (e.g., Vesselolet®, Sweepolet®, insert Weld-o-let®). These types of fittings are used but limited for instrument, vent, and drain connections and only on approved exception basis are used for larger branch sizes required for process flow rate through-put.

#### 2.11.3.2 Flanged Pipets

Typically used for flanged thermowell connections (as an alternative to Thermalets®, which are the preference) and instrument or vent and drain connections in carbon steel lined pipe where flanged Vesselets® cannot be used.

#### 2.11.3.3 Nipolet

This is a one-piece forging that comes in both plain ends for socket-welding or threaded. The use of this fitting eliminates the nipple to o-let weld that has been the main area of past failures. This fitting should be used wherever possible for small instrument and vent and drain branch connections.

#### 2.11.3.4 Weld-o-let/Sock-o-let/Thred-o-let

These o-lets should have only limited applications in instrument and vent or drain connections where flanged pipes or Nipolets® cannot be used. Proper joint design and fit-ups are essential for an adequate weld at the outlet.

#### 2.11.3.5 Insert O-lets

These types of fittings should be used only for new shop fabrication and where the fabrication site is equipped with both welding and machining tools. Field site installation of Insert O-lets® is discouraged in piping due to the exacting dimensional tolerance and machined fit-up requirements.

Insert O-lets® are especially appropriate for header construction with multiple branch openings and for pressure vessel nozzles. Insert O-lets® are available in both flanged and un-flanged, depending on application needs. They are butt-weld, integrally reinforced fittings. Thermalets® should be the primary choice for making flanged thermowell connections. Flanged Vesselets® should be the primary choice for making instrument, vent, and drain connections for carbon steel lined pipe.

#### 2.11.3.6 Using O-lets

- Preferred uses for o-lets are:
  - ½-inch to 3-inch branches to run piping of standard or heavier wall thickness and where the welded branch is less than 50% size of the run pipe.

- O-lets are NOT recommended for:
  - Branches larger than 8 inches in standard wall carbon steel pipe.
  - Branches larger than 4 inches in heavy wall carbon steel pipe.
  - Branches larger than 3 inches in stainless steel
  - Branches greater than 50% size of the run pipe
  - Branches to thin wall run pipe (D/t≥60), except with insert type.

- Branches where severe cyclic loading is expected, except with insert type.

## 2.11.4 Group 2: Inline or Butt-Weld Fittings

Inline or butt-weld fittings are the most preferred type of fitting for making branch connections and should be used wherever feasible. They are especially appropriate for new field or shop fabrication and where in-service lines can be properly isolated and safe-out to allow cutting-in of the fitting. Exceptions to this are the small (¾-inch to 2-inches) branch connections for instruments, vents and drains which are normally accomplished using o-let type of fittings (both insert and non-insert).

Preferred uses of inline or butt-weld fittings:

- For branches larger than 3 inches up to 12-inch carbon steel
- For branches larger than 4 inches C steel in heavy weight line classes
- For branches up to 8 inches in stainless steel
- For branches up to 100% size of the run pipe, all materials
- For branches to thin wall run pipe (D/t>60).

## 2.11.5 Group 3: Stub-on and Split Tee Fittings

### 2.11.5.1 *Split Tees*

Split tees are NOT recommended for corrosive conditions. Use stub-on pipe with saddle reinforcing sleeves. These fittings are full encirclement, integrally reinforced and are almost exclusively used for making hot taps. Weld-o-let® split tees should be limited to noncorrosive applications. In most cases, the fitting welds will require stress relieving.

Most often, stub-on with saddle reinforcement use lap bars with fillet welds applied to the reinforcing sleeve for the longitudinal seam.

If longitudinal groove welds are to be used, split tee typical configuration, backing strips are required behind the groove welds and should extend sufficiently beyond fitting ends to provide arc strike areas.

Split sleeves are commonly used for repairing or reinforcing in-service piping.

### 2.11.5.2 *Stub-ons*

Due to the complexities of design and installation, stub-on pipe branches may be used as branch connections only where they are fully engineered by a knowledgeable engineering group and are reviewed by and have specific approval of the Company.

- "Fully engineered" includes, but is not limited to:
  - Area reinforcement calculations showing compliance with the applicable ASME B31 Code.

- Joint design and configuration.

- Sequence of events for installation.

- Welding procedure with emphasis on assuring optimum root pass application and minimizing back-welding.

- QA/QC procedures that totally encompass the installation from up-front review and approval of designs and procedures to proactive visual inspection during joint preparation and welding, and appropriate NDT methods. Any back-welding should be critically inspected because these areas usually result in highest hardness, which is imperative to control where the branch connection may be in sour service.

- Stub-on pipe wall thickness should be determined as follows:
  - Wall thickness should be equal to or greater than that required to contain the pressure plus allowances.
  - For base header wall thickness up to and including ⅜ inch the stub on wall thickness should equal the base header.
  - Note that the reinforcement added is non-pressure containing. The thickness of reinforcement saddles and full encirclement fittings should be determined by the area reinforcement calculations of the applicable ASME B31 code. Extensions of outlet necks should be used for additional structural stiffening.
  - The optimum joint configuration for installation of reinforcement full encirclement fittings is lap bars with fillet welds at the longitudinal joints and radial continuous fillet welds which smoothly transition to the base header at the fitting ends.
  - If saddle reinforcements extend more than halfway around the run pipe, full encirclement (wrap-around) fittings are required by ASME B31.4.
  - Note that for both types of stub-on reinforcements, a weep hole in the lower extremity is required. This will provide an indication of deterioration of joint condition and at the same time minimize corrosion rates within the reinforcement.

- Recommended uses for stub-on pipe branches and split tees fittings:
  - For branches larger than 12 inches, run pipe in carbon steel. Stub-on branches and reinforcement type branches costs 25-50% less than large tees or O-lets.
  - For branches larger than 6 inches, carbon steel in heavy weight line classes.
  - For branches over 8 inches, run pipe in stainless steel. Stub-on pipe branches and reinforcement type branches cost 25-75% less than large alloy tees.

- For branches up to 80% size of the run pipe, all materials.
  - For branches to thin wall run pipe (D/t>60).
- Where split tees are NOT recommended:
  - Split tees of carbon steel are NOT recommended for corrosive conditions. Use stub-on pipe with external reinforcing sleeve.

## 2.12 Seal Welding of Threaded Piping

Seal welding should be avoided. The use of permanent sealing compounds such as metal-filled epoxy is recommended wherever temperatures permit. All seal welding must be done by qualified welders and in accordance with an approved welding procedure.

- All pipe dope shall be removed from any joint that is to be seal welded.
- The weld shall cover all exposed threads.

The following threaded joints may be seal welded in lines for which socket-welded construction is specified.

- Upstream side of primary block valves where threaded connections were used with the exception of in-line items listed below.

The following threaded joints shall NOT be seal welded:

- Instrument and drain piping downstream of primary block valves
- Control valves
- Relief valves
- Instrument connections
- Union nuts
- Thermowells
- Tubing fittings
- Drain plugs on valve connections or castings
- Galvanized pipe and fittings
- Orifice taps
- Truck connections

Threaded connections should be minimized in hydrocarbon service. It is realized that threaded connections cannot be completely avoided. When determining if a threaded connection is acceptable in

a specific service, consideration should be given to corrosivity, vibration, and risk of leaks (threaded connections are more likely to leak than welded connections).

## 2.13 Isolation Gaskets

### 2.13.1 Insulating Kits

Flange isolation kits are used to electrically isolate flanged connections and still maintain sealing integrity. They are used for various reasons:

- To prevent galvanic corrosion when two different materials are connected by a flanged connection.
- To isolate wells from induced currents in piping or from other wells, causing corrosion of the casing.

### 2.13.2 Dissimilar Metal Connection in a Flanged Connection

When a flange connection is made with two dissimilar materials and the solution being contained is an electrolyte, an isolation kit should be installed. Isolation kits are not required in non-electrolyte environments such as dry crude, dry gas, lean TEG, methanol, and most hydrocarbon based chemicals.

### 2.13.3 Wellhead Isolation

Isolation kits may be installed between the piping and the wellhead if specifically required by the Corrosion Engineer. The electrical connection between the pipeline and the tree should not be shorted by a direct connection between the lines and the well house or via the hydraulic safety system connections. When there is electrical power in a well house, care should be taken to keep the heater and electrical ground connections isolated from the tree and process lines.

### 2.13.4 For Services That Do Not Contain CO₂

The kit consists of a NEMA LI 1 G10 epoxy glass laminated 316 SS core ring type gasket with a Viton® GLT seal ring (300 °F maximum design temperature), a NEMA G10 washer, and a zinc-plated, steel back-up washer for each nut, plus a Mylar sleeve for each bolt, Pikotek VCS, or approved equivalent.

### 2.13.5 For All Services Containing CO₂

The kit consists of a NEMA LI 1 G10 epoxy glass laminated 316 SS core ring type gasket with a spring-energized Teflon® seal ring (300 °F maximum design temperature), a NEMA G10 washer, and a zinc-plated, steel back-up washer for each nut, plus a Mylar sleeve for each bolt, Pikotek VCS, or approved equivalent.

## 2.14 Vents, Drains, Bleeds, and Instrument Connections

### 2.14.1 Vents and Drains

Non-operating vents and drains (hydrotest connections) in piping system do not require valves unless it was shown in the P&ID (i.e., critical service may require a valve). This 1½ inch and smaller connections

should be flushed and plugged after hydrotesting. If vent and drain connections are not provided on equipment, blanked branches shall be provided on the attached piping.

Operational vents, sample points, and drains which may discharge flammable fluids shall be minimized and shall not be less than 50 feet minimum from possible source of ignition (e.g., hot lines, furnace, and rotating machinery).

Drainage line for hazardous or valuable process chemical shall be accessible for inspection and maintenance. Unless otherwise specified by Company, drainage from such equipment shall be permanently piped to a sump tank provided with means of emptying. Where hard pipe cannot be provided, consider block and throttling valve for high pressure drains.

Where a significant release of H₂S, other toxic gas, or large volumes of flammable vapor could occur, vents and drains shall be piped to closed disposal system.

# 3 Valves

The factor involved in valve selection and provides the means of determining the most suitable type (or types) of block and check valves for a particular application given basic information about the service condition. Valve manufacturers should always be asked to confirm suitability of a particular type for the required service and specialist advice should be sought in the more complicated cases.

Factors Affecting Valve Selection

- Required function
- Service conditions
- Fluid type and condition
- fluid characteristic
- Frequency of operation
- Isolation requirements
- Maintenance requirements
- Environmental considerations
- Experience in comparable conditions
- Weight and size
- Cost

This section provides guidance and recommendations for the application of block valves, check valves, pressure relief, and throttling valves. Although factors such as elastomers, materials, maintenance, and availability must be considered when choosing any valve, those areas will be covered in other sections. Ball and butterfly valves application shall be limited to the pressure-temperature ratings given by the valve manufacturer. Exceeding these values will damage the valve soft seats.

## 3.1 Block Valves

Abrasive service is defined as production crude and produced water containing sand and sludge. Valves in these services shall include valves in the crude oil pipeline.

### 3.1.1 Ball Valves

Manufacturers: Cameron, Grove, WKM, PIBIVIESSE, Perrar, PBV, and many others.

- Applications
  - Most often used in nonabrasive service and direction control. Only metal seated valves should be used in abrasive service.
  - Desirable as shut-off valves on nonabrasive service liquid pipelines, but not for throttling.
  - Soft seats generally suitable for maximum 250 °F due to materials.
- Advantages
  - Bubble-tight closure.
  - Quarter-turn operation; easily automated (trunnion only).
  - Reasonable and predictable operating torque (trunnion only).
  - Little or no lubrication required.
  - Block and bleed capability available (trunnion only).
  - Small pressure drop.
  - Can be provided in full port design.
- Limitations
  - Working pressures are limited to the compressive strength of the seat material (floating only).
  - Shutoff is usually in only one direction and requires some differential pressure (floating only).
  - When exposed to vibration, ball valves may open gradually.
  - Soft seats may present a safety hazard in case of fire.
  - Not suitable for throttling service.
  - Soft seats not suitable for abrasive service and at temperatures in excess of 250 °F (typical). If ball valves must be used in abrasive service because of other overriding considerations, they should be placed in horizontal position to prevent buildup of solids on the closed ball which will cut seats. Note that there are some specialty ball valves made specifically for abrasive, erosive services that have been used successfully in sand jetting systems.
- Design
  - Ball valves can be used for block valves in sizes 2 inches and larger. Valves 2 inches and larger should have flanged ends.

- Socket-welded connections should not be used unless extended ends are provided to prevent burning the soft seats during welding. Three-piece bolted body models with socket-weld ends shall not be used. Three-piece models with threaded ends may be used in services where threaded connections are acceptable, such as air, potable water, nitrogen, etc.

- Floating ball type valves should be used in 150-lbs and 300-lbs ratings in sizes 6 inches and smaller. Trunnion mounted ball type valves should be used in 600-lbs and higher ratings and 8-inch and larger sizes in 150-lbs and 300-lbs ratings. Floating ball type valves are less expensive than trunnion mounted ball type valves. Trunnion mounted ball type valves may be substituted for floating ball type valves.

- Ball valve pressure-temperature ratings are limited by the soft seat material. Refer to ball valve manufacturer's catalogs for ratings of other soft seat materials. Two-inch and larger trunnion mounted type ball valves with nylon or reinforced Teflon seats have pressure ratings as per ASME B16.5 at a maximum temperature of 250 °F.

- When the above pressure-temperature ratings are exceeded, gate, plug valves, or Orbit® valves, should be used. Refer to the Orbit® or the plug valve Manufacturer's catalogs for pressure-temperature limitations on their valves.

- Care should be taken when substituting floating ball type valves with trunnion mounted ball type valves. These types of valves differ in face-to-face dimensions in most cases.

## 3.1.2 Gate Valves

Manufacturers: Grove, WKM, Crane, and others.

The majority of gate valves fall into three basic categories: tapered solid wedge, tapered split wedge, and double disc.

1. The solid wedge is predominant, especially in smaller sizes and for steam service; its tightness of seal depends significantly on differential pressure across the valve. To minimize chatter while opening and closing the valve and to guide the wedge to its seat, the wedge is often provided with guides forged or machined into the body of the valve. The faces of the wedge are tapered, causing contact with the seats at the point of closure to minimize wear. Solid wedges are usually made in one piece from the same materials as the trim, but iron and bronze combinations are available. They are used with both rising and non-rising stem valves and can be installed with the stem in any position.

Another type of solid wedge is available, usually referred to as the flexible wedge. Instead of being made with both faces in a rigid position, the faces are separated except for a small center section. This core is strong enough to hold the halves together, but it permits a degree of action between them. This flexibility

requires minimum operating torque, alleviates heat expansion and contraction problems, and provides full inlet and outlet seating surfaces.

2. Split wedges are especially suited for use with air, gas and light volatile liquids. The two-piece wedge disc is tapered and is seated between matching tapered seats in the body. This design is preferred where the body seats might be slightly distorted due to pipeline strains. Installation is recommended with the stem in the vertical position only.

3. Double disc valves are designed to provide seating flexibility. This feature compensates for distortion within the valve and permits tight closure without excessive force being applied to the hand-wheel. The discs are forced against the valve seats by a wedging mechanism as the stem is tightened. The main advantage of this type is that the discs cannot be jammed into the body.

- Applications
  - Generally used as block valves to isolate equipment or lines from service.
  - Primarily used where minimum pressure drop across the valve is important and where the valve will usually be wide open or completely closed.
  - Good for high temperature service.

- Advantages
  - Simple design with small pressure drop.
  - Provides positive seal in both directions.
  - Low pressure drop.
  - Can be provided with full port.
  - In most cases, less expensive than plug or ball valves.

- Limitations
  - Not recommended without a flushing connection for fluids which might solidify (e.g., paraffin) or deposit entrained solids (e.g., sand) under the wedge.
  - Not recommended for throttling. Flow against partly open gate causes vibration and chattering which will damage the seating surfaces.
  - Space requirements (e.g., module construction) may limit the use of rising stem valves.
  - Takes several turns to close.

- Design

- Fabricated type gate valves, such as M&J Valve® conduit gate valves, may be used in lieu of ball valves in size 30 inches and larger. However, the total height of this valve may be a drawback in modular construction because of limited space in the module. The envelope area for this type of valve must be considered in the piping layout. This valve needs larger overhead space.

### 3.1.3 Plug Valves (Lubricated, Pressure Balanced)

Manufacturers: Nordstrom, Serck-Audco, Christensens, Texsteam and others.

- Applications
  - Same general services as gate valves, but where fast shutoff is also needed.
  - Excellent for tight shutoff in abrasive service.
  - Lubricated plug valves are often used for light hydrocarbon liquids, gas, and corrosive service.
  - Very satisfactory as a three-way valve if not requiring sealing against reverse pressure.
  - Better than gate valves for fluids that carry solids.
- Advantages
  - Bubble tight closure if properly maintained.
  - Quarter-turn operation, easily automated.
  - Lubricated plug is self-cleaning, so dirty service is seldom a problem.
  - Leaking valves can often be shut off by injecting sealant.
- Limitations
  - Operating torque requirement is moderately high. Gear and motor operator can be used in these larger sizes but tend to cancel the advantage of quick opening operation.
  - Periodic operation and lubrication are necessary to prevent seizure and to maintain bubble tight sealing capability.
  - Should be avoided for hot gas, as lubricant life is extremely short, requiring frequent maintenance.

### 3.1.4 Plug Valves (Dynamic Positioned, Removable Seats)

Manufacturers: Dresser® Texsteam® (Super G), Nordstrom

- Applications
  - Same general services as gate valves, but where fast shutoff is also needed.

- Excellent for tight shutoff in abrasive service.
- Plug valves are often used for light hydrocarbon liquids, gas, and corrosive service.
- Better than gate valves for fluids that carry solids.
- Preferred valve for 'dirty' crude.

- Advantages
  - Round port, available in full port configuration up to 6 inches.
  - Quarter-turn operation, easily automated.
  - Plug is self-cleaning, so dirty service is seldom a problem.
  - Leaking valves can often be shut off by injecting sealant.
  - Valve is quickly repairable in-line by replacing plug and seats.

- Limitations
  - Only available limited size range.
  - Operating torque requirement higher than ball valve, but no more than dynamic balanced type, if valve is lubricated
  - Lubrication is not required for sealing, but dry torque may be 2-3 times listed values.

### 3.1.5 Orbit Valves

- Applications
  - Orbit® valves are a special design, sole source ball valve, and should be used only in critical services such as major equipment isolation block valves where the plug and gate valves cannot be used. They are more expensive than floating ball type valves but are competitive with the trunnion mounted ball type ball valves and with the pressure balanced plug valves. They are superior to ball valves in handling abrasive services but are less desirable than plug valves. Used successfully where low pressure drop and tight shutoff is a requirement. Relief valve upstream and downstream blocks.

**Note:** Orbit® valves are directional and must be oriented correctly. Orientation should be noted on the piping isometric drawings. Pigging through Orbit® valves is difficult and should be considered before purchase.

### 3.1.6 Butterfly Valves

Butterfly valves should not be used as block valves except in noncritical services.

## 3.2 Throttling Valves

### 3.2.1 Butterfly Valves

- Applications
  - Often used for low pressure non-critical shutoff and control and where little pressure drop is required or in applications where tight shutoff is not required (e.g., direction of flow).
  - By using resilient liners, these valves can provide a fair seal but not tight shutoff.
  - The lug type butterfly valve should be used in applications where the downstream piping may be disassembled.
  - Used for control (throttling) services.
- Advantages
  - Lightweight and compact, requires small installation space and are inexpensive.
  - Quarter-turn operation. Easily automated.
  - Fair for throttling. Specific application should be reviewed by design engineer.
  - Seals flow in either direction.
- Limitations
  - The main drawback to the butterfly valve is that it is basically a low pressure valve. It is not satisfactory when a tight seal is required. They should not be used as isolation valves for equipment that may need to be maintained with the facility in operation. The industry has experienced a substantial amount of leakage problems with butterfly valves used in block valve service.
  - Some are not balanced; opening torque exceeds closing torque.
  - Disc presents obstructions; prevents pigging.
  - May not meet ASME B16.34 (face-to-face dimensions).
- Design
  - Butterfly valves have pressure-temperature limitations similar to ball valves. This is due to the soft seat material such as reinforced TFE.

### 3.2.2 Globe Valves

- Applications
  - Extensively used for flow control and throttling.

[Industry Source 03 logo]

- Suitable for temperatures from -50 °F to 350 °F.
- Mostly used in small sizes up to 4 inches.
- Seldom used for low-pressure water service since cheaper types of valves are adequate.
- Advantages
  - Can withstand frequent operations.
  - Good for throttling.
- Limitations
  - Extremely large pressure drop (up to 25 times that of a gate valve the same size).
  - Not suitable for sluggish fluids (because of large pressure drop) and tends to trap entrained solids.
  - High operating torque.

### 3.2.3 Swing Check Valves

- Recommended for general service and most often used in sizes over 2 inches.
- Works best where flow seldom changes direction; chattering caused by frequent flow reversals can be a problem. Also, rapid closing can cause severe hydraulic hammer.
- In high velocity applications, can create severe turbulence that may cause erosion of downstream piping.
- External lever arms are not recommended because of potential for seal leakage problems.

### 3.2.4 Lift Check Valves

- Recommended for steam, gas, or vapor service.
- Preferable for services having irregular or frequent flow reversals because they have no tendency to slam shut. In fact, spring assist is recommended where rapid closing is a must.

### 3.2.5 Piston Check Valves

- This is another version of the lift check, but is available with floating piston.
- The floating piston valve is specifically designed for pulsating flow and is widely applied in difficult services such as pump and compressor discharge.

### 3.2.6 Ball Check Valves

Recommended for high viscosity fluids and where scale formation and sediment buildup is possible.

### 3.2.7 Wafer Check Valves

Recommend where space is limited. Less expensive and lighter in weight than other types of check valves.

### 3.2.8 Relief and Choke Valves

These are specialty valves to engineer as per specific and unique process applications and require specific approval from Engineer and the Company.

## 3.3 Materials and Metallurgy

### 3.3.1 Sour Service

For valves in sour service, all pressure containing components, including balls, plugs or gates, stems and bolting, and other wetted parts based on their potential and consequences of failure, shall be suitable for continuous H₂S exposure.

### 3.3.2 Valve Seals – Dynamic and Static

#### 3.3.2.1 Valve Seals, Dynamic

The most common materials for dynamic seals (e.g., stem packing) include Teflon® TFE and highly filled elastomeric Industry Source 02 packings, usually nitrile rubbers. The mechanical and chemical characteristics of these materials apply here also. Teflon is excellent but must be properly confined and will need occasional tightening to prevent leaks. Chevrons of nitrile rubber perform well, are often energized by system pressure, and tolerate motion without damage. Nitrile O-rings are acceptable. Viton® should be avoided unless previous experience or laboratory testing indicates otherwise.

#### 3.3.2.2 Valve Seals, Static

Static seals (e.g., body seals) are almost always either elastomeric O-rings or non-elastomeric gaskets. Various types of crushed metal gaskets or rings may be encountered and should be evaluated on metallurgical grounds. General off-the-shelf nitrile O-rings, either standard or low temperature, depending on the conditions of service, are generally acceptable in service. Viton® O-rings for general use should be limited to Parker V747-75 or to other specific compounds known by past history to be acceptable. Graphite type (non-asbestos only) gaskets are acceptable.

### 3.3.3 Valve Elastomers

Selection and availability of elastomers is very dynamic. New elastomers and variations on old formulations are constantly made available. Selection of nonmetallic materials that do not have a service history in a particular application requires consideration of both chemical and mechanical properties

**Teflon® TFE:**

Chemical resistance: inert to oil, gas and water.

Mechanical properties: retains some flexibility to -50°F. Low strength. Tends to flow under stress, thus, must be confined. Properly confine, can be used above 300°F.

**Nylon (polyamide):**

Nylon tends to absorb water; it is hygroscopic. It has a low coefficient of friction. The Industry Source 03 Facility has had good experience with several nylon compounds, including Nylon 660, or 6.12 in sour (H₂S) or CO₂ service. Some nylons are reported to be more stable where water is present. Nylons can be used up to 175 °F and down to about -20 °F depending on composition.

**Kel-F (CTFE) :**

Chemical resistance: similar to Teflon® TFE. Not resistant to halogenated hydrocarbons.

Mechanical properties: very hard and rigid at room temperature. Like Teflon®, tends to cold flow.

**Kynar® PVDF:**

Chemical resistance: excellent resistance to oil, gas and water.

Mechanical properties: useful from approximately -30 °F to 250 °F.

**Nitrile Rubbers (Buna N, acrylonitrile-butadiene copolymers) :**

Chemical resistance: oil resistance excellent for high acrylonitrile compounds, fair to good for low acrylonitrile (low temperature) compounds. CO₂ causes swelling, blistering, and softening of all nitriles, but the effect is more pronounced with the lower acrylonitrile compounds. H₂S tends to harden all nitriles with prolonged exposure.

Mechanical properties: low temperature compounds are serviceable from approximately -20 °F to 225 °F. High acrylonitrile compounds are usable from 0 °F to 225 °F.

## 3.3.4 Valve Elastomers in CO₂ Service

### 3.3.4.1 General

Elastomers in CO₂ service fail by explosive decompression. CO₂ explosive decompression damage can be recognized by severe swelling, blistering, or cracks that run perpendicularly to the cross section diameter of an elastomer seal.

All elastomers that are used in high pressure sealing applications absorb gases, particularly CO₂ if it is present. CO₂ explosive decompression damage occurs when this absorbed CO₂ expands as the pressure on the elastomer is released. The expanding CO₂ swells, cracks, and blisters the elastomer. The severity of the damage is a function of several factors.

### 3.3.4.2 Pressure

CO₂ decompression damage rarely occurs in any system with an operating pressure of 600 psi or lower.

There are specific formulations of elastomers within each group that will perform better or worse than shown above. There is also no one single elastomer choice to use in all cases where high pressure CO₂ is present because other failures causes such as temperature, abrasion and water and oil resistance must also be considered. In general, for all elastomers, seals with cross section diameters larger than 0.15-inch and dynamic seals are more susceptible to decompression damage than smaller or static seals.

### 3.4.4 CO2 Concentration

The CO₂ concentration has a major effect on the decompression damage. The maximum safe CO₂ partial pressures are not known as a function of seal assembly design, elastomer type, pressure, and CO₂ concentration. Several sealing situations are outlined below to help the reader to decide if CO₂ decompression damage is a potential problem for a particular service.

### 3.4.5 Seal Assembly Design and Clearances

Particular designs of seal assemblies, particularly the ones with no extrusion pathways and ones that limit the elastomer's expansion work better than other designs in CO₂ decompression. This factor can cause one Manufacturer's equipment to fail with EPDM O-rings while another manufacturer is successful in the same service employing Buna-Nitrile O-rings.

### 3.4.6 Number of Decompressions

CO₂ decompression damage is physical damage rather than a chemical attack and will not occur if the seals are not depressurized. The number of decompressions required to fail a seal range from one to many. A safe rate of depressurization is not known as a function of seal assembly design, elastomer type, pressure, and CO₂ concentration. It can range from 1,000 psi/day to 1,000 psi/10 minutes for very unfavorable situations.

### 3.3.4.8 Recommendations

CO₂ decompression resistance should not be used as criteria for elastomer selection in CO₂ sealing situations where the CO₂ has a low potential for causing problems. This resistance cannot be obtained without sacrificing more valuable elastomer characteristics such as oil resistance.

Sealing applications where CO₂ decompression damage has been identified as a problem and applications that land in ill-defined "gray areas" should be treated as follows:

- Manufacturers should be supplied with all of the sealing applications information such as high and low temperatures, high and low pressures, anticipated decompression rates, fluids composition, CO₂ partial pressure, and H₂S partial pressure. The CO₂ problem should be emphasized.

[Industry Source 03 logo]

- The manufacturer should supply the names of contacts who can verify that the manufacturer's particular piece of equipment is working properly using the elastomers that are proposed. The satisfactory performance should be confirmed before an order is let.
- In the case that the equipment is new or has not been used in a similar CO₂ environment, the manufacturer should run decompression tests on the equipment before orders are let.

### 3.3.5 Coating and Plating

Internal surfaces of valves that will be exposed to corrosive fluids should be coated for corrosion for corrosion protection. Coatings may be metallic or non-metallic. Both have been used in valves quite extensively.

#### *3.3.5.1 Non-Metallic Coatings*

The primary coating used in Arctic region valves has been a baked phenolic (phenol formaldehyde) coating. This coating is resistant to a corrosive environment, consisting of produced water, CO₂, and H₂S at all conditions of temperature and pressure found at Arctic region. Because of its excellent resistance, this baked phenolic coating was chosen as the only coating specified for valves.

The baked phenolic coatings are thin, ranging in thickness from 5 to 8 mils. They are brittle and easily damaged by direct impact. Care must be used during the valve assembly and during installation to minimize damage to the coating. Because of the necessity for baking the coating to cure it, field repairs are difficult.

The baked phenolic coating must be applied according to strict application procedures. A copy of these application procedures should be included with all valve purchase documents in which internal coating is specified.

#### *3.3.5.2 Metallic Coating or Plating*

Hard overlays, coatings, or electroless nickel plating (ENP) shall be used if necessary to provide wear/erosion resistance and to prevent galling of parts in sliding contact. Electroless nickle-plated carbon steel base material shall not be used if water or other corrosive fluids are present in the process, unless the ENP is applied by qualified supplier and a nominal 75μm (0.003-inch) thickness. Chromium plated carbon steel balls, plugs, and disks shall not be used. The manufacturer shall be able to demonstrate that coating and plating process are subject to appropriate controls sufficient to guarantee quality.

ENP shall be applied by an approved sub-vendor having a significant record supply in similar applications. If trim materials are specified, the supplier may offer a superior alternative having regard to service conditions and required operating characteristics.

## 3.4 Valve Interchangeability

### 3.4.1 Valve Face to Face Dimension

Valve manufacturer's figure numbers may have to be specified in the piping line classes to denote specific types of valves for the required services. The valve code numbers are to be shown on the P&IDs. The piping designers will use the face to face dimension of the valve specified in the piping line class for dimensional purposes.

When a Manufacturer's figure number is specified in a line class, it does not mean that the particular make of valve has to be purchased. A valve of the same type and quality with the same face to face dimension can be purchased.

Manufacturing standards have been issued for gate, globe, and check valves with standardized face-to-face dimensions. The same is true with trunnion mounted ball valves. Lately, manufacturing standards covering face-to-face dimensions have been issued for floating ball type valves and high performance butterfly valves. However, in many cases the face to face dimensions vary from one manufacturer to another on these types of valves.

### 3.4.2 Specifying Type of Valve

At the time the valves are ready for purchase, the piping design drawings will have been issued for design and/or fabrication. To change valve face-to-face dimensions at this point will be costly, time-consuming, and will cause delay of the project. It is therefore necessary to specify valves which are the most economical, perform well in service, are most available, and have common face-to-face dimensions with other valve manufacturers to provide maximum interchangeability. Equivalent valves should be substituted only if the face-to-face dimensions are the same.

### 3.4.3 Valves for Non-Abrasive Services

#### *3.4.3.1    150-lbs and 300-lbs Valves*

- Specify floating ball type valves for sizes up to 6 inches
- Specify trunnion mounted ball type valves for sizes 8 inches and larger.

#### *3.4.3.2    600-lbs and Higher Reduced and Full Port Valves*

- Specify trunnion mounted ball type valves in all sizes.

Note: In nonabrasive services, ball valves, pressure balanced plug valves, and Orbit® valves may be substituted for each other.

### 3.4.4 Valves for Abrasive Service

Pressure balanced plug valves should be specified for all sizes. Occasionally, specialty valves, such as choke valves or abrasive service ball valves, are suitable selections for this service, depending upon service conditions.

### 4.2.2 Wall Thickness

For ASME B31.3, the nominal wall thickness (t<sub>n</sub>) is calculated by factoring the mill tolerance into minimum wall thickness (t<sub>m</sub>) including corrosion allowance (i.e., for ASTM A106: t<sub>n</sub> = t<sub>m</sub>/0.875, where t<sub>m</sub> is the

However, other than as noted above, use of pipe with thicker wall than the specified is limited to the next heavier schedule or weight designation. Additional increase in wall thickness must have approval by the Company or design representative. ## 4.3 Fittings

### 4.3.1 Elbows

Welding elbows shall be long radius unless otherwise approved on a case-by-case basis. Reducing butt-weld elbows may be used instead of elbow-reducer combinations only where a shorter connection is needed.

### 4.3.2 Bushings

Use swages in process flowing conditions where possible. Socket-welded swages are preferred but threaded swages are acceptable when the flow direction is from the smaller diameter component to the larger diameter component. Swages offer gradual flow transitions, minimizing erosion potential, while bushings create step changes resulting in very poor flow transitions and increased erosion potential. Additionally, from an inspection standpoint, bushings are difficult to inspect without disassembly. Whereas there is some potential to radiograph a threaded swage connection, a bushing is much more problematic to inspect with radiography.

Where swages and other preferred piping component installations are impractical, bushing installation is acceptable under the following process service conditions:

- Process service conditions with no flow allowed: dirty water, produced water, seawater, sandy crude, hydrocarbons, corrosive hydrocarbons, wet gas, sump discharge and gravity drains.

- Process service conditions with flow allowed: lube oil, seal oil, hydraulic oil, fuel gas, dry gas, diesel fuel, methanol, nitrogen, instrument air, plant air and chemical injection.

As a general rule, flow direction should only be from the smaller diameter component to the larger diameter component. Exceptions may be made when the responsible engineer closely reviews the system design and approves of the installation.

Due to the product's high affinity for leaching through threaded connections, threaded piping components should not be used in heating glycol, cooling glycol, and Halon service.

Bushing material (i.e. carbon steel, stainless steel) shall be specified on a case-by-case basis for the respective piping system. Careful consideration should be given, but not limited to the following:

- Galvanic issues with dissimilar materials

- Potential erosion

- Process flow should never be from large diameter to small (i.e., typical usage is for gauge connections no flow condition)

- Potential for thread galling (i.e., rolled or cut threads)

- Potential for hex head to contact fitting shoulder before threads are tight (i.e., loose tolerances on bushing and/or fitting)

Hex head bushings shall be the only bushing types allowed. Hex head bushings and swages accommodate a pipe wrench for tightening or loosening the fitting insuring the user knows which joint is being worked on. As a common rule, threaded pipe construction requires a minimum of 5 threads engaged.

Flush bushings shall not be used in any service condition (flowing or non-flowing).

Hex head bushings of one-size reduction shall NOT be used in any service condition. According to ANSI/ASME B16.11, hex head bushings of one-size reduction should NOT be used in services wherein they might be subject to harmful loads and forces other than internal pressure.

In the smaller bushing sizes, the hex head area may be too thin to fit a full size wrench.

During assembly, ensure that only one joint is tightened at a time and that each are correctly fitted with a least one full thread showing.

### 4.3.3 Reducers

Eccentric reducers, arranged flat-on-bottom, should be used for size changes in horizontal lines, except at pump suction connections, where they should be arranged flat-on-top.
