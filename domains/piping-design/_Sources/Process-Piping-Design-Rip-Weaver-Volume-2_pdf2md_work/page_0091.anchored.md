# Fired Heaters

**damper** A flat steel plate located directly above the convection section, connected to a shaft and damper wheel. Control cables at grade allow operators to regulate burner draft by opening or closing the damper. Dampers can be connected so as to work automatically.

**draft gage connection** Usually a coupling located just beneath the damper and in the radiant section near burners. A differential pressure instrument, called a draft gage, is connected to these two couplings and measures the burner draft.

**snuffing steam connection** Usually a 2 in. coupling located in both the convection section and radiant sections. Live steam is used to extinguish (by smothering) flames. If tube rupture causes a fire, a snuffing steam valve is opened to introduce live snuffing steam into the heater. Large heaters have many snuffing steam connections.

**stack transition** A fabricated section below the stack effecting the transition from the rectangular heater shape to a cylindrical stack. Is also used below the convection section to reduce flow area between two rectangular patterns.

**access door** Removable door allowing entrance into the heater to inspect tubes or make repairs. Access doors must be kept clear.

**tube support rings** The ring that supports tubes in a vertical heater. Figure 3-1 shows this ring at the top, however tubes may be bottom-supported. Often, the piping designer can have tube support rings pinned to the piping expansion thereby helping to alleviate stress problems.

**header box** Square section of the tube which contains 180° bends. Header box doors are removable for inspection and must not be obstructed by piping.

**tube guides** Merely direct the tubes' expansion, keeping them from *snaking*. For vertical heaters. In horizontal heaters, tube supports also serve as guides.

**tube anchor** A tube point that is firmly attached to something, anchored, directing expansion away from the point of attachment. Applies mainly to horizontal heaters.

**observation doors** Small doors opened by operators to view flame size and color and tube glow color. Burner control valves should be located near these doors so fuel flow can be regulated while watching burner flame. These doors may be located in the heater's sidewall and must not be obstructed by piping. They are commonly called *peep holes*.

**heater floor** Bottom steel plate of vertical and horizontal heaters.

**pilot gas** Constant burning small flame which ignites the burner fuel.

**crossovers** Not shown in Figure 3-1 or 3-2, crossovers are piping connecting convection tubes to radiant tubes. Crossover piping may be located inside or outside the heater shell. External crossovers must be insulated. Such piping is usually (not always), furnished by the heater vendor, although he rarely supplies the insulation.

**explosion doors** Designed to give and relieve pressure in case of an internal heater explosion. Route piping clear of all explosion doors.

## Heater Passes

The fired-heater manufacturer is given fluid inlet-temperature, quantities and pressure as well as outlet conditions. His responsibility is to provide a heater to meet the specifications, which include a maximum allowable pressure drop from heater inlet to heater outlet. He could get proper heat transfer by going through 32 tubes but this might cause too much pressure drop. Perhaps eight tubes are the limit, causing maximum allowable pressure drop. He must then use these eight tubes for equal heat transfer. Each eight-tube section will have an inlet and an outlet, comprising a heater pass. A 32-tube heater with eight tubes in a section will have four passes, meaning the inlet fluid will be separated into four *equal* flow parts, each entering the heater via a separate nozzle and each leaving via a separate nozzle. Each pass contains an identical number of tubes with identical flow and heat transfer. The piping designer must provide means to divide flow equally among the passes.
