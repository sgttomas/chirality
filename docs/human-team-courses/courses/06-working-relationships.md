# Course 06: Communication and Working Relationships

[Series](../index.html) · [Northbank case](../case-study.html) · [Glossary](../glossary.html) · [References](../references.html)

Project participants hold different knowledge, interests, and responsibilities, but their contributions must become a common understanding that supports coordinated action. Good communication makes evidence, disagreement, decisions, and obligations clear, and it also gives people a practical way to correct that understanding.

This course teaches communication, persuasion, negotiation, and supervision within a human team. Each participant remains responsible for the work and decisions assigned to them.

## Prerequisites and outcomes

Complete Courses 01 to 05 and bring your decision memorandum from Course 05.

After this course, you can:

- Establish shared understanding through explanation and listening.
- Identify stakeholder interests and decision rights.
- Explain a recommendation without overstating its grounds.
- Handle disagreement and route unresolved concerns.
- Assign work with clear inputs, outputs, and support.

Use Northbank Packets A and B, but do not use later events as evidence of what participants already knew.

## Lesson 1: Establish a shared picture

People can use the same words and mean different things. “Approved” might refer to scope, expenditure, a drawing, or technical suitability. So start by naming the activity and the decision under discussion, and explain the terms that change its meaning.

Ask participants what they understand and what they need, because their answers may reveal an omitted operating condition or a misunderstood boundary. Listening requires more than waiting to speak: identify the speaker's concern before you propose a solution.

Use this sequence:

1. State your understanding of the situation.
2. Ask the other person to explain their concern.
3. Restate the concern in concrete terms.
4. Invite correction.
5. Identify the decision or information now needed.

Check understanding through consequences: ask what the participant expects to happen after the proposed decision. An acknowledgment proves that a message was received, but it may leave important differences in interpretation unresolved.

Choose the communication method to suit the question. A short conversation can resolve wording quickly, but a consequential decision needs an identifiable record. Prepare that record from the decision actually made, including unresolved disagreement when it affects later work.

## Lesson 2: Understand interests and authority

A **stakeholder** affects the project or is affected by it. Stakeholders can hold legitimate but competing interests: operations may value reliability and access, procurement may need a complete purchase package, and a sponsor may need a credible investment and delivery outlook.

An interest explains why a participant cares, authority identifies which decisions they can make, and expertise covers what they can assess competently. Do not infer one from another: a persuasive participant may lack the relevant authority, while a quiet specialist may hold decisive technical knowledge.

Prepare a small stakeholder table:

| Field | Useful content |
| --- | --- |
| Participant | Person or role involved |
| Interest | Outcome or consequence important to them |
| Contribution | Evidence, knowledge, work, or decision needed |
| Authority | Identified decision right |
| Constraint | Obligation or limit that affects the discussion |

Use the appointment as evidence for decision rights, but check applicable professional and legal obligations separately.

Consider who is absent from the discussion, because maintenance, construction, and affected users may reveal consequences that design participants overlook.

Ask about interests instead of attributing motives without evidence: schedule pressure can reflect a real operating need. Explain conflicts as consequences to resolve. Avoid treating a person as the problem when requirements or responsibilities conflict.

## Lesson 3: Explain and persuade credibly

A useful technical explanation connects a proposed action to its grounds and consequences, and the listener should know what decision you are asking for.

Lead with the recommendation, then identify the governing requirement, the evidence, the uncertainty, and the effects on the listener's responsibilities.

Keep observed facts, interpretations, forecasts, and proposals distinct, because this helps the reader work out how each statement can be used.

Use plain words for the intended audience and define necessary technical terms. Keep conditions and units when you shorten an explanation.

Explain uncertainty through the decision it limits. “The hydraulic basis is incomplete” becomes useful when you identify the missing conditions and the dependent selection.

Use this message structure:

1. State the requested decision or action.
2. Identify the relevant basis.
3. Explain the important finding.
4. State the consequence.
5. Propose the response and required owner.

Persuasion helps people understand why action matters, but it must stay open to evidence that challenges the recommendation. Avoid hiding a weakness because it makes agreement harder. A decision made without disclosure of a material limitation can expose others to consequences they did not understand.

Protect confidential information by using the authorized communication route, and share enough permitted evidence for the recipient to examine the proposed reliance.

Identify a conflict of interest when it could affect a contribution. Use the actual appointment and professional obligations to determine how it must be handled.

Do not treat confidentiality as a reason to issue a misleading conclusion; look for an appropriate route for the necessary concern.

Tailor detail without changing substance: a sponsor may need the decision and commitment effects, while a specialist may also need the technical derivation.

Ask the recipient to state the expected next action. Correct any misunderstanding before connected work relies on it.

## Lesson 4: Handle disagreement and negotiation

Disagreement can be about facts, interpretation, objectives, acceptable consequences, or authority, and these differences require different responses. A disputed measurement may need another observation, a disputed scope choice may need the sponsor's decision, and a technical deviation needs competent technical assessment.

First establish which kind of disagreement exists, and separate a shared observation from the conclusions participants draw from it.

Use a fair account of the other person's reasoning, and ask what evidence or condition could change each participant's position.

Compare options against shared requirements and constraints. Name differences in objectives where a common criterion cannot resolve them.

Negotiation seeks an arrangement that participants can carry out within their obligations. Agree on an action only when its effects and authority are understood.

Do not trade an unsupported technical acceptance for agreement on the schedule; examine scheduling or commercial alternatives through their own consequences.

If the issue is still unresolved:

1. State the disputed proposition.
2. Record the evidence and positions.
3. Identify the work that depends on resolution.
4. Refer the question to the appropriate decision owner.
5. Continue independent authorized work.

Give participants a route to raise concerns outside the immediate meeting, and use the actual organization and professional obligations for consequential escalation.

A meeting can end while disagreement remains. Its record must distinguish the resulting decision from unresolved technical concerns.

## Lesson 5: Support reliable contributions

Human performance depends on competence, workload, available information, and working conditions, so a clear assignment must account for those conditions.

Give the contributor a result they can understand and examine, and identify the purpose, inputs, interfaces, constraints, and required return.

Confirm that the person has enough competence and capacity. Arrange supervision or specialist support where the work needs it. Agree on when the contribution is needed and explain which activity depends on it. Ask the contributor to identify missing inputs and likely difficulties.

Use a review suited to the result's consequences, and state what the review examines and who receives the findings. Keep ownership through repair: a producer should understand the defect and its effect on the intended result.

Check actual progress through usable outputs and unresolved conditions, because document counts alone may hide missing interfaces or unexamined conclusions.

Support truthful reporting through the way you respond to bad news: ask about the finding and the needed action before looking for someone to blame.

Distinguish an isolated error from a repeated competence, workload, or process problem, and correct the cause that evidence supports.

Mentoring should develop independent reasoning, so ask contributors to explain their basis and alternatives before you give your preferred answer.

## Worked example: A Northbank coordination meeting

The supplier recommendation now needs to guide coordinated action, so this meeting checks that participants share an understanding of the next actions, their conditions, and the relevant decision rights.

B02 records pressure to keep Week 12, and B01 requests rapid acceptance. Operations also asks about the proposed 22 litres per second duty.

The project engineer opens the meeting with the decision question: “What must be established before procurement can release an authorized purchase package?”

The engineer identifies A02 as the approved 18 litres per second basis, while A05's 22 litres per second request remains a separate proposal.

The electrical engineer explains the 480 V offer and A04's observed 600 V connection, but capacity, protection, and compatibility remain to be assessed.

The mechanical engineer identifies the incomplete hydraulic basis and the missing selected curve, and the team does not turn those gaps into a pump selection.

The project manager explains the delivery concern, and procurement is asked to clarify the slot conditions and the available commercial responses.

Operations is asked to define the required changeover behavior. Automatic changeover has been proposed, while A02 leaves the function for confirmation.

The team prepares proposed actions for the relevant roles and identifies the technical information needed before acceptance and the scope decision needed for A05.

The meeting record preserves Week 12 as the target, and it also preserves the unresolved technical conditions and the absence of procurement release.

The participants check what the record means before they rely on it. Any new decision is recorded as an exercise decision, separate from the supplied case records.

## Transfer example: A hospital refurbishment

A contractor wants the ceiling closed immediately, but a facilities engineer needs access to inspect services. Both participants have a legitimate delivery concern.

They identify the inspection requirement, the affected area, the evidence needed, and the closure authority. They can then examine a staged response without hiding the inspection condition.

## Practice: Conduct a decision meeting

Use A01, A02, A04 to A06, and B01 to B03. Assign the sponsor, project manager, project engineer, operations, and procurement roles.

Prepare a brief for each role using only its supplied interests, duties, and concerns, and record any exercise preference you add as an assumption.

Conduct a meeting about B01's acceptance request and A05's proposal. A mentor can play several roles for individual study.

Produce a stakeholder table, an opening explanation, a meeting record, and a route for unresolved concerns. Identify decision owners separately from action owners.

After the meeting, each participant explains the next authorized action. Compare those explanations with the record.

Discuss how you would disclose an interest that could affect your recommendation, and identify a permitted route for confidential supporting information.

## Knowledge check

1. Why does receiving a message not establish understanding?
2. How do interest, expertise, and authority differ?
3. What must a credible recommendation disclose about uncertainty?
4. How should a team distinguish factual disagreement from a scope disagreement?
5. What must the record show when a meeting ends with unresolved technical concerns?
6. How can a manager support truthful reporting and competent contributions?

## Assessed assignment: Establish an actionable common understanding

Prepare and conduct a review of your Course 05 memorandum, using Packets A and B as the information available to participants.

Explain the technical findings, the proposed flow change, the procurement needs, and the delivery concern. Ask for decisions only within the authority stated in A01.

Explain how you would handle confidential evidence or a conflicting personal interest. Treat these as transfer questions, separate from Northbank facts.

Submit the stakeholder table, speaking brief, meeting record, participant understanding check, and any corrected wording.

The instructor assesses these five observable criteria:

1. The explanation preserves source status, uncertainty, scope, and technical limits, including a truthful response to pressure.
2. The stakeholder table distinguishes interests, expertise, contributions, and authority.
3. The discussion fairly represents different positions and identifies appropriate routes for conflicting interests and confidential evidence.
4. The record identifies decisions, conditions, action owners, and unresolved concerns accurately.
5. Participant explanations establish a common next action or expose differences for repair.
