# Course 01 — Professional Practice and Responsibility

[Series](../index.html) · [Case](../case-study.html) · [Glossary](../glossary.html) · [References](../references.html)

A project brings together people with different knowledge and different responsibilities. Good practice helps them make a coherent result together. Each person must understand their assignment, its limits, and the consequences of their work.

This course establishes those responsibilities before the team produces technical conclusions. It connects your professional duties with your project appointment, your competence and authority, how your work is examined, and how you communicate. It is a course: it does not grant a licence, an appointment, or authority to approve real engineering work.

## Prerequisites and outcomes

No earlier course is required, and you need no pump-design knowledge. Read Packet A of the Northbank case.

By the end, you can:

- Identify the people affected by an assignment.
- Distinguish responsibility, authority, and accountability.
- Explain the different contributions of project management and project engineering.
- Recognize work that exceeds your competence or appointment.
- Prepare a clear request for assistance or a decision.

## Lesson 1 — Understand whom the work serves

An assignment usually names a client, sponsor, or manager, but its effects can reach many other people. Constructors must install the result, operators must use it, and maintenance staff must service it. Other people can experience its environmental or social effects.

So start with the intended benefit and the people it affects. That tells you why the work matters and which consequences need attention. A narrow brief can leave out an important condition, and you must notice the gap before you treat the brief as complete.

At Northbank, the sponsor wants an improved water-transfer arrangement. Operations must run the equipment and respond to failures. Maintenance staff need access and isolation arrangements. Procurement needs an authorized purchase package. These needs are connected, but each contribution needs a named owner.

An engineer also carries professional obligations, and what they require depends on the actual jurisdiction, licence, and circumstances. A project appointment cannot grant permission that the applicable professional arrangement withholds; the Northbank appointment says so explicitly in A01.

Professional ethics is a basis for evaluating conduct, not only technical outcomes. The [Engineers Canada ethics guideline](https://engineerscanada.ca/guidelines-and-papers/public-guideline-on-the-code-of-ethics) puts public safety, health, welfare, and environmental protection first. It also covers truthful statements, competence, confidentiality, and conflicts of interest. It is national guidance, so for real work check the actual regulator's requirements; Northbank's jurisdiction is still unresolved under A06.

State facts, assumptions, and opinions accurately. If you have an interest that could impair your impartial judgment, disclose it through the appropriate process. Protect confidential information under the applicable arrangements. When confidentiality and public protection seem to conflict, get suitable guidance on what your obligations actually are.

Commercial pressure is never grounds for a false technical statement. When it arises, explain the concern and its consequences, identify the decision and response that the applicable professional arrangement requires, and record the response. Continue the work that has a sound basis.

Competent practice includes technical understanding. Document control, retrieval, interpretation, and communication support that understanding, but they cannot show that a physical design works. You need suitable technical analysis and examination before people rely on that design.

Use this method when you receive an assignment:

1. State the intended result in one sentence.
2. Identify its direct users.
3. Identify other people who can experience its effects.
4. State the important benefits and possible harms.
5. Identify the obligations that need confirmation.
6. Name the person who will resolve each missing obligation.

Scale the detail to the work: a small drawing correction may need a short note, and a new installation needs a broader account. The point is to bring out consequences the assignment could otherwise miss.

## Lesson 2 — Match responsibility with authority

Responsibility is the work or decision a person must carry. Authority covers the acts that person may perform. Accountability is the obligation to explain and answer for that responsibility.

A job title does not settle every decision right. A manager may have authority to approve expenditure but not to accept a technical deviation. An engineer may establish a conclusion in their discipline but lack authority to change the project scope. Check the appointment against the specific decision.

Project management organizes the project's commitments, resources, execution, and delivery. Project engineering coordinates the technical basis, contributions, interfaces, and grounds for the combined result. Each needs information from the other.

At Northbank, the project manager can arrange work within the approved scope and budget. The project engineer coordinates the technical basis. Discipline engineers keep their technical responsibilities. The sponsor decides changes to scope, budget, and targets. Procurement places orders against authorized packages. These are the case appointments recorded in A01.

One person can hold several roles, but they must still know in which capacity they are acting. A small team needs clear responsibilities as much as a large one.

Prepare a decision map when authority is uncertain:

1. Name the required act.
2. State the content and scope of the decision.
3. Identify the appointment that grants authority.
4. Identify the required technical contribution.
5. Identify the affected participants.
6. State the decision that remains reserved.

Avoid assigning a vague phrase such as “overall approval.” Say whether the act concerns scope, expenditure, technical conformity, delivery, or operating readiness, because a person may have authority for only one of these matters.

## Lesson 3 — Work within demonstrated competence

Competence is the knowledge, skill, experience, and judgment that the assigned work needs. It is specific to the task and its conditions: experience with one type of equipment does not establish competence for every installation.

Check your competence before you accept responsibility. Consider the technical methods, the relevant requirements, the operating environment, the tools available, and the possible consequences. Identify what you can do on your own and what needs supervision or specialist input.

You can learn on real work when the supervision and examination arrangements are suitable, so make those arrangements explicit. A learner should know who will examine the work and which acts remain outside the learner's authority.

Time pressure can hide a competence gap. Familiarity with a supplier's brochure can feel like knowledge of the selected equipment, and a confident colleague can make an unsupported conclusion seem settled. Check the actual grounds.

Review adds scrutiny, but it does not take over the producer's responsibility. A reviewer needs suitable competence, a defined purpose, and access to the relevant basis. The producer must still correct defects and explain the result.

The reviewer, in turn, is responsible for the assigned examination and its conclusions. Both sets of duties exist side by side.

Use a competence check before beginning consequential work:

1. List the judgments the assignment requires.
2. Identify your relevant experience for each judgment.
3. Identify unfamiliar methods or conditions.
4. Name the assistance required.
5. Agree the supervision and review arrangements.
6. State the acts you cannot perform under the appointment.

Do not claim competence just because a checklist is complete. Use the check to find where evidence, experience, or assistance falls short.

## Lesson 4 — Communicate concerns and preserve responsibility

A concern becomes useful when another person can understand it and act on it. State the condition you observed, the supporting evidence, the work affected, and the consequence. Then identify the response that is needed.

Keep observation separate from inference. “A04 records 600 V” is a statement of what a source says. “The offered equipment is compatible” would need further technical evidence, and a schedule target cannot supply that evidence.

Escalating means taking a matter to someone who can resolve it or assign its treatment. It does not require abandoning every activity: identify the dependent work that lacks a sound basis, and continue independent work within your authority.

If a concern affects safety or a professional obligation, follow the response requirements that actually apply and get suitable assistance. This course does not define a universal stop-work or reporting procedure.

Persuasion is part of responsible practice. Explain the consequence in terms the other person can assess, listen for information that changes the issue, and keep uncertainty visible when agreement is difficult.

Prepare a concern for decision:

1. State the discrepancy or missing condition.
2. Cite the relevant source and revision.
3. Explain the work that depends on its resolution.
4. State the foreseeable consequence of proceeding.
5. Propose a bounded next action.
6. Identify the person who must decide.

After a decision, check exactly what was decided and on what conditions. A request to investigate permits the investigation; it does not approve the solution that the investigation may recommend.

## Northbank worked example — An assignment under an incomplete basis

The project manager asks the project engineer to prepare the equipment-selection work. Packet A is available, but no supplier selection is available yet.

The project engineer first identifies what the assignment rests on. A02 Rev 1 requires 18 litres per second and two pump units, and leaves the hydraulic conditions unresolved. A05 requests 22 litres per second but records no scope amendment.

The project engineer separates the required contributions:

| Matter | Contribution and responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| Hydraulic conditions | Mechanical engineering and operations establish the necessary inputs. |
| Electrical conditions | Electrical engineering examines capacity, protection, and compatibility after A04. |
| External requirements | Assigned competent participants resolve A06 before dependent decisions. |
| Proposed flow increase | The sponsor decides after an impact assessment. |
| Combined equipment basis | The project engineer coordinates the contributions. |
| Work arrangement | The project manager organizes the authorized work and resources. |

The project engineer can organize the gathering of inputs and define the technical work, but the available records do not support a final equipment selection. A04 supports the observed connection characteristics; it does not establish the electrical design.

A concise return could state: “Prepare the input investigations under A02 Rev 1. Keep the 22 litres per second proposal separate. Complete dependent selection after the basis is established.”

This gives the manager useful work to arrange, while preserving the sponsor's decision and each discipline's responsibilities.

## Transfer example — A software service upgrade

A service manager asks a developer to increase processing speed. A security specialist holds responsibility for the access design. The product owner decides which user functions change.

The developer can examine performance under the current requirements but cannot assume permission to remove access checks. A performance proposal must identify its effects on access, reliability, user behavior, and continuing support.

The same decision map applies: identify the act, its authority, the technical contributions, the affected people, and the reserved decision. The technical details differ, but the responsibilities still need clear homes.

## Practice — Prepare a responsibility brief

Use A01 to A07 and work in pairs. One participant prepares the brief; the other challenges its authority and evidence.

The supplied task is: “Prepare the next two activities needed for Northbank equipment selection. Keep work moving within the approved project basis.”

Use this invented practice event together with Packet A: a manager asks for the statement “Electrical compatibility is confirmed,” saying that a qualified statement may weaken the funding request. No additional electrical evidence is available. Prepare a truthful alternative and a response to the pressure, and explain the possible consequences of the requested claim.

Supply these outputs:

1. A statement of the intended result and affected people.
2. A map of four decisions and their appointed authorities.
3. Two executable activities with owners and needed contributions.
4. One competence limit with a suitable assistance request.
5. A concern note about one unresolved condition, including your ethical-pressure response.

Use Packet A only, and identify its records by revision. Do not invent a legal requirement or a completed technical check. During the challenge, explain which statement would change if new evidence arrived.

## Knowledge check

1. How do responsibility, authority, and accountability differ?
2. What distinguishes project management from project engineering in A01?
3. Why does A05 not establish the equipment-selection flow?
4. What does A04 establish, and what remains for electrical engineering?
5. How should you respond when commercial pressure requests an unsupported technical statement?
6. How does independent review affect the producer's responsibility?

## Assessed assignment — Establish your working responsibility

Prepare a two-page appointment response for the Northbank project engineer. Use Packet A and your practice outputs. Include an activity plan and one decision request.

An instructor assesses five observable criteria:

| Criterion | Evidence required |
| --- | --- |
| Purpose and ethical conduct | The response identifies affected people and gives a truthful, reasoned response to the supplied commercial pressure. |
| Authority | Decisions match A01, and reserved decisions remain identified. |
| Source basis | The response uses A02 Rev 1 and distinguishes proposals, observations, and open inquiries. |
| Competence and examination | Activities include necessary specialist input and a suitable review purpose. |
| Useful continuation | The plan advances independent work and states the conditions limiting dependent decisions. |

Explain your response orally, and repair any unsupported claim found during examination. Acceptance in training confirms that the assignment meets its learning criteria; it grants no professional authorization.
